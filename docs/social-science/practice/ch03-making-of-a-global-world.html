Written questions · Write

SHORTS13 marks

1. What were the Silk Routes and what did they carry besides goods?

Show Answer

Ans:

The Silk Routes were a network of pre-modern trade and cultural routes linking Asia with Europe and northern Africa. Besides goods such as Chinese silk, Indian cotton textiles and spices going west and silver going east, they carried religions — Buddhism, and later Christianity and Islam — and also carried diseases, since germs travelled with the traders and pilgrims.

SHORTS23 marks

2. Explain how the Columbian exchange changed diets in the Old World, giving examples.

Show Answer

Ans:

Before the discovery of America, foods such as potatoes, soya, groundnuts, maize, tomatoes, chillies and sweet potatoes were unknown in Europe and Asia. These American crops reached the Old World after Columbus's voyages and changed what people ate. The poor of Europe, who had earlier eaten mostly bread and porridge, now had a more varied and abundant diet, and in Ireland the potato became the staple of the poorest sections.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Why did the Spanish conquest of America succeed so easily?

Show Answer

Ans:

Mainly because of disease. The Spanish conquerors carried smallpox germs, and the original inhabitants of America had no immunity to them because of their long isolation from the rest of the world. Smallpox spread and killed huge numbers, decimating whole communities, so conquest became easy. Guns could be bought or fought against, but disease could not.

SHORTS43 marks

4. Describe the role of silver from Peru and Mexico in the global economy of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

Show Answer

Ans:

The discovery of rich silver mines in Peru and Mexico made silver available in huge quantities. It was shipped to Europe and from there used to finance Europe's wealth and its expanding trade with Asia. Silver thus became the link that connected the Americas, Europe and Asia in a single flow of money and goods.

LONGL55 marks

5. Discuss “The Making of a Global World” in detail. Include the idea that carries marks, one worked example or answer frame, and why students lose marks.

Show Answer

Ans:

Globalisation is not new. Human societies have always been in contact, but globalisation as we know it — the integration of markets, movement of goods, capital, people and ideas across the world — began to take shape only in the last few centuries. Even in the pre-modern world, trade, migration and the spread of religion and disease linked distant regions.
Marks lens: marks at most, from the pre-modern exchanges, the Silk Routes and the Columbian food trades.
Method / memory trick: Silk Routes = goods + religion + disease. All three travel together; mention all three for full marks.
Do not: Revising the later subtopics (corn laws, gold standard, Bretton Woods) and spending revision time on material that is not assessed.

SHORTS102 marks

10. What was the Corn Laws controversy in Britain?

Show Answer

Ans:

The Corn Laws restricted the import of cheap corn into Britain, keeping bread prices high to protect landowners. Workers and industrialists demanded their repeal; after repeal in 1846, food flowed in from the colonies and eastern Europe, bread became cheaper, but many European peasants were forced to migrate to the Americas.

SHORTS113 marks

11. Explain what indentured labour was and its effects in the nineteenth century.

Show Answer

Ans:

After slavery was abolished, plantations in the Caribbean, Fiji, Mauritius, Sri Lanka and Natal needed cheap labour.
Millions of workers were recruited from India, China and Africa on contracts (girmit) for a fixed number of years, often after being misled about the terms.
They were carried in crowded ships and worked in harsh conditions, but they also carried Indian languages, festivals and food abroad.
Indentured migration from India was finally abolished in 1917 after the nationalist movement raised the issue.

LONGL124 marks

12. Explain how the First World War and the Great Depression changed the world economy.

Show Answer

Ans:

The First World War turned Europe from a creditor into a debtor and made the United States the world's main lender.
The Wall Street Crash of 1929 and the Depression of the 1930s collapsed world trade, output and employment; agricultural prices and farm incomes fell sharply, hitting colonised countries hardest.
Countries raised tariffs and formed imperial preference blocs, and migration and lending fell.
The crisis destroyed confidence in unregulated markets and led to state intervention — for example Roosevelt's New Deal — and to a demand for decolonisation.
