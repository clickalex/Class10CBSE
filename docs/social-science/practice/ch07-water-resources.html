MCQs · Drill

MCQM11 mark

1. Jawaharlal Nehru proudly proclaimed dams as: (a) the lifelines of India (b) the temples of modern India (c) the pride of the nation (d) the wealth of India

Show Answer

Ans:

(b) the temples of modern India — they were seen as the means of developing irrigation, hydroelectricity and flood control together.

MCQM21 mark

2. In which state has rooftop rainwater harvesting been made compulsory for all houses? (a) Rajasthan (b) Meghalaya (c) Tamil Nadu (d) West Bengal

Show Answer

Ans:

(c) Tamil Nadu, with legal provisions to punish defaulters.

MCQM31 mark

3. 'Johads' and 'khadins' are traditional methods of: (a) irrigation canals (b) rainwater harvesting in agricultural fields (c) flood control (d) groundwater pumping

Show Answer

Ans:

(b) rainwater harvesting in agricultural fields, mainly in arid Rajasthan, where rainwater is allowed to stand and moisten the soil.

MCQM41 mark

4. In Rajasthan, underground tanks built to store rainwater, often in the house courtyard, are called: (a) tankas (b) kuls (c) guls (d) bamboo drips

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) tankas. Tankas store rooftop rainwater and are especially common in Bikaner, Phalodi and Barmer.

MCQM51 mark

5. Water is called a renewable resource because it is continuously renewed by the: (a) water (hydrological) cycle (b) food chain (c) soil cycle (d) wind cycle

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) water (hydrological) cycle. Evaporation, condensation and precipitation keep replacing the fresh water in rivers, lakes and the ground.

MCQM61 mark

6. The Bhakra Nangal project is built on the river: (a) Mahanadi (b) Sutlej (c) Narmada (d) Krishna

Show Answer

Ans:

(b) Sutlej. Located in Himachal Pradesh, it serves Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan.

MCQM71 mark

7. The Narmada Bachao Andolan opposed: (a) the construction of large dams (b) canal irrigation (c) groundwater use (d) interlinking of rivers

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) the construction of large dams. It demanded proper resettlement and rehabilitation of the displaced people.

MCQM81 mark

8. Ancient water-harvesting structures in India included: (a) baolis and tankas (b) only tube-wells (c) only dams (d) only canals

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) baolis and tankas. Stepwells, baolis, johads, khadins and tankas were built in different regions to store water.

Written questions · Write

SHORTS12 marks

1. Why is water scarcity in India often a problem of management rather than of availability?

Show Answer

Ans:

India receives plenty of monsoon rain, but most of it flows away unused. Scarcity is caused by over-extraction of groundwater, unequal access between rich and poor, pollution of rivers and tanks by domestic and industrial waste, and wasteful irrigation and domestic use — so the problem is how water is stored, distributed and used.

SHORTS23 marks

2. Why is water scarcity increasing in India?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because of a growing population, which raises the demand for water for drinking and for growing more food and cash crops; rapid urbanisation and changing lifestyles that consume more water; over-exploitation of groundwater by commercial farming; and unequal access between social groups. In many regions water is abundant but polluted by domestic and industrial waste and by pesticides and fertilisers, so it cannot be used without treatment.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Describe the traditional rainwater harvesting system of Rajasthan.

Show Answer

Ans:

In the semi-arid regions of Rajasthan, particularly in Bikaner, Phalodi and Barmer, most houses traditionally had underground tanks or tankas for storing drinking water. The tanks were built inside the house or the courtyard and were connected to the sloping roofs. The first spell of rain was not collected, as it cleans the roof and the pipes; from the second shower onward the water was stored. The stored water lasted through the dry season and was treated as pure enough for religious use.

SHORTS43 marks

4. What is rooftop rainwater harvesting and where has it been made compulsory?

Show Answer

Ans:

It is a method in which rainwater falling on rooftops is collected through pipes and stored in underground tanks or used to recharge groundwater. It is common in Meghalaya, where bamboo pipes are used, and in Tamil Nadu it has been made compulsory for all houses, with legal provisions to punish defaulters.

APPLICATIONA53 marks

5. A student answers “Multipurpose river projects help by providing irrigation, electricity and flood control.” Why would this answer lose marks if the question asks for an evaluation? Add three drawbacks.

Show Answer

Ans:

It lists only the benefits. An evaluation question expects both advantages and disadvantages.
Drawback 1: large numbers of people are displaced, and their land and homes are submerged.
Drawback 2: dams change the natural flow of the river, cause sedimentation, and harm fish and aquatic life.
Drawback 3: they can cause floods or waterlogging and lead to disputes between states over sharing water.

APPLICATIONA63 marks

6. Name one traditional rainwater-harvesting or water-saving method each suited to (i) the hills and (ii) the arid plains of Rajasthan, and say how each works.

Show Answer

Ans:

(i) Hills: guls or kuls, diversion channels in the Western Himalayas that carry water from streams to fields (in Meghalaya, bamboo pipes do a similar job).
(ii) Rajasthan: tankas (underground tanks) that store rainwater collected from roofs and courtyards, or johads and khadins, earthen structures that hold rain for crops and recharge groundwater.
Both capture water when it is plentiful so that it can be used in the dry months.

SHORTS73 marks

7. Mention any three problems caused by large multi-purpose river projects.

Show Answer

Ans:

Large numbers of tribal and peasant families are displaced and often inadequately resettled and rehabilitated.
Forests and biodiversity are submerged, and the flow and ecology of the river are disturbed.
Benefits are unequal: canals usually serve the richer farmers in the command area, causing waterlogging and salinity, while the poor and the downstream areas gain little.

COMPETENCYC84 marks

8. Read and answer: Villagers living along a river protest against a proposed dam that would submerge their fields and homes. Engineers say it will supply water and electricity to distant cities. (a) Whose interests conflict here? (b) Name two movements in India against large dams. (c) How can such conflicts be reduced?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) The interests of the displaced local people and the interests of the cities and farmers who would benefit from the water and power.
(b) The Narmada Bachao Andolan (against the Sardar Sarovar project) and the movement against the Tehri Dam on the Bhagirathi.
(c) By proper rehabilitation and compensation, by involving the people affected in decisions, and by using small projects such as check dams and rainwater harvesting.

COMPETENCYC94 marks

9. Read and answer: In Meghalaya, farmers use a 200-year-old system in which bamboo pipes carry water from hill springs over long distances to plantations, delivering it to the plants as slow drops. (a) Which type of irrigation does this resemble? (b) Why is it efficient? (c) Why is it relevant today?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) Drip irrigation.
(b) Water goes straight to the plants, drop by drop, so very little is lost to evaporation or seepage.
(c) It saves scarce water, is cheap and eco-friendly, and suits hilly terrain, so it is a model for conservation.

LONGL104 marks

10. Write one important fact each about the Hirakud, Bhakra Nangal and Sardar Sarovar projects.

Show Answer

Ans:

Hirakud, on the Mahanadi in Odisha, is one of the longest dams in the world and was built mainly to control floods in the Mahanadi delta.
Bhakra Nangal, on the Sutlej, is India's highest straight gravity dam; it irrigates Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan and generates hydel power.
Sardar Sarovar, on the Narmada in Gujarat, has been at the centre of a long struggle over the displacement of adivasis and peasants, led by the Narmada Bachao Andolan.

LONGL115 marks

11. Multi-purpose river projects have several advantages but also several drawbacks. Explain.

Show Answer

Ans:

Advantages: they provide water for irrigation, generate hydroelectricity, control floods, help inland navigation, supply water to industries and cities, and provide fish breeding, recreation and landscape value. Nehru proudly called dams the temples of modern India.
Drawbacks: (i) large dams submerge forests and agricultural land and displace local communities, who are often not properly rehabilitated — the Narmada Bachao Andolan and the Tehri Dam Andolan grew out of this; (ii) they disturb the natural flow of rivers, causing poor sediment flow and excessive sedimentation at the bottom of the reservoir, which destroys habitats; (iii) they cause waterlogging and soil salinity in the command area; (iv) they trigger inter-state disputes over the sharing of costs and benefits, as between Karnataka, Maharashtra and Andhra Pradesh over the Krishna-Godavari waters; (v) they have induced earthquakes and, in the case of the Sardar Sarovar project, sparked protests when urban water supply was given priority over irrigation for farmers.

LONGL125 marks

12. Describe any four traditional rainwater-harvesting methods and the regions where they are used.

Show Answer

Ans:

Guls and kuls: diversion channels built in the hills of the Western Himalayas to carry stream water to fields.
Rooftop harvesting and tankas: in Rajasthan, rain from roofs flows into underground tanks that give drinking water through the dry season.
Khadins and johads: in Jaisalmer and other dry parts of Rajasthan, earthen embankments hold rain on farmland and recharge groundwater.
Inundation channels: in the flood plains of West Bengal, channels carry floodwater to fields. (Meghalaya’s bamboo drip irrigation is another example.)
