MCQs · Drill

MCQM11 mark

1. The oldest printed Japanese book, the Buddhist Diamond Sutra, was printed in: (a) AD 768 (b) AD 868 (c) AD 1450 (d) AD 1579

Show Answer

Ans:

(b) AD 868, after Chinese Buddhist missionaries brought hand printing to Japan around AD 768–770.

MCQM21 mark

2. The first English newspaper in India, the Bengal Gazette (1780), was started by: (a) Raja Rammohun Roy (b) Bal Gangadhar Tilak (c) James Augustus Hicky (d) William Carey

Show Answer

Ans:

(c) James Augustus Hicky, who described it as 'a commercial paper open to all parties but influenced by none'.

MCQM31 mark

3. Kesari and Maratha were newspapers edited by: (a) Bal Gangadhar Tilak (b) Mahatma Gandhi (c) Raja Rammohun Roy (d) Bharatendu Harishchandra

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) Bal Gangadhar Tilak, whose imprisonment in 1908 for supporting the Punjab revolutionaries provoked protests across the country.

MCQM41 mark

4. Johann Gutenberg developed the printing press in the 1430s in: (a) Venice, Italy (b) Paris, France (c) London, England (d) Strasbourg, Germany

Show Answer

Ans:

(d) Strasbourg, Germany. Gutenberg, a goldsmith, developed the metal-type press at Strasbourg.

MCQM51 mark

5. In England, small, cheap books sold by petty pedlars were called chapbooks, and the pedlars were known as: (a) scribes (b) calligraphers (c) missionaries (d) chapmen

Show Answer

Ans:

(d) chapmen. Penny chapbooks carried by chapmen let even poor people buy printed books.

MCQM61 mark

6. The first book printed by Johann Gutenberg was the: (a) Quran (b) Bible (c) Mahabharata (d) Ramcharitmanas

Show Answer

Ans:

(b) Bible. He printed about 180 copies on parchment and paper around 1455.

MCQM71 mark

7. The Vernacular Press Act was enacted in: (a) 1919 (b) 1905 (c) 1878 (d) 1857

Show Answer

Ans:

(c) 1878. It empowered the government to seize the presses and property of critical vernacular newspapers.

MCQM81 mark

8. Kesari was edited by: (a) Bal Gangadhar Tilak (b) Raja Rammohun Roy (c) Mahatma Gandhi (d) Jawaharlal Nehru

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) Bal Gangadhar Tilak. He used it, along with Mahratta in English, to spread nationalist ideas in Maharashtra.

Written questions · Write

SHORTS12 marks

1. What were the penny dreadfuls and what do they tell us about the readers of print?

Show Answer

Ans:

Penny dreadfuls were cheap serial novels sold in Victorian Britain for a penny. They show that print had begun to reach working-class readers, that publishers were producing for a mass market rather than for the elite, and that the new readers wanted exciting, escapist stories.

SHORTS23 marks

2. How did Gutenberg develop the printing press?

Show Answer

Ans:

Gutenberg grew up on a large agricultural estate and from childhood had seen wine and olive presses. By the 1430s he had perfected the printing press by adapting the olive press, and he developed a metal mould for casting types. His press could print far more copies than hand copying — his first book, the Bible, was printed in about three years, producing 180 copies.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Why did Martin Luther's ideas spread so rapidly across Europe?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because his writings were printed. When he posted his Ninety-Five Theses on the church door at Wittenberg in 1517, printers reproduced them in vast numbers within days, and they were read widely and discussed everywhere. His German translation of the New Testament sold 5,000 copies within a few weeks — something impossible in a manuscript culture.

SHORTS43 marks

4. Why were manuscripts not widely used in everyday life in pre-colonial India?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because they were written on palm leaf or fragile handmade paper, were expensive to produce, and could not be handled or read easily. They were also copied by hand, so copies were few and easily damaged, and the script was written in a variety of styles that made reading difficult. Knowledge was therefore largely transmitted orally — texts were recited and memorised rather than read.

SHORTS53 marks

5. What was the Vernacular Press Act of 1878?

Show Answer

Ans:

It was a law modelled on Irish press laws that gave the government extensive powers to censor reports and editorials in the vernacular press. If a newspaper published anything judged seditious and did not heed an initial warning, its press could be confiscated, including its printing machinery. It was widely resented and became a symbol of colonial repression of the Indian press.

APPLICATIONA63 marks

6. A student writes: “The printing press was invented in India by the British in the nineteenth century.” Correct the statement.

Show Answer

Ans:

Printing technology came from China and Japan (woodblock printing); Marco Polo brought knowledge of it to Italy in 1295.
Johann Gutenberg developed the modern printing press in Strasbourg, Germany, in the 1430s.
The press first came to India with Portuguese missionaries in Goa in the mid-sixteenth century, long before the British press.

APPLICATIONA73 marks

7. What was the Index of Prohibited Books, and what happened to the Italian miller Menocchio?

Show Answer

Ans:

From 1558, the Roman Catholic Church kept an Index of Prohibited Books and controlled printers and booksellers, because print was spreading ideas it did not accept.
Menocchio, a miller, read the books available to him and formed his own interpretation of the Bible and the nature of God.
The Church charged him with heresy and he was executed. His case shows both the power of print to spread ideas and the Church’s effort to control it.

SHORTS83 marks

8. Describe how print came to India and the role it played in social reform.

Show Answer

Ans:

Printing presses were first set up in Goa by Christian missionaries in the mid-sixteenth century; from the 1780s Bengali and other vernacular presses multiplied.
Reformers used print: Raja Rammohun Roy published tracts against sati and idolatry, and Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar wrote in favour of widow remarriage.
Women read and wrote in the popular Bengali almanacs and domestic magazines, and public libraries and cheap books carried these debates to ordinary readers.

COMPETENCYC94 marks

9. Read and answer: Rashsundari Debi, a young married woman in an orthodox household in Bengal, taught herself to read in secret in her kitchen. In 1876 she published her life story. (a) Name her book. (b) Why is it historically important? (c) Why did she have to learn in secret?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) Amar Jiban.
(b) It was the first full-length autobiography published in the Bengali language.
(c) Many families strongly opposed education for women, so she learned secretly in the kitchen.

COMPETENCYC104 marks

10. Read and answer: In 1907, when revolutionaries in Punjab were deported, Bal Gangadhar Tilak wrote with great sympathy about them in his newspaper. (a) Name the newspaper. (b) What happened to Tilak in 1908? (c) What does the episode show about the press and nationalism?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) Kesari.
(b) He was imprisoned, which provoked widespread protests all over India.
(c) Nationalist newspapers openly criticised colonial rule and roused public feeling; the government in turn tried to punish and control them.

LONGL114 marks

11. Explain why colonial governments tried to control the press and how nationalist newspapers responded.

Show Answer

Ans:

The colonial state feared that criticism in the vernacular press would spread sedition and unite people against British rule.
It used measures such as the Vernacular Press Act of 1878, which allowed it to confiscate the assets of newspapers that published objectionable material, and it monitored Indian-owned presses.
Nationalist papers such as Kesari (Tilak), Amrita Bazar Patrika and The Hindu evaded these controls, reported official repression, and used cartoons, ballads and vernacular prose to reach ordinary readers.
Print thus created a public opinion outside the government's control and became a major force in the freedom struggle.

LONGL125 marks

12. How did print culture help the growth of nationalism in India?

Show Answer

Ans:

Newspapers carried nationalist criticism. Vernacular newspapers such as Tilak's Kesari and Maratha reported colonial misrule and supported the Punjab revolutionaries; Tilak's imprisonment in 1908 provoked widespread protest.
Religious and social reform debates were printed. Raja Rammohun Roy's Sambad Kaumudi, the Mirat-ul-Akhbar, and the fatwas of the Deoband seminary spread reformist and religious ideas to a wide readership.
Cheap books and libraries made knowledge available to the poor and to workers, and novels and pamphlets carried political ideas in an easily readable form.
Writings by and about women — Tarabai Shinde's Stripurushtulna, Pandita Ramabai on widowhood — created a new public debate on social questions.
Censorship itself proved the point. The Vernacular Press Act of 1878, which allowed the government to seize presses, showed how powerful print had become, and made the press a recognised weapon of the national movement.

LONGL135 marks

13. Explain how print culture created the conditions for the French Revolution.

Show Answer

Ans:

Print popularised the ideas of Enlightenment thinkers such as Voltaire and Rousseau, who criticised tradition, superstition and despotism and stressed reason.
Print created a new culture of dialogue and debate; people began to question old values and norms and to discuss reform.
By the 1780s there was a flood of literature that mocked the royalty and criticised their morals; cartoons and writings made people hostile to the monarchy.
Together, these developments weakened the authority of the king and the Church and helped the Revolution to take place.
Historians therefore argue that print did not cause the Revolution on its own, but made it possible.
