MCQs · Drill

MCQM11 mark

1. Which of these is an endangered species in India? (a) cattle (b) Asiatic elephant (c) blue sheep (d) black buck

Show Answer

Ans:

(d) Black buck. The Asiatic elephant and the blue sheep are vulnerable; cattle are a normal species.

MCQM21 mark

2. Project Tiger was launched in: (a) 1972 (b) 1973 (c) 1980 (d) 1988

Show Answer

Ans:

(b) 1973. The Wildlife (Protection) Act came a year earlier, in 1972.

MCQM31 mark

3. Forests of the highest conservation value, where entry is banned, are: (a) reserved forests (b) unclassed forests (c) protected forests (d) sacred groves

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) Reserved forests, which cover more than half the total forest land of the country.

MCQM41 mark

4. A species that is not seen in the areas it is known or likely to inhabit, after repeated searches, is called: (a) extinct (b) endangered (c) rare (d) vulnerable

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) extinct. Extinct species are those no longer found anywhere in their known or possible habitats.

MCQM51 mark

5. Which of the following is a biosphere reserve of India? (a) Kaziranga (b) Corbett (c) Ranthambore (d) Nilgiri

Show Answer

Ans:

(d) Nilgiri. The Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve was the first in India; the other three are well-known national parks or tiger reserves.

MCQM61 mark

6. The ‘Red Data Book' is a record of: (a) national parks (b) forest area (c) endangered species (d) soil types

Show Answer

Ans:

(c) endangered species. It is maintained by the IUCN and lists plants and animals facing extinction.

MCQM71 mark

7. The Bishnoi community, famous for protecting blackbuck and khejarli trees, lives in: (a) Kerala (b) Rajasthan (c) Assam (d) West Bengal

Show Answer

Ans:

(b) Rajasthan. In 1731, 363 Bishnois were killed while protecting trees near what is now Jodhpur.

MCQM81 mark

8. The Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act was enacted in: (a) 1992 (b) 1980 (c) 1972 (d) 1952

Show Answer

Ans:

(c) 1972. It provides the legal basis for protected areas and for the protection of endangered species.

Written questions · Write

SHORTS12 marks

1. What is biodiversity and why is it important?

Show Answer

Ans:

Biodiversity is the variety of plant and animal life found in a region or in the world. It keeps ecosystems stable, recycles nutrients, maintains the climate and water cycle, and supplies food, medicines, fibre and other raw materials on which human life depends.

SHORTS23 marks

2. What are the categories of species recognised for conservation? Give one example of each.

Show Answer

Ans:

Normal species — population levels are normal, e.g. cattle and rodents.
Endangered species — in danger of extinction, e.g. the black buck and the Indian rhino.
Vulnerable species — declining and likely to become endangered, e.g. the Asiatic elephant.
Rare species — a small population in a limited area, e.g. the Himalayan brown bear.
Extinct species — no longer found, e.g. the Asiatic cheetah.
Endemic species — found only in one area, e.g. the Andaman teal.

SHORTS33 marks

3. How have local communities conserved forests and wildlife? Give two examples.

Show Answer

Ans:

Villagers around the Sariska Tiger Reserve in Alwar (Rajasthan) fought against mining in the reserve, and villages of the Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri' in the same district framed their own rules banning hunting and protecting wildlife. The Chipko movement stopped felling in the Himalayas when women hugged the trees, the Beej Bachao Andolan saved indigenous seeds, and in sacred groves such as the Sarna and devrai, forests are protected by religious custom rather than by law.

SHORTS43 marks

4. What is Joint Forest Management and why does it matter?

Show Answer

Ans:

Joint Forest Management, introduced in 1988 and first adopted in Odisha, is a programme in which local communities protect and regenerate forests together with the Forest Department, in return for a share of the produce of the forest. It matters because protection works only when the people who live next to the forest have an interest in it.

APPLICATIONA53 marks

5. A student writes that the Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act was passed in 1973, the same year as Project Tiger. Correct the dates and state two things the Act did.

Show Answer

Ans:

The Wildlife (Protection) Act was passed in 1972; Project Tiger was launched in 1973.
The Act banned hunting and gave legal protection to wildlife habitats.
It also set up protected areas such as national parks and sanctuaries, and drew up lists of protected species.

APPLICATIONA63 marks

6. A forest officer finds that in a district more than half of the forest land is permanently reserved for conservation, about one-third is protected from further depletion, and the rest belongs to the government, individuals or communities. Name the three categories and say which is most valuable for conservation.

Show Answer

Ans:

The three categories are reserved forests, protected forests and unclassed forests.
Reserved forests are more than half of the forest land and are regarded as the most valuable for conservation.
Protected forests (almost one-third) are guarded from further depletion; unclassed forests are the rest, owned by the government, individuals and communities.

SHORTS73 marks

7. Distinguish between a wildlife sanctuary, a national park and a biosphere reserve.

Show Answer

Ans:

In a wildlife sanctuary, hunting is banned and some human activity such as grazing or collection of forest produce is allowed under restrictions.
In a national park, no human activity at all is permitted inside the core area; grazing, forestry and cultivation are prohibited.
A biosphere reserve is a larger area divided into core, buffer and transition zones, in which conservation, research and limited human use go together; a reserve may include several sanctuaries and parks.

COMPETENCYC84 marks

8. Read and answer: In some villages, a patch of forest is left untouched as a sacred grove: no tree may be cut and no animal killed, because the grove is considered the home of a deity. (a) What does this show about traditional attitudes to nature? (b) How does a sacred grove help biodiversity? (c) How can the Forest Department work with such communities?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) Many communities protect nature out of religious and cultural belief and see themselves as part of it.
(b) The undisturbed grove keeps the habitat intact, so plants and animals, including rare species, survive there.
(c) Through Joint Forest Management, in which villagers help protect the forest and in return receive a share of the benefits.

COMPETENCYC94 marks

9. Read and answer: In the Tehri region, some farmers have stopped using synthetic chemicals and instead grow a variety of local crops, conserving traditional seeds. (a) Name the movement associated with this. (b) What are the benefits of such crop diversity? (c) How is it linked to forest and wildlife conservation?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) The Beej Bachao Andolan (the Navdanya movement is similar).
(b) It preserves local varieties of seeds, keeps soil healthy, lowers costs and reduces the risk of losing a whole crop to one pest or drought.
(c) It shows that people can earn a living without depending on destroying forests, and it protects agricultural biodiversity.

LONGL104 marks

10. Describe the Chipko movement and what it achieved.

Show Answer

Ans:

It began in 1974 in the Garhwal Himalayas when villagers, mostly women, hugged trees to stop contractors from felling them.
Led by Sunderlal Bahuguna and Gaura Devi, it spread across Uttarakhand and used songs and satyagraha to protect the forests.
The government was forced to ban commercial felling in the Himalayan forests for fifteen years.
The movement showed that local communities can manage forests better than distant contractors, and it inspired later conservation struggles in India.

LONGL115 marks

11. Explain any five causes of the depletion of forests and wildlife in India.

Show Answer

Ans:

Agricultural expansion and shifting cultivation have cleared large forest tracts.
Large development projects — dams, roads, railways and mines — submerge or cut through forests.
Mining and quarrying destroy habitats and pollute streams.
Grazing and the collection of fuel wood prevent regeneration.
Poaching and illegal trade in skins, tusks and horns target the most threatened species.
Habitat destruction and fragmentation leave small populations that a single fire or disease can wipe out.

LONGL125 marks

12. Why should we conserve forests and wildlife? Give five reasons.

Show Answer

Ans:

Ecological balance: forests and wildlife maintain the life-support systems of air, water and soil.
Biodiversity: the variety of plants and animals provides the genetic resources for crops and medicines.
Livelihood: forests give timber, fuel, fodder, fruits and medicinal plants, on which many communities depend.
Climate and water: forests regulate rainfall and temperature, and recharge groundwater.
Culture and heritage: forests and wildlife have spiritual and cultural value, and future generations deserve to inherit them.
