Ch 1 · The Rise of Nationalism in Europe (13)

Save this chapter: Text (.txt) ·

SHORTS12 marks

1. Who were the Jacobins and what did they do during the French Revolution?

Show Answer

Ans:

The Jacobins were members of a political club led by Maximilian Robespierre. They overthrew the monarchy, declared France a republic, and gave the nation its symbols — the tricolour flag, the red cap (liberty cap) and the new national anthem, the Marseillaise — through which people could identify with the nation.

SHORTS23 marks

2. Interpret the significance of the allegory of Marianne and Germania.

Show Answer

Ans:

Both were female allegories used to give the abstract idea of the nation a concrete visual form. Marianne, with her red cap, tricolour and cockade, represented the ideals of liberty and the French Republic, and statues of her were installed in public squares to remind citizens of national unity. Germania, wearing a crown of oak leaves, symbolised heroism and the German nation. Allegories made it easy for people to identify with the nation.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Briefly trace the process of German unification.

Show Answer

Ans:

Middle-class Germans tried to unite the German Confederation through a parliament elected in 1848, but the Frankfurt assembly failed when Friedrich Wilhelm IV rejected the offered crown. Prussia then took over the leadership, and its Chief Minister Otto von Bismarck used the army and the bureaucracy to fight three wars over seven years against Austria, Denmark and France. Prussian victories ended in the proclamation of the German Empire at Versailles in January 1871, with Kaiser Wilhelm I as emperor.

SHORTS43 marks

4. What steps did the French revolutionaries take to create a sense of collective identity among the French people?

Show Answer

Ans:

They introduced the ideas of la patrie (the fatherland) and le citoyen (the citizen), emphasising a united community with equal rights under a constitution. A new tricolour replaced the royal standard, the Estates General was renamed the National Assembly and elected by active citizens, a uniform system of weights and measures was adopted, internal customs duties were abolished, and French as spoken in Paris became the common language. Oaths of loyalty to the constitution and commemoration of martyrs were also used.

SHORTS53 marks

5. How did culture play an important role in creating the idea of the nation in Europe?

Show Answer

Ans:

Romantic artists and poets criticised the glorification of reason and science and turned to emotion, intuition and mysticism, using folk songs, folk poetry and folk dances to reach the people. Language too was used as a weapon of national resistance — in Poland, for example, the use of Polish in schools and church gatherings became a symbol of struggle against Russian dominance. Operas and music carried nationalist feelings to a largely illiterate audience.

APPLICATIONA63 marks

6. A student writes: “The Frankfurt Parliament of 1848 proclaimed the German Empire under Kaiser William I.” Correct the statement.

Show Answer

Ans:

In 1848 the Frankfurt Parliament, meeting in the Church of St Paul, drafted a constitution for a German nation and offered the crown to King Frederick William IV of Prussia, who rejected it.
The German Empire was proclaimed in January 1871, at Versailles, after Prussia defeated France.
William I of Prussia became the German Emperor; the process was led by the Prussian chief minister, Otto von Bismarck, using the army, not by an elected assembly.

APPLICATIONA73 marks

7. What was the main aim of the Treaty of Vienna (1815)? State two arrangements it made.

Show Answer

Ans:

Its aim was to undo most of the changes Napoleon had made and to restore the old monarchies, the conservative order of Europe.
The Bourbon dynasty was restored to France, and states were set up on France’s boundaries (such as the Kingdom of the Netherlands, including Belgium) to prevent French expansion.
Prussia received new territories on its western frontier, and Austria received control of northern Italy.

SHORTS83 marks

8. Describe the condition of the Balkans in the nineteenth century and why the region caused tension in Europe.

Show Answer

Ans:

The Balkans was a region of great ethnic and religious diversity that had been part of the Ottoman Empire.
As Ottoman power weakened, the Slavic peoples demanded independence, and the Balkan states fought each other over territory.
Big powers such as Russia, Austria-Hungary, Britain and Germany each wanted influence over the region. These rivalries made the Balkans the powder keg of Europe and helped trigger the First World War in 1914.

COMPETENCYC94 marks

9. Read and answer: Women had formed political associations, started newspapers and joined political meetings in the 1840s. Yet when the Frankfurt Parliament met in 1848, they were allowed in only as observers, standing in the visitors’ gallery. (a) In which church did the Frankfurt Parliament meet? (b) What does the women’s position show about the right to vote at that time? (c) What conclusion can be drawn about liberal nationalism in 1848?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) The Church of St Paul in Frankfurt.
(b) Women had been denied suffrage; the right to vote was reserved for men.
(c) Liberal nationalism demanded a constitution and representation but was limited: it excluded women (and the propertyless), so it was not fully democratic.

COMPETENCYC104 marks

10. Read and answer: Greece had been part of the Ottoman Empire since the fifteenth century. Greek nationalists abroad and many West Europeans who admired ancient Greek culture supported the fight for independence. (a) Which treaty of 1832 recognised Greece as an independent nation? (b) Name an English poet who helped the Greek cause. (c) What does this show about the role of culture in nationalism?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) The Treaty of Constantinople.
(b) Lord Byron, who raised funds and went to fight in the war; he died of fever in 1824.
(c) Poets, artists and writers can arouse sympathy and public opinion; admiration for Greece as the cradle of European culture helped a political cause.

LONGL114 marks

11. Explain how the French Revolution spread the idea of nationalism across Europe.

Show Answer

Ans:

The revolution transferred sovereignty from the monarch to the nation: the people, not the king, were declared the source of power.
It gave the French people common symbols — the tricolour, the national anthem and a new calendar — and introduced a uniform administrative system with equal laws and one currency.
Napoleon's armies carried these ideas into the territories they conquered; although his rule was resented, the idea that people sharing a common language and history should form one nation took root.
This idea shaped the unification movements of Italy and Germany and the struggles of the Balkan peoples in the nineteenth century.

LONGL125 marks

12. Write a note on the role of Giuseppe Mazzini, Count Cavour and Giuseppe Garibaldi in the unification of Italy.

Show Answer

Ans:

Giuseppe Mazzini — a member of the secret society Carbonari, he founded Young Italy in Marseilles to work for a unitary Italian republic. He provided the ideological inspiration and the programme.
Count Cavour — Chief Minister of Sardinia-Piedmont, he led the unification movement through diplomacy rather than revolution, and with the help of France and the army defeated Austria in 1859.
Giuseppe Garibaldi — he led the armed volunteers known as the Red Shirts, marched into the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies in 1860, won the support of the local peasants and drove out the Spanish rulers.
In 1861 Victor Emmanuel II was proclaimed king of united Italy, with Cavour as his Chief Minister. Much of the peasant population, however, knew nothing of the ideals of liberal nationalism.

LONGL135 marks

13. Describe five administrative changes introduced by Napoleon in the territories under his control. Why did people eventually become unhappy with his rule?

Show Answer

Ans:

He introduced the Civil Code of 1804 (Napoleonic Code): it abolished privileges based on birth, established equality before the law and secured the right to property.
He simplified administrative divisions and abolished the feudal system, freeing peasants from serfdom and manorial dues.
He removed guild restrictions in towns and improved transport and communication.
Uniform weights and measures and a common national currency made trade and the movement of goods easier.
At first people welcomed these reforms as liberation, but later they were disillusioned by higher taxes, censorship and forced conscription into the French armies.

Ch 2 · Nationalism in India (12)

SHORTS12 marks

1. What was the Rowlatt Act of 1919 and why was it opposed?

Show Answer

Ans:

It allowed the colonial government to imprison any person suspected of sedition without trial for up to two years. Indians saw it as a betrayal after the war and Gandhiji called for a hartal on 6 April 1919 against what he called the ‘black act'.

SHORTS23 marks

2. Why was the Salt March an effective symbol of resistance against colonialism?

Show Answer

Ans:

Salt was consumed by rich and poor alike and was one of the most essential items of food, yet the tax on it and the government monopoly over its production revealed the most oppressive face of British rule. Choosing it united the whole country behind a single demand, and breaking the salt law by boiling sea water at Dandi was simple, visible and easy for every villager to repeat, so it quickly spread into a nationwide movement.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Describe the Jallianwala Bagh incident.

Show Answer

Ans:

On 13 April 1919, a large crowd gathered in the enclosed ground of Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar — some to protest against the repressive Rowlatt Act, others to attend the annual Baisakhi fair. Unaware of the martial law imposed in the city, they were trapped when General Dyer entered, blocked the exit points and opened fire on the unarmed crowd, killing hundreds. His object, as he later declared, was to produce a moral effect and create terror in the minds of satyagrahis. News of the massacre spread, and crowds took to the streets in many north Indian towns.

SHORTS43 marks

4. Why did Gandhiji decide to withdraw the Non-Cooperation Movement in February 1922?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because the movement was turning violent. At Chauri Chaura in Gorakhpur, a peaceful demonstration turned into a violent clash and the crowd burnt a police station with policemen inside. Gandhi felt that satyagrahis needed proper training before they could take part in a mass struggle, and that violence would give the British an excuse to crush the movement, so he called it off.

APPLICATIONA53 marks

5. A student writes that the Salt March began in 1920 and that Gandhiji ended the Non-Cooperation Movement because of the salt tax. Correct both statements.

Show Answer

Ans:

The Salt (Dandi) March began on 12 March 1930, from Sabarmati Ashram, and reached Dandi on 6 April, where Gandhiji broke the salt law; it started the Civil Disobedience Movement.
The Non-Cooperation Movement was begun at the end of 1920 and was withdrawn in February 1922, after the violent incident at Chauri Chaura, where a mob set a police station on fire and killed policemen.
The salt tax had nothing to do with calling off Non-Cooperation.

APPLICATIONA63 marks

6. How did the peasants of Awadh and the tribal people of the Gudem Hills each understand Gandhiji’s call for swaraj?

Show Answer

Ans:

Awadh: led by Baba Ramchandra, peasants fought talukdars and landlords who demanded high rents and forced labour (begar). For them swaraj meant lower revenue, an end to begar and a social boycott of oppressive landlords.
Gudem Hills: tribal people, led by Alluri Sitaram Raju, were angered by colonial rules that restricted entry into the forests. For them swaraj meant the freedom to move freely in the forests and use forest produce.
In both cases people gave the call their own local meaning, and some, like Raju, took to militant methods that Gandhiji did not approve.

SHORTS73 marks

7. Describe the participation of women and students in the national movement.

Show Answer

Ans:

Women joined processions, picketed shops selling liquor and foreign cloth, and went to jail; several, such as Sarojini Naidu, became national leaders.
Students left government schools and colleges during the Non-Cooperation Movement and joined national schools and colleges.
Their participation widened the social base of the movement, turning it from a middle-class agitation into a mass struggle.

COMPETENCYC84 marks

8. Read and answer: In December 1929, the Congress session at Lahore, presided over by Jawaharlal Nehru, took a major decision. Earlier, the Simon Commission, which had no Indian member, had been greeted with “Go back, Simon!” slogans. (a) What goal did the Lahore session adopt? (b) On which date was “Independence Day” to be observed? (c) Why did the Congress turn more radical after the Simon Commission?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) Purna Swaraj — complete independence from British rule.
(b) 26 January 1930.
(c) The Commission, set up to review the constitutional system, had no Indian members, so it was boycotted; the Viceroy’s vague offer of dominion status satisfied no one, and the demand moved to full independence.

COMPETENCYC94 marks

9. Read and answer: During the Civil Disobedience Movement, Dr B. R. Ambedkar and Mahatma Gandhi disagreed over how the “depressed classes” should be represented. (a) What did Dr Ambedkar demand? (b) Why did Gandhiji oppose it, and what did he do? (c) What was the outcome in September 1932?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) Separate electorates for the depressed classes.
(b) Gandhiji believed separate electorates would slow their integration into society, and he began a fast unto death.
(c) The Poona Pact: Ambedkar accepted reserved seats for the depressed classes in the provincial and central councils, to be voted for by the general electorate.

LONGL104 marks

10. Describe the course of the Civil Disobedience Movement and explain why it was withdrawn.

Show Answer

Ans:

It began with the Salt March: on 12 March 1930 Gandhiji set out from Sabarmati with 78 followers and broke the salt law at Dandi on 6 April.
People refused to pay taxes, boycotted foreign cloth and liquor, and peasants and tribals agitated against forest laws and land revenue.
Gandhiji suspended it after the Gandhi-Irwin Pact of March 1931, revived it in 1932, and finally withdrew it in April 1934 when mass support had faded and the government had crushed the movement.

LONGL115 marks

11. How did the Non-Cooperation Movement differ from the Civil Disobedience Movement?

Show Answer

Ans:

Non-Cooperation (1920–22): people were asked to refuse all cooperation with the British government — students left schools, lawyers gave up practice, foreign cloth was boycotted and burnt. It was launched with the Khilafat issue and withdrawn by Gandhi after the Chauri Chaura violence.
Civil Disobedience (1930–34): people were asked not merely to refuse cooperation but to break colonial laws — the salt law, forest laws, refusal to pay revenue and chaukidari taxes, boycott of foreign cloth and picketing of liquor shops.
Participation: Civil Disobedience saw much larger participation by women, who left their homes to join protest marches and manufacture salt.
Outcome: Non-Cooperation was withdrawn in 1922; Civil Disobedience was suspended by the Gandhi-Irwin Pact in 1931, revived after the failed Round Table Conference, and finally lost momentum by 1934.

LONGL125 marks

12. Explain the idea of the Sense of Collective Belonging with reference to nationalism in India.

Show Answer

Ans:

Nationalism develops when people begin to believe that they are part of the same nation, and this feeling is created through history and fiction, folklore and songs, and popular prints.
Images: Bharat Mata, first created by Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay and painted by Abhinindranath Tagore as a calm, divine ascetic figure, came to symbolise the nation; devotion to her became a sign of nationalism.
Songs: Vande Mataram, written by Bankim Chandra in the 1870s and set in Anandamath, was widely sung during the Swadeshi movement.
Folklore: in Bengal, Natesan Sastri and others collected folk tales and songs, arguing that these were the truest manifestation of national identity.
Flags: the Swadeshi flag of 1905 and the Swaraj flag of 1921, with the spinning wheel, became symbols of defiance; carrying them was itself an act of rebellion.
History: Indians began writing of India's ancient achievements in mathematics, science, law and philosophy, to counter the colonial view of Indians as backward and to instil pride in the past.

Ch 3 · The Making of a Global World (12)

SHORTS12 marks

1. What was the Corn Laws controversy in Britain?

Show Answer

Ans:

The Corn Laws restricted the import of cheap corn into Britain, keeping bread prices high to protect landowners. Workers and industrialists demanded their repeal; after repeal in 1846, food flowed in from the colonies and eastern Europe, bread became cheaper, but many European peasants were forced to migrate to the Americas.

SHORTS23 marks

2. What were the Silk Routes and what did they carry besides goods?

Show Answer

Ans:

The Silk Routes were a network of pre-modern trade and cultural routes linking Asia with Europe and northern Africa. Besides goods such as Chinese silk, Indian cotton textiles and spices going west and silver going east, they carried religions — Buddhism, and later Christianity and Islam — and also carried diseases, since germs travelled with the traders and pilgrims.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Explain how the Columbian exchange changed diets in the Old World, giving examples.

Show Answer

Ans:

Before the discovery of America, foods such as potatoes, soya, groundnuts, maize, tomatoes, chillies and sweet potatoes were unknown in Europe and Asia. These American crops reached the Old World after Columbus's voyages and changed what people ate. The poor of Europe, who had earlier eaten mostly bread and porridge, now had a more varied and abundant diet, and in Ireland the potato became the staple of the poorest sections.

SHORTS43 marks

4. Why did the Spanish conquest of America succeed so easily?

Show Answer

Ans:

Mainly because of disease. The Spanish conquerors carried smallpox germs, and the original inhabitants of America had no immunity to them because of their long isolation from the rest of the world. Smallpox spread and killed huge numbers, decimating whole communities, so conquest became easy. Guns could be bought or fought against, but disease could not.

SHORTS53 marks

5. Describe the role of silver from Peru and Mexico in the global economy of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

Show Answer

Ans:

The discovery of rich silver mines in Peru and Mexico made silver available in huge quantities. It was shipped to Europe and from there used to finance Europe's wealth and its expanding trade with Asia. Silver thus became the link that connected the Americas, Europe and Asia in a single flow of money and goods.

APPLICATIONA63 marks

6. A student writes that the potato originated in Ireland and that the Irish famine happened because the potato is a poor crop. Correct this.

Show Answer

Ans:

The potato came from the Americas and reached Europe after Columbus’s voyages in the sixteenth century.
It gave poor Europeans cheap, nutritious food, so they ate better and lived longer.
Poor peasants in Ireland came to depend on the potato so heavily that when a disease destroyed the crop in the mid-1840s, hundreds of thousands starved. The problem was the over-dependence, not that the potato itself was poor.

APPLICATIONA73 marks

7. Trace how (i) cowrie shells, (ii) noodles and (iii) Buddhism travelled between regions in the pre-modern world.

Show Answer

Ans:

(i) Cowries were carried from the Maldives to China and to East Africa and were used as a form of money.
(ii) Noodles are believed to have travelled from China to the West, where they became spaghetti in Italy (Arab traders also carried pasta to Sicily).
(iii) Buddhism emerged in eastern India and spread to Central Asia, China, Korea and Japan along the Silk Routes.

SHORTS83 marks

8. Explain what indentured labour was and its effects in the nineteenth century.

Show Answer

Ans:

After slavery was abolished, plantations in the Caribbean, Fiji, Mauritius, Sri Lanka and Natal needed cheap labour.
Millions of workers were recruited from India, China and Africa on contracts (girmit) for a fixed number of years, often after being misled about the terms.
They were carried in crowded ships and worked in harsh conditions, but they also carried Indian languages, festivals and food abroad.
Indentured migration from India was finally abolished in 1917 after the nationalist movement raised the issue.

COMPETENCYC94 marks

9. Read and answer: Before Columbus, European markets had no tomatoes, potatoes, maize or chillies. A few centuries later, these foods were common in many parts of the world. (a) Where did these foods come from? (b) How did some of them help poor people in Europe? (c) How did the arrival of Europeans harm the original inhabitants of the Americas?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) From the Americas.
(b) The potato and maize gave cheap, nourishing food, so the poor ate better and lived longer.
(c) The conquerors brought diseases such as smallpox, to which the local people had no immunity, killing millions; they also took over lands and forced people to work.

COMPETENCYC104 marks

10. Read and answer: The legend of El Dorado, a land of gold, drew many Europeans to the Americas. In the sixteenth century, silver mined in Peru and Mexico began to reach Europe. (a) What was El Dorado? (b) How did American silver affect Europe? (c) How did it affect Europe’s trade with Asia?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) A legendary city or land of gold that Portuguese and Spanish explorers hoped to find.
(b) Silver from the mines of Peru and Mexico added to Europe’s wealth.
(c) It financed Europe’s trade with Asia, since Europe paid for Asian goods such as textiles and spices largely in silver.

LONGL114 marks

11. Explain how the First World War and the Great Depression changed the world economy.

Show Answer

Ans:

The First World War turned Europe from a creditor into a debtor and made the United States the world's main lender.
The Wall Street Crash of 1929 and the Depression of the 1930s collapsed world trade, output and employment; agricultural prices and farm incomes fell sharply, hitting colonised countries hardest.
Countries raised tariffs and formed imperial preference blocs, and migration and lending fell.
The crisis destroyed confidence in unregulated markets and led to state intervention — for example Roosevelt's New Deal — and to a demand for decolonisation.

LONGL125 marks

12. Describe the role of the Silk Routes in the pre-modern world, with examples of what travelled along them.

Show Answer

Ans:

The Silk Routes were a network of land and sea routes linking Asia with Europe and northern Africa; they were named after the Chinese silk carried along them.
Goods that travelled: Chinese pottery, textiles and spices from India, and precious metals going from Europe to Asia.
Ideas and religions travelled too: Christian missionaries, Muslim preachers and Buddhists took their faiths along these routes; Buddhism spread from India to Central Asia, China, Korea and Japan.
Food and technology also moved: noodles are believed to have travelled from China to Italy.
Thus the routes connected distant regions in trade and culture long before the modern age.

Ch 4 · Print Culture and the Modern World (13)

SHORTS12 marks

1. What were the penny dreadfuls and what do they tell us about the readers of print?

Show Answer

Ans:

Penny dreadfuls were cheap serial novels sold in Victorian Britain for a penny. They show that print had begun to reach working-class readers, that publishers were producing for a mass market rather than for the elite, and that the new readers wanted exciting, escapist stories.

SHORTS23 marks

2. How did Gutenberg develop the printing press?

Show Answer

Ans:

Gutenberg grew up on a large agricultural estate and from childhood had seen wine and olive presses. By the 1430s he had perfected the printing press by adapting the olive press, and he developed a metal mould for casting types. His press could print far more copies than hand copying — his first book, the Bible, was printed in about three years, producing 180 copies.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Why did Martin Luther's ideas spread so rapidly across Europe?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because his writings were printed. When he posted his Ninety-Five Theses on the church door at Wittenberg in 1517, printers reproduced them in vast numbers within days, and they were read widely and discussed everywhere. His German translation of the New Testament sold 5,000 copies within a few weeks — something impossible in a manuscript culture.

SHORTS43 marks

4. Why were manuscripts not widely used in everyday life in pre-colonial India?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because they were written on palm leaf or fragile handmade paper, were expensive to produce, and could not be handled or read easily. They were also copied by hand, so copies were few and easily damaged, and the script was written in a variety of styles that made reading difficult. Knowledge was therefore largely transmitted orally — texts were recited and memorised rather than read.

SHORTS53 marks

5. What was the Vernacular Press Act of 1878?

Show Answer

Ans:

It was a law modelled on Irish press laws that gave the government extensive powers to censor reports and editorials in the vernacular press. If a newspaper published anything judged seditious and did not heed an initial warning, its press could be confiscated, including its printing machinery. It was widely resented and became a symbol of colonial repression of the Indian press.

APPLICATIONA63 marks

6. A student writes: “The printing press was invented in India by the British in the nineteenth century.” Correct the statement.

Show Answer

Ans:

Printing technology came from China and Japan (woodblock printing); Marco Polo brought knowledge of it to Italy in 1295.
Johann Gutenberg developed the modern printing press in Strasbourg, Germany, in the 1430s.
The press first came to India with Portuguese missionaries in Goa in the mid-sixteenth century, long before the British press.

APPLICATIONA73 marks

7. What was the Index of Prohibited Books, and what happened to the Italian miller Menocchio?

Show Answer

Ans:

From 1558, the Roman Catholic Church kept an Index of Prohibited Books and controlled printers and booksellers, because print was spreading ideas it did not accept.
Menocchio, a miller, read the books available to him and formed his own interpretation of the Bible and the nature of God.
The Church charged him with heresy and he was executed. His case shows both the power of print to spread ideas and the Church’s effort to control it.

SHORTS83 marks

8. Describe how print came to India and the role it played in social reform.

Show Answer

Ans:

Printing presses were first set up in Goa by Christian missionaries in the mid-sixteenth century; from the 1780s Bengali and other vernacular presses multiplied.
Reformers used print: Raja Rammohun Roy published tracts against sati and idolatry, and Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar wrote in favour of widow remarriage.
Women read and wrote in the popular Bengali almanacs and domestic magazines, and public libraries and cheap books carried these debates to ordinary readers.

COMPETENCYC94 marks

9. Read and answer: Rashsundari Debi, a young married woman in an orthodox household in Bengal, taught herself to read in secret in her kitchen. In 1876 she published her life story. (a) Name her book. (b) Why is it historically important? (c) Why did she have to learn in secret?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) Amar Jiban.
(b) It was the first full-length autobiography published in the Bengali language.
(c) Many families strongly opposed education for women, so she learned secretly in the kitchen.

COMPETENCYC104 marks

10. Read and answer: In 1907, when revolutionaries in Punjab were deported, Bal Gangadhar Tilak wrote with great sympathy about them in his newspaper. (a) Name the newspaper. (b) What happened to Tilak in 1908? (c) What does the episode show about the press and nationalism?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) Kesari.
(b) He was imprisoned, which provoked widespread protests all over India.
(c) Nationalist newspapers openly criticised colonial rule and roused public feeling; the government in turn tried to punish and control them.

LONGL114 marks

11. Explain why colonial governments tried to control the press and how nationalist newspapers responded.

Show Answer

Ans:

The colonial state feared that criticism in the vernacular press would spread sedition and unite people against British rule.
It used measures such as the Vernacular Press Act of 1878, which allowed it to confiscate the assets of newspapers that published objectionable material, and it monitored Indian-owned presses.
Nationalist papers such as Kesari (Tilak), Amrita Bazar Patrika and The Hindu evaded these controls, reported official repression, and used cartoons, ballads and vernacular prose to reach ordinary readers.
Print thus created a public opinion outside the government's control and became a major force in the freedom struggle.

LONGL125 marks

12. How did print culture help the growth of nationalism in India?

Show Answer

Ans:

Newspapers carried nationalist criticism. Vernacular newspapers such as Tilak's Kesari and Maratha reported colonial misrule and supported the Punjab revolutionaries; Tilak's imprisonment in 1908 provoked widespread protest.
Religious and social reform debates were printed. Raja Rammohun Roy's Sambad Kaumudi, the Mirat-ul-Akhbar, and the fatwas of the Deoband seminary spread reformist and religious ideas to a wide readership.
Cheap books and libraries made knowledge available to the poor and to workers, and novels and pamphlets carried political ideas in an easily readable form.
Writings by and about women — Tarabai Shinde's Stripurushtulna, Pandita Ramabai on widowhood — created a new public debate on social questions.
Censorship itself proved the point. The Vernacular Press Act of 1878, which allowed the government to seize presses, showed how powerful print had become, and made the press a recognised weapon of the national movement.

1. Why is power sharing desirable in a democracy?

Show Answer

Ans:

It ensures that no single community or group can dominate the others, reduces the fear of oppression and the chances of conflict, respects the claims of minorities and regions, and makes the government more stable, accountable and legitimate in the eyes of the people.

SHORTS23 marks

Show Answer

Ans:

Prudential reasons stress the good consequences of power sharing — it reduces conflict between social groups, ensures the stability of political order and prevents violence and political instability. Moral reasons stress the intrinsic worth of power sharing — that people have a right to be consulted on how they are to be governed, and that a legitimate government is one in which citizens acquire a stake in the system through participation. The Union List contains subjects of national importance on which only Parliament can make laws, such as defence, foreign affairs, currency, railways and banking. Ans:

In a coming-together federation, independent states come together on their own to form a bigger unit, pooling sovereignty while retaining their identity — the USA, Switzerland and Australia. In a holding-together federation, a large country divides power between the constituent states and the national government, and the centre tends to be more powerful — India, Spain and Belgium.

13. The table shows illustrative figures for three countries. A: per capita income Rs 5,00,000, literacy 70%, life expectancy 65 years. B: Rs 2,00,000, literacy 98%, life expectancy 80 years. C: Rs 1,00,000, literacy 60%, life expectancy 62 years. Compare the countries and say which has the highest human development. Why does high income not guarantee it?

Show Answer

Ans:

By income: A > B > C. By education (literacy): B (98%) > A (70%) > C (60%). By health (life expectancy): B (80) > A (65) > C (62).
The Human Development Index combines income, education and health, not income alone.
So country B has the highest human development: it is ahead of A on two of the three measures.
Income is only a means: A’s high average income does not by itself give its people better schooling or longer lives.
An average also hides inequality, so a rich country can still have many poorly educated or unhealthy people.

Ch 18 · Sectors of the Indian Economy (13)

Save this chapter: Text (.txt) ·

SHORTS12 marks

1. Distinguish between the organised and the unorganised sectors.

Show Answer

Ans:

In the organised sector the work is registered, employees have regular hours, paid holidays, provident fund, gratuity, medical benefits and job security, and labour laws apply. In the unorganised sector the work is small-scale and unregistered, wages are low and irregular, working conditions are poor, and there is no job security, no social security and no protection of labour laws.

SHORTS23 marks

2. Distinguish between the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors with one example of each.

Show Answer

Ans:

The primary sector includes activities that use natural resources directly, such as agriculture, dairy, fishing and mining. The secondary sector includes activities in which natural products are changed into other forms through manufacturing, such as turning sugarcane into sugar or cotton into cloth. The tertiary sector includes activities that support the primary and secondary sectors without producing goods, such as transport, banking, trade, education and health.

SHORTS33 marks

3. What is disguised unemployment? Give an example from rural India.

Show Answer

Ans:

Disguised or hidden unemployment is a situation in which more people are employed in a job than are actually required, so that withdrawing some of them does not reduce production. In rural India, a family of five may all work on a small plot of land although three would be enough; if the extra two members leave, output does not fall. They appear employed but are in fact surplus.

SHORTS43 marks

4. What is GDP and why are only final goods counted in it?

Show Answer

Ans:

Gross Domestic Product is the total value of all final goods and services produced within a country during a particular year. Only final goods are counted because the value of intermediate goods is already included in the value of the final product — the value of wheat is included in the flour, and of the flour in the bread. Counting both would amount to double counting.

SHORTS53 marks

5. What is MGNREGA 2005 and how does it help to reduce unemployment?

Show Answer

Ans:

The Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act 2005 guarantees 100 days of wage employment in a year to every rural household whose adult members volunteer to do unskilled manual work. If the government fails to provide work within fifteen days, the applicant is entitled to an unemployment allowance. It creates a legal right to work, raises rural incomes, and builds rural assets such as ponds, roads and irrigation channels.

APPLICATIONA63 marks

6. To find its GDP contribution, a student adds the value of wheat (Rs 100), flour (Rs 140) and bread (Rs 200). Find the mistake and give the correct contribution.

Show Answer

Ans:

Mistake: wheat and flour are intermediate goods; their value is already included in the price of the bread.
Adding them counts the same value several times (double counting).
Only the final good is counted, so the contribution to GDP is Rs 200, not Rs 440.

APPLICATIONA73 marks

7. Under MGNREGA 2005, what is the maximum number of days of guaranteed wage employment per rural household in a year? If the daily wage were Rs 200, how much could one household earn at most?

Show Answer

Ans:

MGNREGA guarantees 100 days of employment per household in a year to any rural household that asks for work.
Maximum earning = 100 × Rs 200 = Rs 20,000 per year.
If the government cannot provide the work, it must pay an unemployment allowance. This makes work a legal right.

SHORTS83 marks

8. Explain underemployment with an example and suggest two measures to reduce it.

Show Answer

Ans:

Underemployment is the condition in which people work less than they could, or more people are engaged in a job than are needed.
Example: if five members of a family cultivate a plot that needs only two, the other three add nothing to output; they are disguisedly unemployed, especially during the lean season.
Measures: providing non-farm employment in villages through MGNREGA and rural industries, and improving irrigation so that two or three crops can be grown in a year.
Other measures: skill training so that workers can move to manufacturing and services, better roads and markets, and cheap institutional credit.

(a) The tertiary sector (60%).
(b) The primary sector (50% of workers).
(c) Half the workers share only 15% of GDP, so income per worker in the primary sector is low; many may be underemployed or in disguised unemployment.

COMPETENCYC104 marks

(a) Which are public-sector and which are private-sector activities? (b) Why does the government run railways and electricity supply? (c) State one difference in the aims of the two sectors.

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) Public sector: railways and the electricity board. Private sector: the mall and the private hospital.
(b) These services need very large investment and must be available to everyone at reasonable cost, which private firms may not provide.
(c) The private sector aims at profit; the public sector aims at the welfare of the public (as well as raising revenue).

LONGL114 marks

11. Explain why the tertiary sector has become the most important sector in India, and why this is not entirely a good sign.

Show Answer

Ans:

As incomes rise, people spend a larger share of them on services such as education, health, transport, banking, insurance, tourism and entertainment, so the tertiary sector has become the largest contributor to GDP.
Services are also needed to support agriculture and industry — transport, storage, banking, communication and trade.
However, much of the employment in this sector is in low-productivity, unorganised activities such as domestic work, retail and repairs, and the manufacturing sector has not grown fast enough to absorb the workers leaving agriculture.
So a healthy economy needs balanced growth of all three sectors, especially manufacturing, rather than a jump from farms to low-paid services.

LONGL125 marks

12. Compare the working conditions in the organised and unorganised sectors.

Show Answer

Ans:

Organised sector: enterprises are registered with the government and must follow rules on working hours, overtime, leave and holidays. Workers get regular employment, job security, paid leave, provident fund, medical benefits and other facilities, and are protected by laws such as the Factories Act and the Minimum Wages Act.
Unorganised sector: small, scattered units largely outside government control. Work is irregular and low paid, there is no job security, no paid leave, no provident fund and no medical benefits, and working conditions are often unsafe.
Scale: about 90% of the workers in India are in the unorganised sector — small farmers, landless labourers, artisans, street vendors, construction workers and domestic workers.
What is needed: protection of wages, safety at the workplace, and social security such as health cover and old-age support.

LONGL135 marks

13. Why has the tertiary sector become the most important sector in India? Give five reasons.

Show Answer

Ans:

Basic services: services such as hospitals, educational institutions, postal and telegraph services, police stations, courts, banks and insurance companies are required by every citizen and by the government, and their expansion is essential for development.
Growing demand with rising income: as incomes rise, people demand more services such as eating out, tourism, shopping, private hospitals and private schools.
Support to agriculture and industry: the development of the primary and secondary sectors has led to the development of services such as transport, storage and trade.
New services: information and communication technology has made new services essential, and India has become a major provider of software and business process services.
Employment and GDP: the sector now contributes the largest share of GDP and has created a wide range of jobs, from call centres to consultancy, making it the largest producing sector in India.

Ch 19 · Money and Credit (13)

Save this chapter: Text (.txt) ·

SHORTS12 marks

1. List the main functions of money.

Show Answer

Ans:

Money works as a medium of exchange, a unit of account (a common measure of value) and a store of value; it also allows deferred payments. Because it removes the need for a double coincidence of wants, it makes trade, specialisation and economic growth possible.

SHORTS23 marks

2. Why is money called a medium of exchange?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because it acts as an intermediate step in the exchange of goods and services. In a barter system, exchange requires a double coincidence of wants — each person must want what the other has. Money removes this difficulty: a person can sell his goods for money and use that money to buy whatever he wants, so it mediates between the two parties.

SHORTS33 marks

3. What is collateral and why do banks ask for it?

Show Answer

Ans:

Collateral is an asset that the borrower owns — such as land, a building, a vehicle, livestock or deposits with banks — and uses as a guarantee to a lender until the loan is repaid. Banks ask for it so that if the borrower fails to repay, the bank can sell the asset to recover its money. Because poor households often have no collateral, they are excluded from formal credit.

SHORTS43 marks

4. Why do most poor households in India still depend on informal sources of credit?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because banks and cooperatives demand collateral and documentation which poor households do not possess, banks are not present in all rural areas, and small borrowers are treated as risky. Moneylenders and traders, by contrast, lend without collateral and are available in the village, even though they charge much higher rates of interest.

SHORTS53 marks

5. What is the role of the Reserve Bank of India in supervising banks?

Show Answer

Ans:

The RBI supervises the functioning of formal sources of loans. It ensures that banks maintain a minimum cash balance, that they lend not just to profit-making businesses but also to small cultivators, small-scale industries and small borrowers, and that they submit periodical information to the RBI on how much they are lending, to whom and at what interest rate.

APPLICATIONA63 marks

6. A student writes: “Grameen Bank, founded by Muhammad Yunus in India, lends to poor women.” Find the error and state what Grameen Bank is known for.

Show Answer

Ans:

Grameen Bank is in Bangladesh, not India. Muhammad Yunus founded it.
It is known for giving small loans to poor rural women, mostly through groups and without the collateral that ordinary banks demand.
It showed that poor borrowers, especially women, can be reliable: repayment rates are high.

APPLICATIONA73 marks

7. Kishore needs Rs 50,000 for a tailoring shop. A bank offers the loan at 8% a year, but asks for collateral. A moneylender offers it at 5% a month, with no papers. Compare the interest for one year and say which is better for him.

Show Answer

Ans:

Bank: 8% of Rs 50,000 = Rs 4,000 a year.
Moneylender: 5% a month = 60% a year, so 60% of Rs 50,000 = Rs 30,000 a year.
The bank loan is far cheaper. The moneylender’s loan is easy to get but could trap Kishore in debt. The bank’s collateral demand is the price of cheap credit.

SHORTS83 marks

8. Explain how the Reserve Bank of India supervises banks and why this supervision is needed.

Show Answer

Ans:

The RBI is the apex bank: it issues currency and keeps the cash reserves of the commercial banks.
It decides how much of their deposits banks must keep as reserves and how much they may lend, and it sets the rules for interest rates and priority-sector lending.
It inspects banks, licenses them and can penalise them if they break the rules.
This supervision is needed because banks hold other people's savings; without it they could lend recklessly and collapse, wiping out depositors' money and unsettling the whole economy.

LONGL114 marks

11. Explain the terms of credit and how they decide whether a loan helps or harms the borrower.

Show Answer

Ans:

The terms of credit are the interest rate, the collateral or security, the documents required and the mode and time of repayment.
A loan helps when the borrower can repay it out of the extra income it creates — a farmer irrigating land to grow a second crop, or a weaver buying yarn.
It harms when the activity does not earn enough to repay, when the interest rate is high, or when the borrower loses the collateral and has to borrow again to repay the first loan — a debt trap.
This is why formal credit from banks and cooperatives at lower rates, with supervision and advice, is better than borrowing from an informal moneylender.

LONGL125 marks

12. Explain with an example how credit can either help or harm the borrower.

Show Answer

Ans:

Where credit helps — Salim: Salim, a shoe manufacturer in Delhi, received a large order for 30,000 pairs of shoes to be delivered within a month. He took a loan from a trader, bought the raw material, completed the order on time, made a profit and repaid the loan. Here credit enabled him to expand production and earn more.
Where credit harms — Swapna: Swapna, a small farmer in Andhra Pradesh, borrowed ₹ 2,500 from a moneylender at a high rate of interest to cultivate groundnut. The crop failed because of pest attack, and to repay the first loan she had to take another. Over three years her debt grew, and she finally had to sell a part of her land to repay the loan. This is a debt trap — a situation where credit pushes the borrower into a cycle from which she cannot escape.
Conclusion: whether credit improves a person's situation depends on the risk involved and on whether there is support if something goes wrong. Credit is therefore essential but must be available on fair terms.

LONGL135 marks

13. What are self-help groups and how do they help poor borrowers?

Show Answer

Ans:

A self-help group is a group of about 15–20 members, usually women belonging to one neighbourhood, who meet regularly and save, each contributing anything from ₹ 25 to ₹ 100 or more.
Internal lending: members can take small loans from the group itself, at a rate of interest decided by the group but lower than that charged by moneylenders.
Bank loans: after a year or two of regular savings, the group becomes eligible for a loan from a bank. The loan is sanctioned in the name of the group, and the group is responsible for repayment, so members keep track of each other's borrowing.
Why it helps: it overcomes the problem of lack of collateral, provides credit at a reasonable rate, makes women financially self-reliant, and creates a platform for women to discuss health, nutrition, domestic violence and education. The Grameen Bank of Bangladesh, started by Muhammad Yunus, demonstrated that poor women are reliable borrowers; today more than 9 crore women in India belong to self-help groups.

Ch 20 · Globalisation and the Indian Economy (13)

Save this chapter: Text (.txt) ·

SHORTS12 marks

1. Distinguish between foreign trade and foreign investment.

Show Answer

Ans:

Foreign trade is the buying and selling of goods and services across national borders. Foreign investment is the money spent by a company of one country to buy land, buildings, machines or companies in another country; it brings capital, technology and management along with ownership and control.

SHORTS23 marks

2. What is a multinational corporation? How do MNCs spread their production?

Show Answer

Ans:

A multinational corporation is a corporation that owns or controls production of goods or services in two or more countries other than its home country. MNCs set up factories in regions where they can get cheap labour, resources and other factors of production, and they keep the large profits with themselves. They spread production by setting up new factories, by buying or acquiring a local company and expanding it, or by placing orders with small producers which are then sold under the MNC's brand name.

SHORTS33 marks

3. What is liberalisation and why did the Indian government introduce it in 1991?

Show Answer

Ans:

Liberalisation means removing barriers or restrictions set by the government on trade and on foreign investment. The government introduced it in 1991 because it felt that Indian producers needed to improve the quality of what they produced, and that foreign competition would improve the performance of producers in India, since they would have to improve quality to compete with imports.

SHORTS43 marks

4. What are Special Economic Zones and what facilities do they offer?

Show Answer

Ans:

Special Economic Zones are industrial areas set up by the government with world-class facilities such as electricity, water, roads, transport, storage, recreational and educational facilities. Companies that set up production in an SEZ do not have to pay tax for an initial period of five years, and labour laws are applied flexibly. SEZs are located at places such as Noida, Gandhidham, Falta, Cochin, Chennai and Kandla, with the aim of attracting foreign investment.

SHORTS53 marks

5. What is fair globalisation and how can it be achieved?

Show Answer

Ans:

Fair globalisation would create opportunities for all and ensure that the benefits of globalisation are shared better, rather than concentrating them in the hands of the rich. It can be achieved by the government supporting small producers with credit, technology and training until they are strong enough to compete, by implementing labour laws properly and protecting workers' rights, by negotiating with other developing countries at the WTO for fairer rules, and by supporting people's movements that campaign against unfair trade rules.

APPLICATIONA63 marks

6. A student writes: “Liberalisation means the government has removed all control over the economy.” Correct the statement and name two measures taken after 1991.

Show Answer

Ans:

Liberalisation means removing the barriers and restrictions the government had placed on foreign trade, foreign investment and many industries. The government still regulates areas such as labour, the environment and competition.
Measures after 1991: import taxes and quotas were reduced, and licensing of industries was largely removed.
Foreign companies were also allowed to invest more freely in India.

APPLICATIONA73 marks

7. Explain how foreign trade connects markets, with an example, and how it affects consumers and producers.

Show Answer

Ans:

Foreign trade lets producers sell in markets outside their own country and lets consumers buy goods produced elsewhere, so markets in different countries become linked.
Consumers gain a wider choice and often lower prices; for example, they can choose between an Indian-made and an imported television.
Producers face competition, which can push them to improve quality, but small producers who cannot match cheap imports may lose business.

SHORTS83 marks

8. How does a multinational corporation decide where to set up production? Give examples.

Show Answer

Ans:

MNCs look for cheap and skilled labour, cheap raw materials, low production costs, good infrastructure and a large, growing market.
They are also attracted by favourable government policies such as tax concessions, cheap land and relaxed labour and environmental rules.
For example, garment makers set up units in Bangladesh and Vietnam where labour is cheap, and sell in the markets of the US and Europe.
Ford and Maruti set up plants in India to serve the Indian market, and Tata Motors bought Jaguar Land Rover in 2008 to gain technology and markets abroad.

LONGL114 marks

11. What is meant by fair globalisation? Suggest measures to achieve it.

Show Answer

Ans:

Fair globalisation means that the benefits of globalisation — investment, technology, jobs, cheaper and better goods — are shared more equally, and that workers, small producers and consumers are protected.
Measures: labour laws that protect workers' rights, safety and wages, especially for women in the export sector.
Support for small producers through cheap credit, technology, cooperatives and marketing help.
A stronger voice for developing countries in the WTO, IMF and World Bank, and fairer rules on agricultural subsidies in rich countries.
Pressure on MNCs to pay fair wages and to follow environmental and safety standards, and the right of citizens and governments to regulate them.

LONGL125 marks

12. How has globalisation affected small producers and workers in India?

Show Answer

Ans:

Small producers: many small industries producing items such as batteries, capacitors, plastics, toys, tyres, dairy products, vegetable oil, groundnut oil and matchboxes could not compete with cheap imports and had to shut down, leaving lakhs of workers unemployed. Small farmers have also been affected by cheap agricultural imports from developed countries.
Workers: globalisation has brought pressure on employers to be 'flexible', so workers are increasingly hired on temporary contracts for short periods, with low wages, long hours and no job security or benefits such as provident fund and medical cover. Labour laws are often not implemented.
The result: globalisation has made the rich richer and has increased the insecurity of the poor, so many workers' organisations and people's movements have been demanding fairer rules.
But: in some sectors, such as information technology and services, globalisation has created new employment opportunities, and consumers have benefited from lower prices and wider choice.

LONGL135 marks

13. What is the WTO and what criticism is made of it?

Show Answer

Ans:

The World Trade Organization was started in 1995 at the insistence of developed countries. It sets the rules for international trade, and all decisions are taken by consensus among its member countries, of which there are about 164.
Aim: to liberalise international trade, and to ensure that countries remove trade barriers such as taxes on imports, since it is believed that this will allow countries to specialise and grow.
Criticism: the rules of the WTO are often biased in favour of developed countries. Developed countries have kept some trade barriers, particularly in agriculture, and have supported their own farmers with subsidies, while asking developing countries to remove their barriers. Developing countries have had to comply with WTO rules, but the benefits have not been shared equally. Hence the demand for fair globalisation.
