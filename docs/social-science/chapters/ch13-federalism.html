Chapter 13 · Federalism

🎯 Marks lens

📖 Deep concepts

1. What is federalism? A system of government in which power is divided between a central authority and various constituent units of the country. Usually a federation has two levels of government: one for the entire country, responsible for subjects of common interest, and governments at the level of provinces or states, looking after much of the day-to-day governing. In a unitary system there is only one level of government, or the sub-units are subordinate to the central government. Both levels have their own legislature, executive and judiciary, and each is separately answerable to the people.
2. Two routes to a federation. Coming together — independent states come together on their own to form a bigger unit, increasing their security while retaining some sovereignty; examples are the USA, Switzerland and Australia. Holding together — a large country decides to share its power between the constituent states and the national government, with the centre tending to be more powerful; examples are India, Spain and Belgium. India is a holding-together federation, but the constitution does not use the word federation — the Indian Union is based on the principles of federalism.
3. The constitutional scheme. The constitution distributes legislative powers between the Union and the states through three lists. Union List (about 100 subjects) — subjects of national importance such as defence, foreign affairs, banking, communications and currency; only Parliament can legislate. State List (about 61 subjects) — subjects of state and local importance such as police, trade, commerce, agriculture and irrigation; only the state legislature can legislate. Concurrent List (about 52 subjects) — subjects of common interest such as education, forest, trade unions, marriage, adoption and succession; both can legislate, but if the two laws conflict, the central law prevails. Residuary subjects — those that came up after the constitution was made, such as computer software — fall to the Union. Union Territories such as Chandigarh, Lakshadweep and Delhi do not have the powers of a state; they are run directly by the Union government.
4. How federalism works in practice. The constitution laid down the principle, but its success depends on the nature of politics. Three features stand out: linguistic states — boundaries were redrawn so that people who spoke the same language lived in the same state, which strengthened unity and made administration easier, though it also created disputes; language policy — Hindi was declared the official language but only about 40% of Indians speak it, and 21 other languages were recognised as scheduled languages, with English continuing as an additional official language; and centre-state relations — in the early years the same party ruled both the centre and most states, so state governments did not exercise their rights fully; since the 1990s, coalition governments at the centre have led to a new culture of power sharing and respect for the autonomy of state governments, and the Supreme Court has made it difficult for the centre to dismiss state governments on political grounds.
5. Decentralisation in India. Decentralisation is the transfer of power from the central and state governments to local government. It rests on the idea that there are a large number of problems best settled at the local level, that people have better knowledge of problems in their localities, and that direct participation in decision-making is a way to train citizens in democratic habits. Panchayati Raj existed in a limited way earlier, but a major step was taken in 1992 with the constitutional amendment that made the third tier of democracy more powerful and effective: it is now constitutionally mandatory to hold regular elections to local government bodies; seats are reserved for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Other Backward Classes; at least one-third of all positions are reserved for women; an independent State Election Commission has been created to conduct these elections; and state governments are required to share some powers and revenue with local bodies.
6. Rural and urban local bodies. Gram panchayat — the decision-making body for a village, consisting of elected members and the sarpanch who is the president of the panchayat and the political head. Panchayat samiti (block or mandal) — the group of all gram panchayats in a block, headed by a president or chairperson. Zilla parishad — the district-level body, whose chairperson is the political head of the district. Municipalities govern urban areas, headed by a municipal chairperson; in big cities a municipal corporation is headed by a mayor.
7. Federalism and the special case of states. Some states enjoy special provisions. Article 370 gave special status to Jammu and Kashmir until it was revoked in 2019; the north-eastern states such as Nagaland, Mizoram and Manipur enjoy considerable autonomy; and Article 371 contains special provisions for several states. India's federalism is thus flexible, and the NITI Aayog and the GST Council are examples of the centre and states working together — the GST Council is chaired by the Union Finance Minister and includes state finance ministers.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Union List = national, State List = local, Concurrent = both. If a subject matters to the whole country (defence, currency) it is Union; if it is local (police, agriculture) it is State.
  • Conflict in the Concurrent List = the central law prevails. Always add this line — it is a marked step.
  • 1992 amendment = regular elections, reservation (SC/ST/OBC, one-third women), State Election Commission, sharing of powers and revenue. Four points, four marks.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Saying the Indian constitution uses the word 'federation'. It does not; the Indian Union is based on federal principles.
  • Putting education in the State List. Education was moved to the Concurrent List in 1976.
  • Forgetting residuary subjects go to the Union — computer software is the standard example.

❓ Exam Q&A

3 marks Distinguish between 'coming together' and 'holding together' federations with two examples each.

In a coming-together federation, independent states come together on their own to form a bigger unit, pooling sovereignty while retaining their identity — the USA, Switzerland and Australia. In a holding-together federation, a large country divides power between the constituent states and the national government, and the centre tends to be more powerful — India, Spain and Belgium.

5 marks Describe how legislative powers are distributed between the Union and the states in India.

Union List: about 100 subjects of national importance such as defence, foreign affairs, banking, communications and currency. Only Parliament can make laws on them, because uniform policy is needed for the whole country.
State List: about 61 subjects of state and local importance such as police, trade, commerce, agriculture and irrigation. Ordinarily only the state legislature can make laws on these.
Concurrent List: about 52 subjects of common interest to both, such as education, forest, trade unions, marriage, adoption and succession. Both Parliament and the state legislatures can make laws, but if the two conflict, the central law prevails.
Residuary subjects: subjects that came up after the constitution was made, such as computer software, fall under the jurisdiction of the Union government.

5 marks What major step was taken in 1992 towards decentralisation in India?

Before 1992, elections to local bodies were not held regularly, and local bodies did not have adequate powers or resources of their own. The constitutional amendment of 1992 made the third tier of democracy far more powerful and effective.
Regular elections to local government bodies are now constitutionally mandatory.
Seats are reserved in these bodies for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Other Backward Classes.
At least one-third of all positions are reserved for women.
An independent institution called the State Election Commission has been created in each state to conduct panchayat and municipal elections.
State governments are required to share some powers and revenue with local government bodies, so that local bodies have the means to function.

3 marks Explain any three features of federalism practised in India.

Linguistic states — the boundaries of old states were changed and new states created so that people who speak the same language live in the same state; this made administration easier and strengthened national unity. Language policy — Hindi was declared the official language, but because only about 40% of Indians speak it, the constitution recognised 21 other languages as scheduled languages and allowed English to continue as an additional official language. Centre-state relations — the rise of coalition governments at the centre since the 1990s has created a new culture of power sharing and respect for the autonomy of state governments, and the judiciary has made it harder to dismiss state governments for political reasons.

3 marks Why is decentralisation considered a good idea in a democracy?

Because there are a large number of problems and issues that are best settled at the local level, such as local planning, sanitation and water supply; people have better knowledge of the problems in their own localities and can take better decisions about how to spend money; and direct participation in local decision-making is a way to train citizens in the habits of democracy and to build a democratic culture.
