Chapter 3 · The Making of a Global World

🎯 Marks lens

3 marks at most, from the pre-modern exchanges, the Silk Routes and the Columbian food trades.
Later subtopics — the corn laws, the gold standard, the inter-war economy and the Bretton Woods system — are not assessed in the current syllabus. Check the current session's PDF before revising them.

📖 Deep concepts

1. Globalisation is not new. Human societies have always been in contact, but globalisation as we know it — the integration of markets, movement of goods, capital, people and ideas across the world — began to take shape only in the last few centuries. Even in the pre-modern world, trade, migration and the spread of religion and disease linked distant regions.
2. The Silk Routes. These were vibrant pre-modern trade and cultural routes linking Asia with Europe and northern Africa. Chinese silk, Indian cotton textiles and spices travelled west, while precious metals — especially silver from Peru and later from Europe — flowed east. The routes also carried Buddhism from India to East Asia and, later, Christian missionaries and Muslim preachers. Chinese pottery and textiles moved along them, and the routes spread disease as well — germs carried by travellers.
3. Food travels. Long before the modern era, food crops travelled across continents. Noodles from China became spaghetti in Europe; traders and travellers introduced new crops. The ready availability of food in Europe reduced starvation, but poverty and misery remained widespread, and until the nineteenth century much of the world's population was still affected by famine.
4. The Columbian exchange. The discovery of the sea route to Asia and the crossing of the Atlantic to America transformed the world. America's vast lands and abundant crops transformed trade and lives everywhere. Potatoes, soya, groundnuts, maize, tomatoes, chillies and sweet potatoes were unknown in Europe and Asia before Columbus's journeys; they came from the Americas and changed diets across the world. The poor of Europe ate better, and in Ireland the potato became the staple of the poorest — which is why the potato blight of the mid-1840s caused a famine in which about a million people died and about twice that number emigrated.
5. Precious metals and disease. Silver from mines in Peru and Mexico financed Europe's wealth and its trade with Asia. Equally decisive was the transmission of disease: the Spanish conquerors of America carried smallpox, to which the original inhabitants had no immunity because of their long isolation. Smallpox killed and decimated communities, paving the way for conquest — a far more effective weapon than guns, since guns could be bought and fought against but disease could not.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Silk Routes = goods + religion + disease. All three travel together; mention all three for full marks.
  • Columbian exchange = American crops to the Old World. Potato, maize, tomato, chilli, groundnut, soya, sweet potato — memorise six.
  • Smallpox, not guns, conquered America — immunity is the keyword.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Revising the later subtopics (corn laws, gold standard, Bretton Woods) and spending revision time on material that is not assessed.
  • Saying the potato originated in Ireland. It came from the Americas; Ireland only depended on it.
  • Writing 'silver from Bolivia' where the textbook says Peru and Mexico.

SHORTS12 marks

1. What was the Corn Laws controversy in Britain?

Show Answer

Ans:

The Corn Laws restricted the import of cheap corn into Britain, keeping bread prices high to protect landowners. Workers and industrialists demanded their repeal; after repeal in 1846, food flowed in from the colonies and eastern Europe, bread became cheaper, but many European peasants were forced to migrate to the Americas.

SHORTS23 marks

2. What were the Silk Routes and what did they carry besides goods?

Show Answer

Ans:

The Silk Routes were a network of pre-modern trade and cultural routes linking Asia with Europe and northern Africa. Besides goods such as Chinese silk, Indian cotton textiles and spices going west and silver going east, they carried religions — Buddhism, and later Christianity and Islam — and also carried diseases, since germs travelled with the traders and pilgrims.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Explain how the Columbian exchange changed diets in the Old World, giving examples.

Show Answer

Ans:

Before the discovery of America, foods such as potatoes, soya, groundnuts, maize, tomatoes, chillies and sweet potatoes were unknown in Europe and Asia. These American crops reached the Old World after Columbus's voyages and changed what people ate. The poor of Europe, who had earlier eaten mostly bread and porridge, now had a more varied and abundant diet, and in Ireland the potato became the staple of the poorest sections.

SHORTS43 marks

4. Why did the Spanish conquest of America succeed so easily?

Show Answer

Ans:

Mainly because of disease. The Spanish conquerors carried smallpox germs, and the original inhabitants of America had no immunity to them because of their long isolation from the rest of the world. Smallpox spread and killed huge numbers, decimating whole communities, so conquest became easy. Guns could be bought or fought against, but disease could not.

SHORTS53 marks

5. Describe the role of silver from Peru and Mexico in the global economy of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

Show Answer

Ans:

The discovery of rich silver mines in Peru and Mexico made silver available in huge quantities. It was shipped to Europe and from there used to finance Europe's wealth and its expanding trade with Asia. Silver thus became the link that connected the Americas, Europe and Asia in a single flow of money and goods.

SHORTS83 marks

8. Explain what indentured labour was and its effects in the nineteenth century.

Show Answer

Ans:

After slavery was abolished, plantations in the Caribbean, Fiji, Mauritius, Sri Lanka and Natal needed cheap labour.
Millions of workers were recruited from India, China and Africa on contracts (girmit) for a fixed number of years, often after being misled about the terms.
They were carried in crowded ships and worked in harsh conditions, but they also carried Indian languages, festivals and food abroad.
Indentured migration from India was finally abolished in 1917 after the nationalist movement raised the issue.

LONGL114 marks

11. Explain how the First World War and the Great Depression changed the world economy.

Show Answer

Ans:

The First World War turned Europe from a creditor into a debtor and made the United States the world's main lender.
The Wall Street Crash of 1929 and the Depression of the 1930s collapsed world trade, output and employment; agricultural prices and farm incomes fell sharply, hitting colonised countries hardest.
Countries raised tariffs and formed imperial preference blocs, and migration and lending fell.
The crisis destroyed confidence in unregulated markets and led to state intervention — for example Roosevelt's New Deal — and to a demand for decolonisation.
