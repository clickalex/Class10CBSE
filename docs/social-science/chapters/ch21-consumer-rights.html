Chapter 21 · Consumer Rights

🎯 Marks lens

Project work in the internal assessment — the usual project is a survey of a local shop, a study of ISI and Agmark labels, or a consumer-court case write-up.
As a theory question it appears as a 3-marker on the consumer's rights or on the redressal machinery under COPRA.

📖 Deep concepts

1. The consumer in the marketplace. A consumer is a person who buys goods and services for personal use. In the marketplace, consumers are often exploited: shopkeepers may give less weight than they claim, sell adulterated or defective goods, add hidden charges, or refuse to give a bill so that there is no proof of purchase. Producers and traders are far fewer in number, better organised and more powerful than consumers, who are scattered and buy in small quantities. False advertising and the sale of goods that do not meet safety standards are other common problems.
2. Why consumers must be aware. Awareness is the first step. A consumer should check the MRP, the expiry date, the date of manufacture, the batch number, the net quantity, and the ISI, Agmark or Hallmark certification marks where relevant. The consumer should insist on a cash memo (bill), because it is proof of purchase and is needed to file a complaint. Consumers should also know their rights and the process of redressal.
3. The consumer movement in India. It arose out of dissatisfaction with the many unfair practices of sellers, and because there was no legal system to protect consumers from exploitation in the marketplace. Until the 1960s, consumer organisations were mostly writing articles and holding exhibitions. The movement gained force after the 1980s, when issues such as food adulteration, hoarding, black marketing and shortage of goods were raised. In 1986 the Indian Parliament enacted the Consumer Protection Act (COPRA), which recognises consumers' rights and sets up the machinery for redressal. A major step was the right to seek redressal against unfair trade practices and exploitation. India also observes 24 December as National Consumers' Day, because the Act was enacted on that day in 1986.
4. Consumer rights. (a) Right to be informed — the right to know about the quality, quantity, potency, purity, standard and price of goods, so that the consumer is protected against unfair trade practices. This is why the government made it mandatory to print the MRP, expiry date and ingredients. (b) Right to choose — the right to be assured of access to a variety of goods and services at competitive prices. (c) Right to seek redressal — the right to seek remedy against unfair trade practices or exploitation, and to be compensated. (d) Right to represent — the right to be heard and to represent oneself in the consumer courts. (e) Right to safety — protection against goods that are hazardous to life and health. (f) Right to consumer education.
5. The redressal machinery under COPRA. A three-tier quasi-judicial machinery was set up: the District Commission (formerly District Forum) for claims up to a specified value, the State Commission for higher claims, and the National Commission at the top. A consumer aggrieved by the order of a District or State Commission can appeal to the higher level. The process: the consumer files a complaint with a copy of the bill and other proof; if the court finds the case genuine, it may direct the trader to remove the defect, replace the goods, refund the price, pay compensation for loss or injury, or discontinue the unfair practice.
6. Consumer organisations. Consumer protection councils, consumer groups, women's organisations and residents' welfare associations play a major role in spreading awareness, helping consumers file cases, and pressuring traders and the government. Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) certifies goods with the ISI mark; Agmark certifies agricultural produce; the Hallmark certifies the purity of gold and silver. Legal Metrology ensures correct weights and measures. RTI (Right to Information) Act 2005 also empowers consumers to ask government departments about their functioning.
7. Duties of a consumer. Check the expiry and manufacturing dates and the certification marks; ask for a bill; do not buy adulterated or defective goods; complain when cheated, since silence encourages the trader; form consumer groups; and buy only standardised goods where certification is available.

🧮 Formulas to memorise

  • Six rights: Safety, Information, Choice, Redress, Representation, Consumer education
  • COPRA 1986 → three-tier commissions: District (up to ₹1 crore), State, National
  • Always keep the cash memo — a complaint without proof of purchase does not survive
  • Standard marks: ISI (goods), Agmark (agriculture), Hallmark (gold), FSSAI (food), ISO (systems)

🛠️ Method, step by step

  1. State the right that has been violated — safety, information, choice, redress, representation, or consumer education.
  2. Describe the exploitation with a concrete case: underweight packets, adulteration, a faulty appliance, or a misleading advertisement.
  3. Name the remedy and the forum — the Consumer Protection Act, the district/state/national commissions, and the right to file a complaint with a cash memo as evidence.
  4. Close with COPRA and consumer awareness — the movement exists because an individual buyer cannot fight a company alone.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • COPRA 1986, enacted on 24 December = National Consumers' Day. The date and the Act go together.
  • Six rights: informed, choose, redressal, represent, safety, education. 'I Choose Redressal, Representation, Safety, Education'.
  • Three-tier machinery: District, State, National Commission. Always in that order.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Calling the district level a 'District Forum'. Under the present Act it is the District Commission.
  • Saying a consumer can go straight to the National Commission. Appeals go district → state → national.
  • Confusing ISI (industrial goods, Bureau of Indian Standards) with Agmark (agricultural produce).

SHORTS12 marks

1. What does the right to be informed mean and how is it protected in India?

Show Answer

Ans:

It means a consumer must be given all the information needed to make a sensible choice: the ingredients, quantity, date of manufacture, expiry date, batch number and maximum retail price of a product. It is protected by rules requiring this information on labels and packaging, by standardisation marks such as ISI, Agmark and Hallmark, and indirectly by the Right to Information Act, 2005.

SHORTS23 marks

2. Explain any three rights of a consumer.

Show Answer

Ans:

Right to be informed: the right to know about the quality, quantity, purity, standard and price of goods so as to be protected against unfair trade practices; this is why the MRP, expiry date and ingredients must be printed on packets. Right to choose: the right to be assured of access to a variety of goods and services at competitive prices. Right to seek redressal: the right to seek remedy against unfair trade practices or exploitation, and to be compensated for loss or injury.

SHORTS33 marks

3. What is COPRA and what machinery did it set up?

Show Answer

Ans:

COPRA is the Consumer Protection Act, enacted by the Indian Parliament in 1986, which recognises the rights of consumers and provides machinery for redressal. It set up a three-tier quasi-judicial machinery — the District Commission, the State Commission and the National Commission — before which a consumer can file a complaint along with proof of purchase.

SHORTS43 marks

4. What is the significance of 24 December in the context of consumer rights in India?

Show Answer

Ans:

It is observed as National Consumers' Day in India because the Consumer Protection Act, 1986 (COPRA) was enacted by Parliament on 24 December 1986. The day is used to spread awareness about consumer rights and the machinery available for redressal.

SHORTS53 marks

5. What are the duties of a consumer?

Show Answer

Ans:

A consumer should check the expiry and manufacturing dates and the certification marks such as ISI, Agmark and Hallmark; ask for a bill as proof of purchase; avoid adulterated or defective goods and report them; complain to the trader or to the consumer commission when cheated instead of remaining silent; join or form consumer groups; and prefer standardised goods where certification is available.

LONGL114 marks

11. Describe the three-tier redressal machinery under the Consumer Protection Act and how a consumer uses it.

Show Answer

Ans:

The district commission (district forum) hears complaints where the value of the goods or services and compensation claimed is up to ₹20 lakh.
The state commission hears claims between ₹20 lakh and ₹1 crore, and appeals against the district commission.
The National Commission in New Delhi hears claims above ₹1 crore and appeals against the state commissions.
A consumer files a written complaint with the cash memo, guarantee card or any other proof of purchase, and consumer organisations and voluntary agencies help fight the case; the forum can order a refund, replacement, removal of the defect, compensation and payment of costs.

LONGL125 marks

12. Why is the consumer often exploited in the marketplace and how can this be overcome?

Show Answer

Ans:

Why: traders and producers are few in number, well organised and powerful, while consumers are scattered and buy in small quantities, so they cannot bargain effectively. Sellers give less weight, sell adulterated or defective goods, add hidden charges, and refuse to give bills so that there is no proof of purchase. False advertising and unsafe goods add to the problem.
How it can be overcome: (1) the consumer must be aware — check the MRP, expiry and manufacture dates, batch number, net quantity and the ISI, Agmark or Hallmark marks; (2) always insist on a cash memo, since it is the proof of purchase needed to file a complaint; (3) know and use the consumer's rights, including the right to seek redressal before the District, State or National Commission; (4) form and support consumer groups and organisations, which have been instrumental in spreading awareness and pressuring traders and the government; (5) use the RTI Act to obtain information from government departments; and (6) the government must enforce standards and penalise unfair trade practices.
