Chapter 20 · Globalisation and the Indian Economy

🎯 Marks lens

3–5 marks. MNCs and how they control production, and liberalisation with its effects on workers and small producers, are the set questions.
The terms WTO, MNC, liberalisation and special economic zones must be defined precisely — definitions carry marks here.

📖 Deep concepts

1. What is globalisation? The integration of the economies of various countries of the world through the free movement of goods, services, capital and technology, and the free movement of labour. It is the result of a long historical process, but it has accelerated in the last three decades. Its two main features are the interconnection of production and markets across countries, and the integration of markets so that the price of a product tends to become the same everywhere.
2. Production across countries — MNCs. A multinational corporation (MNC) is a corporation that owns or controls production of goods or services in two or more countries other than its home country. It sets up offices and factories for production in regions where it can get cheap labour, resources and other factors, and keeps the large profits with itself. MNCs control production in three ways: setting up new factories in the host country (which creates employment); buying or acquiring a local company and expanding it, which is the quickest way and is possible because foreign investment rules have been eased — Ford Motors acquired a stake in Mahindra; and placing orders with small producers — garments, footwear and sports items — which are then sold under the MNC's brand name. The third route lets the MNC control small producers without owning them: it sets the price, quality, delivery and labour conditions.
3. Foreign trade and integration of markets. Foreign trade creates an opportunity for producers to reach beyond the domestic market, so they can sell in markets abroad and buyers can choose between products produced in different countries. Integration happens when prices of similar products in two markets tend to become the same, producers in the two countries become close competitors, and even when protected by import barriers, foreign producers enter and change the domestic market. Example: in India, after the 1990s, the number of car models rose sharply, and buyers got a much wider choice because of foreign trade and foreign investment.
4. Liberalisation in India (1991). Liberalisation means removing barriers or restrictions set by the government on what can be imported or exported, and on foreign investment. Until 1991, India protected domestic producers because many industries were just coming up in the 1950s and 1960s and would not have been able to compete with foreign imports. In 1991 the government decided that Indian producers had to improve their quality and compete internationally, and that foreign competition would improve the performance of producers in India. So import barriers were reduced, and decisions about what to import or export, and how much, were left to individual producers and buyers. Foreign investment rules were also liberalised, and organisations such as the WTO pushed for this. Flexibility in labour laws was also introduced, allowing companies to employ workers on a contract basis for short periods, which reduced job security.
5. The World Trade Organization (WTO). Started in 1995 at the insistence of developed countries, the WTO sets the rules for international trade and ensures that member countries follow them. Its aim is to liberalise international trade and to remove barriers. It has about 164 member countries, and all decisions are taken by consensus among members. The criticism is that the rules are often biased in favour of developed countries, and that developing countries have had to remove trade barriers while developed ones continue to protect their own agriculture — for example the USA supporting its farmers while pressing India to remove agricultural subsidies.
6. Impact of globalisation on India. Positive: MNCs have increased investment in India — Ford, Samsung, Hyundai, Coca-Cola, Pepsi, Nokia and others have set up production; Indian companies such as Tata Motors, Infosys, Ranbaxy, Sundaram Fasteners have themselves become multinationals by acquiring foreign companies; consumers have greater choice at lower prices and better quality; top Indian companies have benefited from competition and now compete globally; IT and services have grown rapidly, with business process outsourcing employing lakhs; and India receives a large part of the world's remittances from Indians working abroad. Negative: globalisation has hit small producers and workers hardest. Small industries making batteries, capacitors, plastics, toys, tyres, dairy products, vegetable oil, groundnut oil and matchboxes could not compete with cheap imports and many units were shut down, leaving workers unemployed. Workers are increasingly employed on temporary contracts at low wages with no job security, and small farmers have been affected by cheap agricultural imports.
7. Special Economic Zones and fair globalisation. Special Economic Zones (SEZs) are industrial areas set up by the government with world-class facilities — electricity, water, roads, transport, storage, recreational and educational facilities — where companies that set up production get tax exemption for five years. SEZs are at Noida, Gandhidham, Falta, Cochin, Chennai, Visakhapatnam, Santacruz, Kandla, Surat, Nellore and elsewhere. Fair globalisation would create opportunities for all and ensure that the benefits of globalisation are shared better. For this the government must support small producers to improve their performance until they become strong enough to compete, protect workers' rights and implement labour laws properly, negotiate for fairer rules at the WTO, and align with other developing countries. People's movements and workers' organisations also play a role in ensuring that globalisation is fair.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • MNC = production in two or more countries besides the home country. That exact wording is a marked step.
  • Three ways MNCs control production: build, buy, place orders. 'Build, buy, brand.'
  • 1991 = liberalisation; 1995 = WTO. Two dates, no ambiguity.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Saying liberalisation removes all government control. It removes barriers on trade and foreign investment, not all regulation.
  • Describing the WTO as an Indian institution or giving its start date as 1991. It began in 1995.
  • Attributing the growth of SEZs to private initiative alone. They are set up by the government with world-class facilities.

❓ Exam Q&A

3 marks What is a multinational corporation? How do MNCs spread their production?

A multinational corporation is a corporation that owns or controls production of goods or services in two or more countries other than its home country. MNCs set up factories in regions where they can get cheap labour, resources and other factors of production, and they keep the large profits with themselves. They spread production by setting up new factories, by buying or acquiring a local company and expanding it, or by placing orders with small producers which are then sold under the MNC's brand name.

3 marks What is liberalisation and why did the Indian government introduce it in 1991?

Liberalisation means removing barriers or restrictions set by the government on trade and on foreign investment. The government introduced it in 1991 because it felt that Indian producers needed to improve the quality of what they produced, and that foreign competition would improve the performance of producers in India, since they would have to improve quality to compete with imports.

5 marks How has globalisation affected small producers and workers in India?

Small producers: many small industries producing items such as batteries, capacitors, plastics, toys, tyres, dairy products, vegetable oil, groundnut oil and matchboxes could not compete with cheap imports and had to shut down, leaving lakhs of workers unemployed. Small farmers have also been affected by cheap agricultural imports from developed countries.
Workers: globalisation has brought pressure on employers to be 'flexible', so workers are increasingly hired on temporary contracts for short periods, with low wages, long hours and no job security or benefits such as provident fund and medical cover. Labour laws are often not implemented.
The result: globalisation has made the rich richer and has increased the insecurity of the poor, so many workers' organisations and people's movements have been demanding fairer rules.
But: in some sectors, such as information technology and services, globalisation has created new employment opportunities, and consumers have benefited from lower prices and wider choice.

5 marks What is the WTO and what criticism is made of it?

The World Trade Organization was started in 1995 at the insistence of developed countries. It sets the rules for international trade, and all decisions are taken by consensus among its member countries, of which there are about 164.
Aim: to liberalise international trade, and to ensure that countries remove trade barriers such as taxes on imports, since it is believed that this will allow countries to specialise and grow.
Criticism: the rules of the WTO are often biased in favour of developed countries. Developed countries have kept some trade barriers, particularly in agriculture, and have supported their own farmers with subsidies, while asking developing countries to remove their barriers. Developing countries have had to comply with WTO rules, but the benefits have not been shared equally. Hence the demand for fair globalisation.

3 marks What are Special Economic Zones and what facilities do they offer?

Special Economic Zones are industrial areas set up by the government with world-class facilities such as electricity, water, roads, transport, storage, recreational and educational facilities. Companies that set up production in an SEZ do not have to pay tax for an initial period of five years, and labour laws are applied flexibly. SEZs are located at places such as Noida, Gandhidham, Falta, Cochin, Chennai and Kandla, with the aim of attracting foreign investment.

3 marks What is fair globalisation and how can it be achieved?

Fair globalisation would create opportunities for all and ensure that the benefits of globalisation are shared better, rather than concentrating them in the hands of the rich. It can be achieved by the government supporting small producers with credit, technology and training until they are strong enough to compete, by implementing labour laws properly and protecting workers' rights, by negotiating with other developing countries at the WTO for fairer rules, and by supporting people's movements that campaign against unfair trade rules.

✍️ Hands-on task

Look at five products in your home and find where each was made and which company owns the brand. Mark the ones made by an MNC and note the country of origin. Then write three sentences on how that brand reached your town — through a factory, an acquisition, or an order placed with a local producer.
