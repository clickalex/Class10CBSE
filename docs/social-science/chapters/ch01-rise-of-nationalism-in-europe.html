Chapter 1 · The Rise of Nationalism in Europe

Chapter 1 of 21. One long story with four set answers: the idea of the nation, the 1848 revolutions, the three unifications, and the visual symbols.

🎯 Marks lens

5 marks from this chapter, usually a 3–5 mark answer on one of the unifications or on the liberal nationalism of the middle class.
The exam rewards dates and names: 1848 Frankfurt Parliament, 1871 German unification, Cavour and Garibaldi, Otto von Bismarck. An answer without a year scores poorly in History.

📖 Deep concepts

1. Frédéric Sorrieu's print (1848). Four prints showing a vision of a world of democratic and social republics. Men and women of Europe and America march past the Statue of Liberty, offering homage to it. On the ground lie the shattered remains of the symbols of absolutist institutions — the age of nationalism had begun.
2. The French Revolution and the idea of the nation (1789). Power passed from the monarchy to the citizens; sovereignty came to reside in the people. The revolutionaries introduced la patrie and le citoyen, a new tricolour, a National Assembly, a common language, and the abolition of internal customs. Napoleon carried the ideas abroad through the Civil Code of 1804 (the Napoleonic Code), which removed privileges based on birth, established equality before the law and secured the right to property — but his rule also brought increased taxation, censorship and forced conscription.
3. The making of nationalism in Europe. Before the nineteenth century there were no nation-states: Germany, Italy and Switzerland were divided into kingdoms, duchies and cantons, and the Habsburg Empire was a patchwork of regions. A landed aristocracy dominated, while a new middle class of industrialists and professionals emerged and pushed for the abolition of privilege, a representative government and freedom of the press — the core of liberal nationalism, which meant government by consent and the end of autocracy and clerical privileges. Zollverein, the customs union formed in 1834 at Prussia's initiative, abolished tariff barriers and reduced currencies from over thirty to two.
4. The age of revolutions, 1830–1848. The July Revolution in France (1830) sparked an uprising in Brussels, which led to Belgium separating from the United Kingdom of the Netherlands. Greek independence was recognised by the Treaty of Constantinople in 1832. 1848 — the Revolution of the Liberals: the Frankfurt Parliament was convened in the Church of St Paul, drafted a constitution for a German nation headed by a monarchy subject to a parliament, but King Friedrich Wilhelm IV of Prussia rejected the crown and the assembly lost its social base and disintegrated. Women were admitted only as observers, in the visitors' gallery.
5. The making of Germany and Italy. Germany: after the failure of 1848, Prussia took the lead; Otto von Bismarck, with the army and bureaucracy, fought three wars over seven years against Austria, Denmark and France, ending in Prussian victory and the proclamation of the German Empire at Versailles in January 1871, with Kaiser Wilhelm I of Prussia as emperor. Italy: divided into seven states; Giuseppe Mazzini founded Young Italy for a unitary republic; Count Cavour of Sardinia-Piedmont led the movement diplomatically, allied with France, and defeated Austria in 1859; Giuseppe Garibaldi led the Red Shirts into the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies in 1860; Victor Emmanuel II was proclaimed king of united Italy in 1861 — but Cavour was neither a revolutionary nor a democrat. Britain: a long-drawn-out process, no revolution; the Act of Union 1707 between England and Scotland created the United Kingdom of Great Britain; the Act of Union 1801 incorporated Ireland after a failed revolt led by Wolfe Tone; the British nation grew around England, its symbols being the Union Jack, the national anthem and the English language.
6. Visualising the nation and nationalism in Europe. The nation was portrayed as a female figure — Marianne in France (red cap, tricolour, cockade) and Germania in Germany (wearing a crown of oak leaves, which stand for heroism). These allegories made the abstract idea of the nation concrete. By the last quarter of the nineteenth century, nationalism had narrowed: the Balkans, under the Ottoman Empire and containing many ethnic groups (Roumans, Bulgarians, Greeks, Serbs, Croats), became the scene of intense nationalist struggle and jealous rivalry among the great powers, and were the region that led Europe into the First World War.
7. What the new order could not hold. The conservative settlement of 1815 restored monarchs and redrew borders, but it could not restore a way of life. Industrialisation had already created a middle class that read newspapers and wanted a say, peasants carried grievances about tithes and forced labour, and educated young men moved between cities where they met others who spoke the same language and read the same books. Nationalism worked because it gave all three groups one story in which they had a stake — and that is why the revolutions of 1848, though mostly defeated, were rehearsals rather than failures.

🛠️ Method, step by step

  1. Give the year and the treaty before anything else — 1815, the Treaty of Vienna, and the conservative order it tried to freeze.
  2. Separate the three strands: cultural (romanticism, language, folklore), political (the revolutionaries of 1848), and economic (Zollverein, tariff unions).
  3. For Italy and Germany, name the man, the method and the year — Cavour's diplomacy, Garibaldi's Red Shirts, Bismarck's blood-and-iron, 1871.
  4. Close with what changed — the nation-state replaced the dynastic empire, and that idea travelled outwards into the colonies.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Three unifications, three years: Germany 1871, Italy 1861, Britain 1707/1801 (no revolution — the 'odd one out').
  • Three Italian heroes in order of what they did: Mazzini gave the idea (Young Italy), Cavour gave the diplomacy, Garibaldi gave the army.
  • Bismarck's plan = three wars, seven years — Denmark, Austria, France.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Confusing the Frankfurt Parliament (1848) with the proclamation of the German Empire (Versailles, 1871).
  • Writing that Cavour was a revolutionary. He was the Chief Minister of Sardinia-Piedmont and used diplomacy, not mass revolt.
  • Forgetting that the Frankfurt Parliament was held in the Church of St Paul and that its constitution was rejected by Friedrich Wilhelm IV.
  • Describing the Treaty of Constantinople (1832) as recognising Belgian independence. It recognised Greek independence.

SHORTS12 marks

1. Who were the Jacobins and what did they do during the French Revolution?

Show Answer

Ans:

The Jacobins were members of a political club led by Maximilian Robespierre. They overthrew the monarchy, declared France a republic, and gave the nation its symbols — the tricolour flag, the red cap (liberty cap) and the new national anthem, the Marseillaise — through which people could identify with the nation.

SHORTS23 marks

2. Interpret the significance of the allegory of Marianne and Germania.

Show Answer

Ans:

Both were female allegories used to give the abstract idea of the nation a concrete visual form. Marianne, with her red cap, tricolour and cockade, represented the ideals of liberty and the French Republic, and statues of her were installed in public squares to remind citizens of national unity. Germania, wearing a crown of oak leaves, symbolised heroism and the German nation. Allegories made it easy for people to identify with the nation.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Briefly trace the process of German unification.

Show Answer

Ans:

Middle-class Germans tried to unite the German Confederation through a parliament elected in 1848, but the Frankfurt assembly failed when Friedrich Wilhelm IV rejected the offered crown. Prussia then took over the leadership, and its Chief Minister Otto von Bismarck used the army and the bureaucracy to fight three wars over seven years against Austria, Denmark and France. Prussian victories ended in the proclamation of the German Empire at Versailles in January 1871, with Kaiser Wilhelm I as emperor.

SHORTS43 marks

4. What steps did the French revolutionaries take to create a sense of collective identity among the French people?

Show Answer

Ans:

They introduced the ideas of la patrie (the fatherland) and le citoyen (the citizen), emphasising a united community with equal rights under a constitution. A new tricolour replaced the royal standard, the Estates General was renamed the National Assembly and elected by active citizens, a uniform system of weights and measures was adopted, internal customs duties were abolished, and French as spoken in Paris became the common language. Oaths of loyalty to the constitution and commemoration of martyrs were also used.

SHORTS53 marks

5. How did culture play an important role in creating the idea of the nation in Europe?

Show Answer

Ans:

Romantic artists and poets criticised the glorification of reason and science and turned to emotion, intuition and mysticism, using folk songs, folk poetry and folk dances to reach the people. Language too was used as a weapon of national resistance — in Poland, for example, the use of Polish in schools and church gatherings became a symbol of struggle against Russian dominance. Operas and music carried nationalist feelings to a largely illiterate audience.

SHORTS83 marks

8. Describe the condition of the Balkans in the nineteenth century and why the region caused tension in Europe.

Show Answer

Ans:

The Balkans was a region of great ethnic and religious diversity that had been part of the Ottoman Empire.
As Ottoman power weakened, the Slavic peoples demanded independence, and the Balkan states fought each other over territory.
Big powers such as Russia, Austria-Hungary, Britain and Germany each wanted influence over the region. These rivalries made the Balkans the powder keg of Europe and helped trigger the First World War in 1914.

LONGL114 marks

11. Explain how the French Revolution spread the idea of nationalism across Europe.

Show Answer

Ans:

The revolution transferred sovereignty from the monarch to the nation: the people, not the king, were declared the source of power.
It gave the French people common symbols — the tricolour, the national anthem and a new calendar — and introduced a uniform administrative system with equal laws and one currency.
Napoleon's armies carried these ideas into the territories they conquered; although his rule was resented, the idea that people sharing a common language and history should form one nation took root.
This idea shaped the unification movements of Italy and Germany and the struggles of the Balkan peoples in the nineteenth century.

LONGL125 marks

12. Write a note on the role of Giuseppe Mazzini, Count Cavour and Giuseppe Garibaldi in the unification of Italy.

Show Answer

Ans:

Giuseppe Mazzini — a member of the secret society Carbonari, he founded Young Italy in Marseilles to work for a unitary Italian republic. He provided the ideological inspiration and the programme.
Count Cavour — Chief Minister of Sardinia-Piedmont, he led the unification movement through diplomacy rather than revolution, and with the help of France and the army defeated Austria in 1859.
Giuseppe Garibaldi — he led the armed volunteers known as the Red Shirts, marched into the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies in 1860, won the support of the local peasants and drove out the Spanish rulers.
In 1861 Victor Emmanuel II was proclaimed king of united Italy, with Cavour as his Chief Minister. Much of the peasant population, however, knew nothing of the ideals of liberal nationalism.
