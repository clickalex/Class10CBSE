🎯 Marks lens

3–4 marks. The geographical conditions of rice/wheat/tea/coffee and the three cropping seasons are asked almost every year.
Crops are also a map pointing item — learn the producing states with their positions.

📖 Deep concepts

1. Agriculture in India. About two-thirds of India's population is engaged in agriculture, which provides most of the food consumed, produces raw material for industries, and earns foreign exchange through exports of tea, coffee and spices. It is the backbone of the economy.
2. Cropping seasons. Rabi — sown in winter from October to December, harvested in summer from April to June (wheat, barley, peas, gram, mustard). Kharif — sown with the onset of the monsoon in different parts of the country from June to July, harvested in September–October (paddy, maize, jowar, bajra, tur, moong, urad, cotton, jute, groundnut, soybean). Zaid — a short season in the summer between rabi and kharif (watermelon, muskmelon, cucumber, vegetables, fodder).
3. Types of farming. Primitive subsistence — slash-and-burn agriculture, known as jhumming in the north-eastern states, pamlou in Manipur, dipia in Bastar and milpa in Central America; low productivity, no fertilisers. Intensive subsistence — labour-intensive, high doses of biochemical inputs and irrigation, practised where population pressure on land is high. Commercial — single crop over a large area (plantation: tea in Assam, coffee in Karnataka, rubber in Kerala, banana in the south), using capital-intensive inputs and migrant labour.

4. Technological and institutional reforms. After independence India tried land reforms (consolidation of holdings, abolition of the zamindari system), then Bhoodan and Gramdan movements — the Bloodless Revolution — followed by the Green Revolution (HYV seeds, irrigation, fertilisers, especially in Punjab, Haryana and western UP) and the White Revolution (Operation Flood, milk). Later measures: Kissan Credit Card, Personal Accident Insurance Scheme, minimum support price and remunerative procurement prices, subsidies on power and fertilisers, and Grameen banks and cooperative societies to reduce dependence on moneylenders.
5. Food security and globalisation. Buffer stock is created by the government by procuring wheat and rice through the Food Corporation of India and distributing them through ration shops at prices lower than the market price. India's farmers face competition from subsidised agriculture in developed countries, so many farmers, especially smallholders, have withdrawn from farming. Organic farming — farming without factory-made chemicals such as fertilisers and pesticides — is increasingly popular because it does not damage the environment.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Kharif = monsoon (June–July to Sept–Oct); Rabi = winter (Oct–Dec to April–June); Zaid = summer. 'Kharif rain, Rabi grain, Zaid melon.'
  • Rice needs 100 cm, wheat needs 50–75 cm, jute needs the most (150–250 cm).
  • Tea = Assam/Darjeeling/Nilgiris; coffee = Karnataka/Kerala/TN. Tea needs more water than coffee.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Mixing up kharif and rabi crops. Wheat, gram, mustard and barley are rabi; rice, cotton, jute and maize are kharif.
  • Forgetting that plantation agriculture is commercial, not subsistence.
  • Writing that tea needs a waterlogged plain. Tea needs well-drained soil on slopes — waterlogging damages the roots.

❓ Exam Q&A

3 marks Describe the geographical conditions required for the growth of rice.

Rice is a kharif crop that requires a temperature above 25 °C and high humidity, with annual rainfall above 100 cm. It grows best in the plains of north and north-eastern India, the coastal areas and the deltaic regions. In areas of less rainfall, such as Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh, it is grown with the help of irrigation and canal networks.

3 marks Distinguish between kharif and rabi crops with two examples of each.

Kharif crops are sown with the onset of the monsoon in June–July and harvested in September–October — for example paddy, maize, cotton and jute. Rabi crops are sown in winter from October to December and harvested in summer from April to June — for example wheat, barley, peas, gram and mustard.

5 marks Describe the impact of globalisation on Indian agriculture.

Indian agriculture has faced the impact of globalisation since colonial times — indigo was grown for export and forced upon farmers, leading to the Champaran revolt.
Today, Indian farmers face competition from highly subsidised agriculture in developed countries, so the prices of agricultural produce are unstable and many small farmers find cultivation unviable.
Liberalisation has removed restrictions on imports, so cheaper imports of edible oils and grains have reduced demand for Indian produce.
Positive side: improved technology, HYV and genetically modified seeds, better irrigation and access to information have raised productivity in some regions, and organic farming is opening new markets.
Result: a large number of small and marginal farmers have withdrawn from farming, and agriculture is no longer a rewarding occupation for them.

3 marks What is plantation agriculture? Give one example.

It is a type of commercial farming in which a single crop is grown on a large area, using capital-intensive inputs and migrant labour, and the produce is used as raw material in industries. Tea in Assam and North Bengal, coffee in Karnataka, rubber in Kerala and banana in southern India are examples; the production of tea in Assam was developed by the British with capital, technology and indentured labour.

3 marks What is buffer stock and why is it created?

Buffer stock is a stock of foodgrains, mainly wheat and rice, procured by the government through the Food Corporation of India. It is created to distribute foodgrains in deficit areas and among the poorer strata of society at a price lower than the market price, and to meet the shortage during a bad harvest or a calamity.
