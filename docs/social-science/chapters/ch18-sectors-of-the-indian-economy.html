Chapter 18 · Sectors of the Indian Economy

Chapter 18 of 20. Six classifications of economic activity. Learn one example for each and every question on this chapter is answerable.

🎯 Marks lens

3–5 marks. The difference between organised and unorganised sectors, and disguised unemployment with rural labourers, are the set questions.
Table-based questions on the share of GDP and of employment by sector appear often — learn that the tertiary sector produces the largest share of GDP but the primary sector still employs the most people.

📖 Deep concepts

1. Sectors of economic activity. Primary — activities that use natural resources directly: agriculture, dairy, fishing, forestry, mining. Secondary — activities in which natural products are changed into other forms through manufacturing: turning sugarcane into sugar, cotton into cloth, or iron ore into steel. Tertiary — activities that support the primary and secondary sectors instead of producing goods: transport, banking, communication, trade, education, health, insurance, and information technology. The tertiary sector is also called the service sector.
2. Why the tertiary sector is growing. As income rises, people demand more services such as eating out, tourism, shopping and private hospitals. Basic services such as hospitals, schools, postal and communication services are required by the government and by every citizen. The development of agriculture and industry has led to the development of services such as transport and trade. New services based on information and communication technology have become essential. As a result, in India the tertiary sector has emerged as the largest producing sector, replacing the primary sector, and it now contributes the largest share to GDP.
3. Employment in the sectors. Although the tertiary sector produces the largest share of GDP, the primary sector still employs the largest number of people in India — more than half the workforce. This means that the growth of the service sector has not generated enough employment for everyone.
4. Disguised unemployment and underemployment. Underemployment means people are working less than what they are capable of and willing to do. In agriculture, a family of five may all work on a small plot although three would be enough — if the extra members are withdrawn, production does not fall. This is disguised unemployment, also called hidden unemployment. In urban areas, casual workers such as painters, plumbers and repair persons work only when they get work. Underemployment is also seen where highly educated people work as clerks or labourers. Remedy: in rural areas, more irrigation facilities, credit and cheap inputs would raise agricultural output and create work; construction such as dams, canals and roads would create employment; and in urban areas, more industries and service enterprises could absorb the surplus labour. The Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act 2005 (MGNREGA) guarantees 100 days of wage employment in a year to every rural household whose adult members volunteer to do unskilled manual work, and gives a legally guaranteed right to work.
5. Organised and unorganised sectors. The organised sector has enterprises registered with the government, which follow rules on working conditions, hours, overtime, leave and holidays; workers get job security, paid leave, provident fund, medical benefits and other facilities protected by law, and are covered by the Factories Act and other labour laws. The unorganised sector has small, scattered units largely outside government control; jobs are low-paid, irregular and insecure, with no paid leave, no provident fund and no job security. Most workers in the unorganised sector are small farmers, landless labourers, artisans, street vendors and construction workers. About 90% of workers in India are in the unorganised sector. They need protection on three issues: wages, safety at work, and social security such as health and old-age support.
6. Public and private sectors. The public sector is owned by the government and works with the objective of public welfare rather than profit — railways, posts, NTPC, ONGC, SAIL. It provides essential services at reasonable rates, cares for the welfare of the poor, and supports areas where private enterprise will not invest. The private sector is owned by individuals or companies and works for profit — TISCO, Reliance, Infosys.
7. Gross Domestic Product (GDP). The total value of all final goods and services produced within a country during a particular year is called GDP. Only final goods are counted, to avoid double counting — the value of the wheat is included in the value of the flour, and that in the value of the bread. The production of each sector is estimated by the concerned ministry and department, and the Central Statistics Office collects the data. GDP is measured for the whole country and is divided into the contribution of the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors.

8. National Rural Employment Guarantee and the shift in the economy. The rise of the tertiary sector and the fall of agriculture's share in GDP is a feature of economic development — in developed countries the service sector dominates. But employment has not shifted at the same rate, so India has a large workforce still dependent on agriculture with low incomes, which is why MGNREGA and rural development matter.

🧮 Formulas to memorise

  • GDP = value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a year
  • Primary + Secondary + Tertiary = total production; count a good only once, at its final stage
  • Organised sector: registered, regular wages, social security; unorganised: none of these guaranteed
  • Sectoral share today: tertiary largest by output, primary still largest by employment

🛠️ Method, step by step

  1. Classify the activity — primary, secondary or tertiary — by what it does to the product, not by how modern it looks.
  2. Give the sectoral share and note the trend: the tertiary sector is now the largest, while the primary still employs the most people.
  3. Distinguish organised and unorganised work, with the figures for job security, wages and social protection.
  4. Close with one policy — MGNREGA, rural credit, or the push for skill development — and what it is meant to fix.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Primary = extract, Secondary = make, Tertiary = support. One word each.
  • The paradox to remember: tertiary produces the most GDP, primary employs the most people.
  • Disguised unemployment = extra people, same output. The test question is 'if some are withdrawn, does production fall?'

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Counting intermediate goods in GDP. Only final goods are counted, to avoid double counting.
  • Saying the tertiary sector employs the largest number of people in India. It produces the largest share of GDP, but the primary sector employs the most.
  • Forgetting that MGNREGA guarantees 100 days, not 200.

SHORTS12 marks

1. Distinguish between the organised and the unorganised sectors.

Show Answer

Ans:

In the organised sector the work is registered, employees have regular hours, paid holidays, provident fund, gratuity, medical benefits and job security, and labour laws apply. In the unorganised sector the work is small-scale and unregistered, wages are low and irregular, working conditions are poor, and there is no job security, no social security and no protection of labour laws.

SHORTS23 marks

2. Distinguish between the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors with one example of each.

Show Answer

Ans:

The primary sector includes activities that use natural resources directly, such as agriculture, dairy, fishing and mining. The secondary sector includes activities in which natural products are changed into other forms through manufacturing, such as turning sugarcane into sugar or cotton into cloth. The tertiary sector includes activities that support the primary and secondary sectors without producing goods, such as transport, banking, trade, education and health.

SHORTS33 marks

3. What is disguised unemployment? Give an example from rural India.

Show Answer

Ans:

Disguised or hidden unemployment is a situation in which more people are employed in a job than are actually required, so that withdrawing some of them does not reduce production. In rural India, a family of five may all work on a small plot of land although three would be enough; if the extra two members leave, output does not fall. They appear employed but are in fact surplus.

SHORTS43 marks

4. What is GDP and why are only final goods counted in it?

Show Answer

Ans:

Gross Domestic Product is the total value of all final goods and services produced within a country during a particular year. Only final goods are counted because the value of intermediate goods is already included in the value of the final product — the value of wheat is included in the flour, and of the flour in the bread. Counting both would amount to double counting.

SHORTS53 marks

5. What is MGNREGA 2005 and how does it help to reduce unemployment?

Show Answer

Ans:

The Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act 2005 guarantees 100 days of wage employment in a year to every rural household whose adult members volunteer to do unskilled manual work. If the government fails to provide work within fifteen days, the applicant is entitled to an unemployment allowance. It creates a legal right to work, raises rural incomes, and builds rural assets such as ponds, roads and irrigation channels.

SHORTS83 marks

8. Explain underemployment with an example and suggest two measures to reduce it.

Show Answer

Ans:

Underemployment is the condition in which people work less than they could, or more people are engaged in a job than are needed.
Example: if five members of a family cultivate a plot that needs only two, the other three add nothing to output; they are disguisedly unemployed, especially during the lean season.
Measures: providing non-farm employment in villages through MGNREGA and rural industries, and improving irrigation so that two or three crops can be grown in a year.
Other measures: skill training so that workers can move to manufacturing and services, better roads and markets, and cheap institutional credit.

LONGL114 marks

11. Explain why the tertiary sector has become the most important sector in India, and why this is not entirely a good sign.

Show Answer

Ans:

As incomes rise, people spend a larger share of them on services such as education, health, transport, banking, insurance, tourism and entertainment, so the tertiary sector has become the largest contributor to GDP.
Services are also needed to support agriculture and industry — transport, storage, banking, communication and trade.
However, much of the employment in this sector is in low-productivity, unorganised activities such as domestic work, retail and repairs, and the manufacturing sector has not grown fast enough to absorb the workers leaving agriculture.
So a healthy economy needs balanced growth of all three sectors, especially manufacturing, rather than a jump from farms to low-paid services.

LONGL125 marks

12. Compare the working conditions in the organised and unorganised sectors.

Show Answer

Ans:

Organised sector: enterprises are registered with the government and must follow rules on working hours, overtime, leave and holidays. Workers get regular employment, job security, paid leave, provident fund, medical benefits and other facilities, and are protected by laws such as the Factories Act and the Minimum Wages Act.
Unorganised sector: small, scattered units largely outside government control. Work is irregular and low paid, there is no job security, no paid leave, no provident fund and no medical benefits, and working conditions are often unsafe.
Scale: about 90% of the workers in India are in the unorganised sector — small farmers, landless labourers, artisans, street vendors, construction workers and domestic workers.
What is needed: protection of wages, safety at the workplace, and social security such as health cover and old-age support.

LONGL135 marks

13. Why has the tertiary sector become the most important sector in India? Give five reasons.

Show Answer

Ans:

Basic services: services such as hospitals, educational institutions, postal and telegraph services, police stations, courts, banks and insurance companies are required by every citizen and by the government, and their expansion is essential for development.
Growing demand with rising income: as incomes rise, people demand more services such as eating out, tourism, shopping, private hospitals and private schools.
Support to agriculture and industry: the development of the primary and secondary sectors has led to the development of services such as transport, storage and trade.
New services: information and communication technology has made new services essential, and India has become a major provider of software and business process services.
Employment and GDP: the sector now contributes the largest share of GDP and has created a wide range of jobs, from call centres to consultancy, making it the largest producing sector in India.

✍️ Hands-on task

Take five people you know who work — a farmer, a shopkeeper, a driver, a teacher, a factory worker — and classify each by sector, and as organised or unorganised. Then note which of the five has job security and a provident fund. That single list covers both the sector and the security question.
