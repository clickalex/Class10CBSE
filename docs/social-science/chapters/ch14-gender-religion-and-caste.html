Chapter 14 · Gender, Religion and Caste

Chapter 14 of 20. Three sections in one chapter: women in politics, religion and communalism, and caste. Each one carries a set 3- or 5-mark answer.

🎯 Marks lens

Among the surest marks in Political Science. The definition of communalism and the two faces of the caste–politics relationship are asked almost every year.
Learn the three sections as three separate answers, each with its own list of points — the paper usually asks only one of them.

📖 Deep concepts

1. Gender and the sexual division of labour. Girls and boys are brought up differently: women do the housework and look after children, men work outside the home for wages. This division of work is based on social expectation, not on biology, and it is where gender inequality begins. Because work outside the home is paid and visible while work at home is unpaid and unseen, women's contribution is undervalued.
2. The public and the private. Politics is organised in the public sphere — parties, elected bodies, government, law — while the private sphere of home and family was thought to be outside politics altogether. Women's role was confined to the private sphere, so politics came to be seen as a man's world. The divide is patriarchal: a system that values men more and gives them power over women.
3. What women face. Literacy: about 65 per cent of women are literate against about 82 per cent of men (Census 2011). Wages: women are paid less than men for the same work, which is why the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976 requires equal wages for equal work. Preference for sons: sex-selective abortion has lowered the child sex ratio in many states. Violence and burden: harassment, domestic violence and the unequal load of housework. In almost every field, far fewer women reach the top than their share in the population would suggest.
4. Women in political office. In the Lok Sabha, women have never been more than about 14 per cent of the members, and state assemblies are similar — well below the world average. Local bodies show that reservation works: one-third of the seats in panchayats and municipalities are reserved for women (several states have raised this to half), and today more than a million women hold elected positions in rural and urban local bodies. In 2023 Parliament passed the 106th Constitutional Amendment, reserving one-third of the seats in the Lok Sabha and the state assemblies for women; it takes effect after the next census and delimitation. Studies of panchayats find that women raise issues of drinking water, fuel, fodder and health, and that their presence changes decisions rather than merely adding numbers.
5. Religion, politics and communalism. Religion enters politics when the beliefs of one religion dominate political life, when one community seeks to dominate another, and when parties are formed on religious lines. Communalism is the belief that people who follow the same religion have common secular interests, and that people of different religions have separate and opposed interests. It has four faces: everyday religious prejudice and stereotypes; the desire to dominate other religious communities; the political dominance of the majority religion; and the demand for separate political units or parties for each religion. Communal politics turns dangerous when religion is treated as the only identity, when elections are fought on religious appeals, and when it spills over into violence.
6. India is a secular state. The Constitution gives no special status to any religion, allows every person the freedom to profess, practise and propagate a religion of their choice, prohibits discrimination on the ground of religion, and permits the state to intervene in religious matters to ensure equality — abolishing untouchability, for instance, and making laws for social reform. The Indian state is therefore neither hostile to religion nor walled off from it: it keeps a principled distance, and that is what makes equality possible in a multi-religious society.
7. Caste and politics. Caste is a hereditary system of graded inequality fixed by birth, and for centuries it decided access to education, work and power. Caste in politics: parties choose candidates with an eye to the dominant caste of a constituency, voters' choices are shaped by caste, and governments are formed keeping caste arithmetic in mind. Politics in caste: politics has also sharpened caste identity — castes have organised into associations and asserted themselves to claim a share of power, which is how OBCs and Dalits entered politics in large numbers.
8. But caste does not decide everything. No parliamentary constituency has a majority of a single caste, so every party has to win votes across castes; no party wins the votes of every voter of a caste; no caste votes as one block; and voters weigh the performance of the government and the appeal of the leader along with caste. Caste inequality has not ended, but caste alone cannot win an election.

🛠️ Method, step by step

  1. Name the division the question is about — gender, religion or caste — and say how it enters politics.
  2. Give the Indian evidence: the literacy and sex-ratio figures for women, the share of women in legislatures, caste and voting behaviour, or communal riots.
  3. Distinguish the three views on religion in politics — Gandhi's, that religion guides ethics; the secularist's, that the state must stay away; and the communalist's, that one religion should dominate.
  4. Close with the conclusion the chapter reaches: each division becomes dangerous when it is the only identity in play, and manageable when other cross-cutting differences also count.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Three sections, three lists: gender (work, wages, seats), religion (communalism and secularism), caste (caste in politics, politics in caste).
  • Communalism in one line: the same religion means common secular interests; different religions means opposed interests.
  • Numbers to quote: Equal Remuneration Act 1976, one-third of local body seats for women, one-third for the Lok Sabha and assemblies under the 106th Amendment (2023).
  • 'Caste in politics' — caste shapes elections. 'Politics in caste' — politics sharpens caste identity. The first word tells you what is doing the shaping.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Calling the Indian state anti-religious. Secularism here is a principled distance: the state can and does intervene in religion to end inequality.
  • Treating communalism as simply being religious. Communalism begins only when religion becomes the basis of a nation and of rival political interests.
  • Writing that caste is finished because caste discrimination is banned. Discrimination is banned and declining, but caste identity is still politically active.
  • Reading the chapter as women's issues only. The marks are for the sexual division of labour, patriarchy and representation.

SHORTS12 marks

1. What is the feminist movement and what did it demand?

Show Answer

Ans:

Feminism is a movement and a way of thinking that questions the unequal and oppressive treatment of women simply because they are women. It demanded equal rights in the family, at work and in politics, an end to violence and discrimination, and a change in the way society defines the roles of men and women.

SHORTS23 marks

2. What is the sexual division of labour and how does it lead to gender inequality?

Show Answer

Ans:

The sexual division of labour is the arrangement in which women do the housework, cook and look after children while men work outside the home for wages. It rests on social expectation, not on biology. Because paid work outside is treated as real work while the unpaid work at home is not counted at all, women's contribution is undervalued. That is where gender inequality begins: it keeps women out of the public sphere and out of better-paid, higher-status work.

SHORTS33 marks

3. What does it mean to say that India is a secular state?

Show Answer

Ans:

The Constitution gives no special status to any religion — there is no official religion.
Every person has the freedom to profess, practise and propagate any religion of their choice.
Discrimination on the ground of religion is prohibited, so no community can be treated as inferior.
The state may intervene in religious matters to ensure equality — for example, by abolishing untouchability and making laws for social reform. India is therefore not anti-religious: the state keeps a principled distance from religion.

SHORTS43 marks

4. Name any three steps taken to increase women's representation in India.

Show Answer

Ans:

One-third of the seats in panchayats and municipalities are reserved for women, and several states have raised this to half, so more than a million women now hold elected positions in local bodies. In 2023 Parliament passed the 106th Constitutional Amendment reserving one-third of the seats in the Lok Sabha and the state assemblies for women, to take effect after the next census and delimitation. The Equal Remuneration Act, 1976 — equal wages for equal work — supports women's participation outside the home as well.

SHORTS73 marks

7. Distinguish between communalism and secularism, with examples from India.

Show Answer

Ans:

Communalism is the belief that people of one religion share common political and economic interests that are opposed to those of other religions; it turns religious identity into political rivalry.
Examples: the partition riots of 1947 and the 2002 Gujarat riots, in which members of one community were targeted by another.
Secularism means that the state has no official religion, treats all religions equally and does not discriminate on grounds of religion.
In India this is expressed in the ban on religious instruction in government-funded schools, the right of every person to profess and practise any religion, and the state's duty to protect all communities.

LONGL104 marks

10. Explain how caste continues to influence Indian politics, and whether this is desirable.

Show Answer

Ans:

Caste shapes political behaviour: parties choose candidates with an eye on caste arithmetic, voters often prefer candidates of their own caste, and caste associations influence policy and mobilise support.
Caste can also be a means of asserting the dignity of disadvantaged groups — for example, the Dalit Panthers and the Bahujan Samaj Party brought Dalit concerns into the political mainstream.
Used positively, caste-based mobilisation gives a voice to the excluded and increases their share in power, which is not undemocratic.
It becomes a problem when it turns into casteism — the oppression or exclusion of other castes and violence against them — and when it replaces all discussion of development and governance.

LONGL115 marks

11. What is communalism? Explain any three forms in which it appears in politics.

Show Answer

Ans:

Communalism is the belief that people who follow the same religion have common secular interests, and that people of different religions have separate and opposed interests. It is the most common form in which religion is used in politics.
Everyday prejudice: religious prejudices, stereotypes about other communities and the belief that one's own religion is superior.
Domination: the desire to establish the dominance of one's own religious community over others and to use state power for it.
Majoritarian dominance: the belief that the majority community should rule, which turns religion into the basis of political power.
Separate political units or parties: demanding a separate state, or forming parties on religious lines.
Communal politics turns violent when religion is treated as the only identity and elections are fought on religious appeals.

LONGL125 marks

12. Explain the relationship between caste and politics in India.

Show Answer

Ans:

Caste in politics: parties take caste into account while choosing candidates, since the dominant caste of a constituency influences the result; voters' preferences are shaped by caste; and governments are formed keeping caste arithmetic in mind.
Politics in caste: politics has sharpened caste identities — castes have formed associations and asserted themselves to claim a share of political power, which is how OBCs and Dalits entered politics in large numbers.
But caste is not everything: no parliamentary constituency has a majority of one caste, so every party must appeal across castes; no caste votes as a single block; and voters also judge the government's performance and the leader. Caste inequality persists, but caste alone does not win an election.
