📖 Deep concepts

1. Minerals. A mineral is a homogeneous, naturally occurring substance with a definite internal structure, found in various forms from the hardest diamond to the softest talc. They are classified as metallic (iron, manganese, copper, bauxite) and non-metallic (limestone, mica, gypsum), and as ferrous and non-ferrous. Minerals occur in veins and lodes in igneous and metamorphic rocks, as beds or layers in sedimentary rocks, as residual masses from the decomposition of surface rocks (bauxite), as placer deposits in alluvial sands (gold, silver, tin, platinum), and in ocean waters (common salt, magnesium, bromine).
2. India's mineral belts. Iron ore — the basic mineral and backbone of industrial development: hematite (the finest, with up to 70% iron) and magnetite (60–70% iron, excellent magnetic properties for the electrical industry). Belts: Odisha-Jharkhand (Badampahar, Mayurbhanj), Durg-Bastar-Chandrapur (Bailadila in Chhattisgarh, exported to Japan and South Korea), Bellary-Chitradurga-Chikkamagaluru-Tumakuru in Karnataka (Kudremukh), and Maharashtra-Goa (Ratnagiri). Manganese — used in steel, ferro-manganese alloy, bleaching powder, insecticides and paints; mainly in Odisha, Jharkhand, MP, Maharashtra, Goa and Karnataka.
3. Copper, bauxite and mica. Copper is malleable, ductile and a good conductor; used in electrical cables, electronics and chemical industries; Balaghat in MP, Singhbhum in Jharkhand and Jhunjhunu-Alwar in Rajasthan. Bauxite, from which aluminium is obtained, is used where aluminium replaces steel and copper because it is light, resistant, malleable and a good conductor; found in the Amarkantak plateau, Maikal hills and the Bilaspur-Katni plateau in MP — Odisha is the largest producer (Panchpatmali). Mica — a mineral made of plates or leaves, used in the electrical and electronic industries because of its excellent dielectric strength and resistance to high voltage; Koderma-Gaya-Hazaribagh belt in Jharkhand is the largest producer, followed by Ajmer in Rajasthan and Nellore in Andhra Pradesh.
4. Conventional sources of energy. Coal — the most abundantly available fossil fuel and the major source of energy in India; four types: anthracite (best, hard, high carbon), bituminous (most popular commercially), lignite (brown, soft, high moisture — Neyveli in Tamil Nadu), and peat. Major fields: Damodar valley (Jharia, Raniganj, Bokaro), Godavari, Mahanadi, Son and Wardha valleys. Petroleum — the next major energy source, and a raw material for the chemical industry; about 63% of India's production comes from the Bombay High, 18% from Gujarat (Ankleshwar) and 16% from Assam (Digboi, Naharkatiya, Moran-Hugrijan). Natural gas — an environmentally friendly fuel because of low CO2 emissions; the KG basin has large reserves. Electricity is generated mainly by hydroelectric plants (Bhakra Nangal, Damodar Valley Corporation, Kopili) and thermal plants burning coal, petroleum and natural gas.
5. Non-conventional (renewable) sources. Solar energy — becoming rapidly popular in rural India through photovoltaic technology; the Bhuj solar plant and the large Bhadla plant in Rajasthan; solar plants at Neyveli; solar cookers and solar water heaters. Wind power — India is a superpower in wind energy; the largest wind farm cluster is in Tamil Nadu (from Nagercoil to Madurai), with important plants in Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Gujarat, Kerala, Maharashtra and Lakshadweep. Biogas — produced from shrubs, farm waste, animal and human waste; a higher thermal efficiency than charcoal and better than kerosene; gobar gas plants in rural areas. Tidal, geothermal and atomic energy — the Gulf of Khambhat and Gulf of Kuchchh in Gujarat and the Sundarbans in West Bengal have tidal potential; Manikaran in Himachal Pradesh and Puga Valley in Ladakh have geothermal potential; atomic energy from uranium and thorium — uranium in Jharkhand and the Aravalli ranges of Rajasthan, and monazite sands in Kerala containing thorium.
6. Conservation. Minerals are exhaustible and non-renewable, take millions of years to form, and the rate of replenishment is infinitely small compared with the rate of consumption. We must use them in a planned and sustainable manner, improve technology to use low-grade ores at low cost, and recycle metals and scrap.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Coal ranks: Anthracite > Bituminous > Lignite > Peat. 'A Better Life for Peasants'. Anthracite is best but rare; bituminous is what India mostly uses.
  • Non-conventional = solar, wind, biogas, tidal, geothermal, atomic. Anything that does not come from burning a fossil fuel.
  • Largest producer list: bauxite — Odisha; mica — Koderma (Jharkhand); wind — Tamil Nadu; petroleum — Bombay High.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Calling lignite the best quality coal. It is low-grade, brown and has high moisture — anthracite is the best.
  • Saying petroleum is a renewable resource because it keeps being formed. It is non-renewable on a human time scale.
  • Placing the largest wind farm cluster in Gujarat. It is in Tamil Nadu, from Nagercoil to Madurai.

SHORTS13 marks

1. Distinguish between conventional and non-conventional sources of energy with two examples each.

Show Answer

Ans:

Conventional sources have been in use for a long time, are largely exhaustible, cause pollution and are costly to obtain — for example coal and petroleum. Non-conventional sources are recently developed, are renewable and eco-friendly, and have lower running costs — for example solar and wind energy.

SHORTS23 marks

2. Why is there a pressing need to use non-conventional sources of energy in India?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because the growing consumption of energy has resulted in a rising dependence on coal and petroleum, whose prices are rising and whose reserves are limited; importing petroleum puts a heavy burden on foreign exchange. Conventional sources also cause environmental degradation and global warming. India has abundant potential for solar, wind, tidal, geothermal and biogas energy, which is renewable, eco-friendly and can be generated locally, so developing it improves energy security and reduces pollution.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Describe the distribution of iron ore in India.

Show Answer

Ans:

Iron ore is the basic mineral and the backbone of industrial development. Hematite, the finest quality with up to 70% iron, and magnetite, with 60–70% iron and excellent magnetic properties, are found in four belts: the Odisha-Jharkhand belt (Badampahar and Mayurbhanj in Odisha, Kiriburu in Jharkhand); the Durg-Bastar-Chandrapur belt in Chhattisgarh and Maharashtra, including the famous Bailadila mines whose ore is exported to Japan and South Korea; the Bellary-Chitradurga-Chikkamagaluru-Tumakuru belt in Karnataka with the large Kudremukh mines; and the Maharashtra-Goa belt, whose ore is exported through the Marmagao port.

LONGL45 marks

4. Explain any five non-conventional sources of energy available in India.

Show Answer

Ans:

Solar energy: converted using photovoltaic technology; rapidly becoming popular in rural India, used for lighting, cooking and water heating — the largest solar plants are in Rajasthan (Bhadla) and Tamil Nadu (Neyveli).
Wind energy: India is a superpower in wind power, with the largest cluster of wind farms in Tamil Nadu from Nagercoil to Madurai.
Biogas: produced from farm waste, animal and human waste in gobar gas plants; it has higher thermal efficiency than charcoal and is used for lighting and cooking in villages.
Tidal energy: generated by using floodgate dams across inlets — potential in the Gulf of Khambhat, the Gulf of Kuchchh and the Sundarbans.
Geothermal energy: heat from the earth's interior used to generate power — the Manikaran hot springs in Himachal Pradesh and the Puga Valley in Ladakh are promising; six plants have been set up there.
Atomic energy: produced from uranium and thorium; uranium is found in Jharkhand and the Aravalli range, and thorium in the monazite sands of Kerala.
