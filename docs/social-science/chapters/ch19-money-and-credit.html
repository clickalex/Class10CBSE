🎯 Marks lens

3–4 marks. The role of banks as mediators, the difference between formal and informal credit, and self-help groups are the set questions.
Case-based questions describe a farmer borrowing from a moneylender and ask you to identify the problem and the better alternative.

📖 Deep concepts

1. Money as a medium of exchange. Before money, exchange took place through the barter system, which required a double coincidence of wants — each party had to want what the other offered. Money solves this problem because it acts as an intermediate step in exchange, and is therefore called a medium of exchange. Modern money is currency, which is accepted as a medium of exchange not because it is useful in itself or made of precious metal, but because it is authorised by the government. The Reserve Bank of India issues currency notes on behalf of the Central Government; no other individual or organisation is allowed to issue currency. The RBI also supervises the functioning of banks, ensures that banks maintain a minimum cash balance and lend to small borrowers as well as big ones, and monitors that banks submit information on lending.
2. Loan activities of banks. Banks keep a small proportion of deposits as cash (about 15% in India) to pay depositors who may come to withdraw on any day, and use the major portion to extend loans. In return, banks pay interest on deposits and charge a higher interest on loans; the difference between what is charged from borrowers and what is paid to depositors is the bank's main source of income. Banks thus act as mediators between those who have surplus money and those who need it.

3. Two different credit situations. Salim, a shoe manufacturer in Delhi, took a loan from a trader, completed the order and repaid the loan with profit — here credit played a vital and positive role. Swapna, a small farmer in Andhra Pradesh, borrowed from a moneylender at a high rate to grow groundnut; the crop failed, and to repay the earlier loan she had to take another, and eventually had to sell part of her land — here credit pushed her into a debt trap. So credit can either help or harm, depending on the situation, the risk and the support available.
4. Formal sector credit in India. Banks and cooperatives are the main sources of formal credit. Cooperative societies pool the resources of their members, accept deposits and lend to members at a low rate of interest; they are supervised by the RBI. But formal credit reaches only a small part of rural India: in urban areas about 85% of poor households borrow from informal sources, and in rural areas about 70% do. Only about half of the total poor households in India borrow mostly from informal sources — moneylenders, traders, employers, relatives and friends. Why the imbalance: banks and cooperatives require collateral and documentation, which poor households do not have; banks are not present everywhere in rural India; and small borrowers are considered risky. The result is that informal lenders charge much higher interest, and the high cost of borrowing means that a large part of the borrower's earnings goes to repayment, so the debt grows.
5. Self-help groups. A self-help group (SHG) is a group of 15–20 members, usually women from one neighbourhood, who meet and save regularly, each contributing ₹ 25 to ₹ 100 or more per saving session. Members can take small loans from the group itself at a low rate of interest. After a year or two of regular savings, the group becomes eligible for a loan from a bank, which is sanctioned in the name of the group, with the group itself responsible for repayment. By 2017–18, more than 9 crore women were members of SHGs. Grameen Bank of Bangladesh, started by Muhammad Yunus in the 1970s, showed that poor women can repay loans reliably; it grew to over 9 million members. SHGs help borrowers overcome the problem of lack of collateral, provide a platform to discuss social issues such as health, nutrition and domestic violence, and make women financially self-reliant.
6. Why credit should be available to all. Cheap and affordable credit is essential for a country's development, because farmers need loans for seeds, fertilisers and equipment, and small entrepreneurs need loans to start or expand enterprises. At present most of the credit is available only to the rich, so the government should encourage formal sources of lending, expand banks and cooperatives into rural areas, and ensure that small borrowers get loans at a reasonable rate. This is the role of the RBI's supervision and of SHGs.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Credit helps Salim and traps Swapna. Name both in any credit question and you have the whole argument.
  • Formal = banks and cooperatives (RBI supervised, low rates, collateral needed). Informal = moneylenders (high rates, no supervision, no collateral).
  • SHG = 15–20 women, save ₹25–100, lend internally, then bank loan to the group. Those four facts cover the question.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Saying currency is accepted because it has intrinsic value. It is accepted because it is authorised by the government, and issued by the RBI.
  • Attributing Grameen Bank to India. It is in Bangladesh, founded by Muhammad Yunus.
  • Writing that SHG members borrow individually from the bank. The loan is sanctioned in the name of the group.

❓ Exam Q&A

3 marks Why is money called a medium of exchange?

Because it acts as an intermediate step in the exchange of goods and services. In a barter system, exchange requires a double coincidence of wants — each person must want what the other has. Money removes this difficulty: a person can sell his goods for money and use that money to buy whatever he wants, so it mediates between the two parties.

3 marks What is collateral and why do banks ask for it?

Collateral is an asset that the borrower owns — such as land, a building, a vehicle, livestock or deposits with banks — and uses as a guarantee to a lender until the loan is repaid. Banks ask for it so that if the borrower fails to repay, the bank can sell the asset to recover its money. Because poor households often have no collateral, they are excluded from formal credit.

5 marks Explain with an example how credit can either help or harm the borrower.

Where credit helps — Salim: Salim, a shoe manufacturer in Delhi, received a large order for 30,000 pairs of shoes to be delivered within a month. He took a loan from a trader, bought the raw material, completed the order on time, made a profit and repaid the loan. Here credit enabled him to expand production and earn more.
Where credit harms — Swapna: Swapna, a small farmer in Andhra Pradesh, borrowed ₹ 2,500 from a moneylender at a high rate of interest to cultivate groundnut. The crop failed because of pest attack, and to repay the first loan she had to take another. Over three years her debt grew, and she finally had to sell a part of her land to repay the loan. This is a debt trap — a situation where credit pushes the borrower into a cycle from which she cannot escape.
Conclusion: whether credit improves a person's situation depends on the risk involved and on whether there is support if something goes wrong. Credit is therefore essential but must be available on fair terms.

5 marks What are self-help groups and how do they help poor borrowers?

A self-help group is a group of about 15–20 members, usually women belonging to one neighbourhood, who meet regularly and save, each contributing anything from ₹ 25 to ₹ 100 or more.
Internal lending: members can take small loans from the group itself, at a rate of interest decided by the group but lower than that charged by moneylenders.
Bank loans: after a year or two of regular savings, the group becomes eligible for a loan from a bank. The loan is sanctioned in the name of the group, and the group is responsible for repayment, so members keep track of each other's borrowing.
Why it helps: it overcomes the problem of lack of collateral, provides credit at a reasonable rate, makes women financially self-reliant, and creates a platform for women to discuss health, nutrition, domestic violence and education. The Grameen Bank of Bangladesh, started by Muhammad Yunus, demonstrated that poor women are reliable borrowers; today more than 9 crore women in India belong to self-help groups.

3 marks Why do most poor households in India still depend on informal sources of credit?

Because banks and cooperatives demand collateral and documentation which poor households do not possess, banks are not present in all rural areas, and small borrowers are treated as risky. Moneylenders and traders, by contrast, lend without collateral and are available in the village, even though they charge much higher rates of interest.

3 marks What is the role of the Reserve Bank of India in supervising banks?

The RBI supervises the functioning of formal sources of loans. It ensures that banks maintain a minimum cash balance, that they lend not just to profit-making businesses but also to small cultivators, small-scale industries and small borrowers, and that they submit periodical information to the RBI on how much they are lending, to whom and at what interest rate.

✍️ Hands-on task

Ask three adults how they would raise ₹ 20,000 in an emergency and record the source, whether collateral is needed and roughly what interest would be charged. Sort the answers into formal and informal — this is the data the exam expects you to reason with.
