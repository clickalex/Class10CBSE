🎯 Marks lens

Part of the 20 geography marks. Usually one 3-mark or 5-mark question, plus map pointing from the national parks and sanctuaries named here.
The causes of depletion and the categories of species are the two lists to learn word-perfect; both are asked almost every year.

📖 Deep concepts

1. Biodiversity. The variety of plants, animals and micro-organisms, and the ecosystems they live in. India is one of the world's mega-diverse countries: about 8 per cent of all recorded species are found here — roughly 47,000 species of plants and 81,000 species of animals, of which about 15,000 are flowering plants (6 per cent of the world's total). Three levels are protected together: genetic diversity, species diversity and ecosystem diversity.
2. How species are classified. The IUCN Red List groups species by how close they are to extinction, and a species found only in one area is endemic to that area.

CategoryMeaningExamples
Normal speciesPopulation levels are normalCattle, rodents, some fisheries
Endangered speciesIn danger of extinction; survival is difficult if the negative factors continueBlack buck, crocodile, Indian wild ass, Indian rhino, lion-tailed macaque, sangai deer
Vulnerable speciesPopulation is declining and the species is likely to become endangeredBlue sheep, Asiatic elephant, gangetic dolphin
Rare speciesSmall population in a limited area; may become endangeredHimalayan brown bear, wild Asiatic buffalo, desert fox, hornbill
Extinct speciesNo longer found in the areas they once lived inAsiatic cheetah, pink-headed duck, Himalayan mountain quail
Endemic speciesFound only in one particular areaAndaman teal, Nicobar pigeon, Andaman wild pig, mithun

3. Causes of depletion. Agricultural expansion and shifting cultivation; large development projects — dams, roads, railways and mines; mining and quarrying; grazing and the collection of fuel wood; forest fires; poaching and the illegal trade in skins, tusks and horns; pollution; and habitat destruction, which splits a population into small groups that a single fire or disease can wipe out. Over-population and unequal consumption in the richer countries put further pressure on the forest.
4. What the government did. The Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 gave legal protection to listed species and their habitats and banned hunting. Project Tiger (1973) and Project Elephant followed, along with crocodile breeding and special protection for the rhino, the Kashmir stag and the great Indian bustard. National parks, wildlife sanctuaries and biosphere reserves were set up. In the 1980s and 1990s the Supreme Court ordered a ban on hunting protected species and on trade in their products, and stopped mining in several reserves.
5. Who owns the forest. The Forest Department classifies forest land three ways:

TypeWhat it isShare
Reserved forestsThe most valuable forests for conservation; entry and grazing are bannedMore than half of the total forest land
Protected forestsProtected from further depletion; limited grazing and collection allowedAbout one-third of the total forest land
Unclassed forestsForests and wastelands held by the government, by communities or by private ownersThe remaining land

6. Conservation by communities. Much of the best protection has come from the people who live in the forest. Villagers near the Sariska Tiger Reserve (Alwar, Rajasthan) fought against mining inside the reserve, and villages around the Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri' in the same district framed their own rules banning hunting and protecting wildlife. The Chipko movement in the Himalayas stopped felling when women hugged the trees, and the Beej Bachao Andolan at Tehri saved indigenous seeds and crops. Joint Forest Management (JFM), introduced in 1988 and first adopted by Odisha, has communities protecting and regenerating forests along with the Forest Department in return for a share of the produce. Sacred groves — the Sarna and devrai patches left untouched because a deity is believed to live there, protected by custom rather than by law — have preserved some of the oldest forest left in the country.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Order of seriousness: normal → rare → vulnerable → endangered → extinct. Rare is few and local, vulnerable is falling, endangered is nearly gone.
  • One example each: black buck (endangered), Asiatic elephant (vulnerable), Himalayan brown bear (rare), Asiatic cheetah (extinct), mithun (endemic).
  • Movements: Chipko hugs trees, Beej Bachao saves seeds, JFM shares the forest, sacred groves are protected by custom.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Mixing up rare and endangered. Rare means a small population in a limited area; endangered means the species will be wiped out if the present trend continues.
  • Writing that the Wildlife (Protection) Act is of 1973. The Act is 1972 and Project Tiger is 1973.
  • Forgetting that JFM was first adopted in Odisha in 1988, and that reserved forests have the largest share of forest land.

❓ Exam Q&A

3 marks What are the categories of species recognised for conservation? Give one example of each.

Normal species — population levels are normal, e.g. cattle and rodents.
Endangered species — in danger of extinction, e.g. the black buck and the Indian rhino.
Vulnerable species — declining and likely to become endangered, e.g. the Asiatic elephant.
Rare species — a small population in a limited area, e.g. the Himalayan brown bear.
Extinct species — no longer found, e.g. the Asiatic cheetah.
Endemic species — found only in one area, e.g. the Andaman teal.

5 marks Explain any five causes of the depletion of forests and wildlife in India.

Agricultural expansion and shifting cultivation have cleared large forest tracts.
Large development projects — dams, roads, railways and mines — submerge or cut through forests.
Mining and quarrying destroy habitats and pollute streams.
Grazing and the collection of fuel wood prevent regeneration.
Poaching and illegal trade in skins, tusks and horns target the most threatened species.
Habitat destruction and fragmentation leave small populations that a single fire or disease can wipe out.

3 marks How have local communities conserved forests and wildlife? Give two examples.

Villagers around the Sariska Tiger Reserve in Alwar (Rajasthan) fought against mining in the reserve, and villages of the Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri' in the same district framed their own rules banning hunting and protecting wildlife. The Chipko movement stopped felling in the Himalayas when women hugged the trees, the Beej Bachao Andolan saved indigenous seeds, and in sacred groves such as the Sarna and devrai, forests are protected by religious custom rather than by law.

3 marks What is Joint Forest Management and why does it matter?

Joint Forest Management, introduced in 1988 and first adopted in Odisha, is a programme in which local communities protect and regenerate forests together with the Forest Department, in return for a share of the produce of the forest. It matters because protection works only when the people who live next to the forest have an interest in it.

✍️ Hands-on task

Take an outline map of India and mark five national parks or sanctuaries from the CBSE map list — for example Corbett, Ranthambore, Kanha, Periyar, Sunderbans, Kaziranga — writing the state against each. Then answer in five points: why does the protection of a forest succeed only when the people living next to it are involved?
