Chapter 4 · Print Culture and the Modern World

Chapter 4 of 21. Three blocks: printing in China, Japan and Korea; Gutenberg and Europe; and print in India. The India section carries most of the marks.

🎯 Marks lens

3–5 marks, usually on print in colonial India — the vernacular press, censorship, or how print helped the spread of nationalist ideas.
Gutenberg and the woodblock tradition in China and Japan are the standard 3-mark answers from the first half.

📖 Deep concepts

1. Print in China, Japan and Korea. China was the major producer of printed matter for a long time, using hand printing — rubbing paper against a woodblock with inked characters. It developed because of the large imperial state bureaucracy, for which textbooks for the civil service examinations were printed in volume. Print was used by Buddhist missionaries (the oldest printed book, the Diamond Sutra, AD 868), by merchants and by scholars; from the seventeenth century urban culture also used it for plays, tea-house gatherings and illustrated novels. Japan learned hand printing from Chinese Buddhist missionaries around AD 768–770; the oldest Japanese book, the Buddhist Diamond Sutra, was printed in AD 868, and by the medieval period books were printed at Kyoto, though hand printing did not decline. Korea developed metal type printing early, but woodblocks remained common.
2. Print comes to Europe and Gutenberg. Marco Polo returned to Italy in 1295 after years of exploration in China and brought the technology of woodblock printing with him; it spread across Europe. Demand for books grew but hand-copying manuscripts on vellum was slow, expensive and fragile, so the wealthy patronised manuscripts while students and universities began to prefer printed books. Johann Gutenberg grew up on a large agricultural estate, knew wine and olive presses from childhood, and by the 1430s had perfected the printing press, adapting the olive press and developing a metal type mould. His first book was the Bible, printed in about three years — 180 copies — which was fast for the time. He printed books with decorative borders and hand-painted illustrations, so early printed books closely resembled manuscripts.
3. The print revolution and its impact. Between 1450 and 1550 printers set up presses in most European countries; about 20 million copies of books were printed in Europe in the second half of the fifteenth century. Printed books were cheaper and reached a wider audience, so a new reading public emerged. Martin Luther posted his Ninety-Five Theses on a church door in Wittenberg in 1517, protesting the abuses of the Catholic Church; his writings were immediately reproduced in vast numbers and read widely, and his translation of the New Testament sold 5,000 copies within a few weeks. Luther himself wrote gratefully of printing, calling it 'the greatest and extremest grace of God'. The Roman Catholic Church, alarmed, began keeping an Index of Prohibited Books from the mid-sixteenth century. Print also produced the reading mania of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and the spread of ideas that helped the French Revolution — the writings of Voltaire and Rousseau created a culture of dialogue and debate, and caricatures and cartoons criticised the monarchy.
4. Print comes to India. Manuscripts in Sanskrit, Arabic and Persian were copied on palm leaf or handmade paper, but they were fragile, expensive and not easily handled, so they were not widely used in everyday life even though pre-colonial India had a rich tradition of oral knowledge — sacred texts were recited and heard, not read. The printing press first came to Goa with Portuguese missionaries in the mid-sixteenth century; Jesuit priests learned Konkani and printed several tracts, and by 1674 about 50 books had been printed in Konkani and Kanara. Catholic priests printed the first Tamil book in 1579 at Cochin, and the first book in Malayalam in 1713. The Dutch Protestants printed 32 Tamil texts, many of them translations. English printing came late — the English East India Company began importing presses from the late seventeenth century, and James Augustus Hicky began the Bengal Gazette in 1780, described as 'a commercial paper open to all parties but influenced by none'.
5. Print and the nineteenth-century Indian public sphere. Religious reform produced a flood of printed debate: Raja Rammohun Roy published the Sambad Kaumudi from 1821, and the Mirat-ul-Akhbar in Persian; the Deoband seminary, founded in 1867, published thousands of fatwas. Hindi print grew from the 1870s; Bharatendu Harishchandra and others used newspapers to promote Hindi. Urdu print expanded after the 1850s with popular romances and qissas. Women too began publishing — Tarabai Shinde's Stripurushtulna (1882) and Pandita Ramabai's writings on widowhood — though most writings by and about women were still by men. Cheap books and libraries multiplied from the early twentieth century, and vernacular newspapers spread nationalism: Bal Gangadhar Tilak's Kesari and Maratha supported the Punjab revolutionaries, and his imprisonment in 1908 for defending them sparked protests. The British responded with censorship — the Vernacular Press Act of 1878, modelled on Irish press laws, gave the government power to confiscate the printing press of any newspaper that published 'seditious' material.
6. Print and the making of public opinion. Cheap print did more than spread information — it created a reading public that could be addressed, and therefore a public opinion that governments had to reckon with. Newspapers carried advertisements, letters and debates side by side, so a reader in one city could follow, and join, an argument going on elsewhere. This is why every authoritarian regime in the chapter's story begins by licensing, taxing or seizing presses rather than by arguing back.
7. Reading is never passive. The chapter ends by showing that readers made their own use of print: workers in England read aloud in taverns, women in India read novels that were denounced from the pulpit, and the poor who could not read listened to those who could. Oral culture did not disappear with print — it carried print further. That is the nuance examiners reward: print multiplied readership, but it depended on listeners too.

🛠️ Method, step by step

  1. Start with the technology and the route — woodblock printing from China, Gutenberg's press in 1448, and the spread of paper.
  2. Explain the social effect of cheap print — more readers, lower prices, and ideas that a government can no longer contain by licensing alone.
  3. For India, give two concrete developments — the vernacular presses, the nineteenth-century reform debates, and the colonial Vernacular Press Act of 1878.
  4. Close on what print did to religion and reform — it froze some traditions in standard form, and it gave reformers an audience far beyond the pulpit.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Three 'firsts' in India: press came with the Portuguese at Goa; first Tamil book 1579 (Cochin); first English newspaper Bengal Gazette, 1780, by Hicky.
  • Gutenberg = wine press + metal type; first book = the Bible. Those two facts cover the 3-mark question.
  • Vernacular Press Act 1878 = the censorship answer; Kesari and Maratha = Tilak.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Saying the printing press was invented in India by the British. It first came to Goa with Portuguese missionaries in the mid-sixteenth century.
  • Giving the Diamond Sutra's date as AD 768–770. That is when Chinese printing reached Japan; the Diamond Sutra was printed in AD 868.
  • Attributing Kesari to Gandhi. It was Bal Gangadhar Tilak.
  • Describing the Vernacular Press Act as encouraging Indian newspapers. It was a repressive act that allowed confiscation of presses.

SHORTS12 marks

1. What were the penny dreadfuls and what do they tell us about the readers of print?

Show Answer

Ans:

Penny dreadfuls were cheap serial novels sold in Victorian Britain for a penny. They show that print had begun to reach working-class readers, that publishers were producing for a mass market rather than for the elite, and that the new readers wanted exciting, escapist stories.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Why did Martin Luther's ideas spread so rapidly across Europe?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because his writings were printed. When he posted his Ninety-Five Theses on the church door at Wittenberg in 1517, printers reproduced them in vast numbers within days, and they were read widely and discussed everywhere. His German translation of the New Testament sold 5,000 copies within a few weeks — something impossible in a manuscript culture.

SHORTS43 marks

4. Why were manuscripts not widely used in everyday life in pre-colonial India?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because they were written on palm leaf or fragile handmade paper, were expensive to produce, and could not be handled or read easily. They were also copied by hand, so copies were few and easily damaged, and the script was written in a variety of styles that made reading difficult. Knowledge was therefore largely transmitted orally — texts were recited and memorised rather than read.

SHORTS53 marks

5. What was the Vernacular Press Act of 1878?

Show Answer

Ans:

It was a law modelled on Irish press laws that gave the government extensive powers to censor reports and editorials in the vernacular press. If a newspaper published anything judged seditious and did not heed an initial warning, its press could be confiscated, including its printing machinery. It was widely resented and became a symbol of colonial repression of the Indian press.

SHORTS83 marks

8. Describe how print came to India and the role it played in social reform.

Show Answer

Ans:

Printing presses were first set up in Goa by Christian missionaries in the mid-sixteenth century; from the 1780s Bengali and other vernacular presses multiplied.
Reformers used print: Raja Rammohun Roy published tracts against sati and idolatry, and Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar wrote in favour of widow remarriage.
Women read and wrote in the popular Bengali almanacs and domestic magazines, and public libraries and cheap books carried these debates to ordinary readers.

LONGL114 marks

11. Explain why colonial governments tried to control the press and how nationalist newspapers responded.

Show Answer

Ans:

The colonial state feared that criticism in the vernacular press would spread sedition and unite people against British rule.
It used measures such as the Vernacular Press Act of 1878, which allowed it to confiscate the assets of newspapers that published objectionable material, and it monitored Indian-owned presses.
Nationalist papers such as Kesari (Tilak), Amrita Bazar Patrika and The Hindu evaded these controls, reported official repression, and used cartoons, ballads and vernacular prose to reach ordinary readers.
Print thus created a public opinion outside the government's control and became a major force in the freedom struggle.

LONGL125 marks

12. How did print culture help the growth of nationalism in India?

Show Answer

Ans:

Newspapers carried nationalist criticism. Vernacular newspapers such as Tilak's Kesari and Maratha reported colonial misrule and supported the Punjab revolutionaries; Tilak's imprisonment in 1908 provoked widespread protest.
Religious and social reform debates were printed. Raja Rammohun Roy's Sambad Kaumudi, the Mirat-ul-Akhbar, and the fatwas of the Deoband seminary spread reformist and religious ideas to a wide readership.
Cheap books and libraries made knowledge available to the poor and to workers, and novels and pamphlets carried political ideas in an easily readable form.
Writings by and about women — Tarabai Shinde's Stripurushtulna, Pandita Ramabai on widowhood — created a new public debate on social questions.
Censorship itself proved the point. The Vernacular Press Act of 1878, which allowed the government to seize presses, showed how powerful print had become, and made the press a recognised weapon of the national movement.
