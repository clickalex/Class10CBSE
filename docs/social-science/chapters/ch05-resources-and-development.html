Chapter 5 · Resources and Development

Chapter 5 of 20. One classification table, one soil table, and a handful of Indian examples with state names. That is the whole chapter.

🎯 Marks lens

3–4 marks. Soil types with crops and states is the most repeated question; sustainable development and Agenda 21 recur as short answers.
Always give an Indian example with a state — an answer with only textbook definitions scores at most half.

📖 Deep concepts

1. What is a resource? Anything available in our environment that can be used to satisfy our needs, provided it is technologically accessible, economically feasible and culturally acceptable. Human beings are themselves resources — hence human resource. Development of resources happens through interaction between nature, technology and institutions.
2. Classification of resources. By origin: biotic (forest, animals) and abiotic (rocks, metals). By exhaustibility: renewable (solar, wind, water) and non-renewable (coal, petroleum). By ownership: individual, community, national and international (resources beyond 200 nautical miles of the Exclusive Economic Zone are open resources for all). By status of development: potential, developed, stock and reserves.
3. Sustainable development. Resource depletion is a global concern; the answer is development that does not harm the environment and does not compromise the needs of future generations. The Club of Rome's Limits to Growth (1972) first argued this systematically, and Brundtland Commission Report, Our Common Future (1987) made the term standard. Agenda 21, adopted at the first Earth Summit at Rio de Janeiro in 1992, is a global plan of action for sustainable development; India's National Environment Policy and the Rio Convention follow it.
4. Land use in India. India's total geographical area is 3.28 million sq km, of which land-use data is available for only about 93%. Net sown area is about 43%; forest is about 22.8% against the 33% recommended by the National Forest Policy of 1952. Land degradation — about 130 million hectares — comes from mining (Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, MP, Odisha), overgrazing (Gujarat, Rajasthan, MP, Maharashtra), over-irrigation causing waterlogging and salinity (Punjab, Haryana, western UP), and industrial effluents. Remedies: afforestation, proper grazing management, shelter belts, control on mining, treatment of industrial effluent.
5. Soil as a resource. Soil formation depends on relief, parent rock, climate, vegetation, other life forms and time. The main types:

6. Soil erosion and conservation. Erosion is the washing away of the top soil by water, wind or glacial action; a badland or ravine is land made unfit for cultivation (the Chambal basin). Gully erosion (running water cuts channels), sheet erosion (water flows as a sheet over a slope) and wind erosion in arid areas. Conservation: contour ploughing, terrace cultivation on slopes (Western and Central Himalayas), strip cropping, shelter belts of trees, and mulching.

🛠️ Method, step by step

  1. Classify the resource first — renewable or non-renewable, actual or potential, ubiquitous or localised — because the classification drives every answer.
  2. Name the problem that follows from use: depletion, pollution, or unequal access.
  3. Give the Indian example that fits — the Jharkhand iron ore belt, the Aravalli ranges, groundwater in Punjab, or black soil in Maharashtra.
  4. Close with sustainable development and one concrete measure: reduce, recycle, or substitute, with afforestation and rainwater harvesting as the standard examples.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Four ways to classify: origin, exhaustibility, ownership, status of development — 'O-E-O-S'.
  • Black soil = cotton = Deccan trap = Maharashtra. One chain of four words that answers most black-soil questions.
  • Laterite is formed by leaching in high-rainfall hilly areas — that is why it is poor for cereals but good for tea and coffee.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Giving forest cover as 33%. Actual forest area is about 22.8%; 33% is the target of the National Forest Policy of 1952.
  • Describing a resource as anything useful. It must also be technologically accessible and economically feasible — uranium in Ladakh is a potential resource.
  • Attributing waterlogging and salinity to mining. They come from over-irrigation in Punjab, Haryana and western UP.

SHORTS12 marks

1. Why is it said that a resource is a function of human wants and technology?

Show Answer

Ans:

A substance becomes a resource only when human beings find a use for it and have the technology and the will to use it. Petroleum was just a black liquid until refining technology made it valuable; as technology and wants change, substances move in and out of being resources.

SHORTS23 marks

2. Distinguish between renewable and non-renewable resources with examples.

Show Answer

Ans:

Renewable resources can be renewed or reproduced by physical, chemical or mechanical processes and are inexhaustible in the long run — solar and wind energy, water, forests and wildlife. Non-renewable resources occur over very long geological periods and cannot be replenished once used up — coal, petroleum and natural gas, and metallic minerals such as iron ore.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Explain the main features of black soil.

Show Answer

Ans:

It is also called regur soil and is ideal for growing cotton. It is made of extremely fine clayey material, is well known for its capacity to hold moisture, and is rich in soil nutrients such as calcium carbonate, magnesium, potash and lime, though poor in phosphoric content. It develops deep cracks during hot weather, which helps aeration, and becomes sticky when wet. It is found in the Deccan trap region — Maharashtra, Saurashtra, Malwa and parts of Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh.

SHORTS43 marks

4. What are the causes of land degradation in India and how can it be controlled?

Show Answer

Ans:

Causes: mining in Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, MP and Odisha has left deep scars; overgrazing in Gujarat, Rajasthan, MP and Maharashtra; over-irrigation in Punjab, Haryana and western UP causing waterlogging and increased salinity and alkalinity; and industrial effluents polluting land and water.
Control: large-scale afforestation, proper management of grazing, shelter belts of plants, stabilisation of sand dunes by growing thorny bushes, proper discharge and treatment of industrial effluents, and control on mining.

SHORTS73 marks

7. Suggest any three methods of soil conservation used in India.

Show Answer

Ans:

Contour ploughing and terracing on hill slopes slow down the flow of water and check soil erosion.
Strip cropping and planting shelterbelts of trees reduce erosion caused by wind, especially in dry regions.
Controlling overgrazing, preventing deforestation and practising crop rotation and gully plugging keep the soil covered and fertile.

LONGL104 marks

10. Compare alluvial soil and laterite soil — how each is formed, where it is found and what it grows.

Show Answer

Ans:

Alluvial soil is deposited by rivers and is very fertile, rich in potash and lime but poor in nitrogen and humus; it covers the northern plains and the deltas and grows rice, wheat, sugarcane and pulses.
Laterite soil forms in hot, wet areas where heavy rain leaches away lime and silica, leaving iron and aluminium oxides; it is acidic and low in humus.
It is found on the summits of the Western Ghats, eastern Maharashtra, Odisha, parts of Chhattisgarh and the north-east.
With manures and irrigation it grows tea, coffee, rubber, cashew and tapioca.

LONGL115 marks

11. What is sustainable development and why is it important? Refer to Agenda 21.

Show Answer

Ans:

Sustainable development is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. It requires that economic growth go hand in hand with environmental protection and social equity.
Why it matters: indiscriminate use of resources has led to depletion, concentration of resources in a few hands, land degradation and global ecological crises such as global warming and ozone depletion.
Agenda 21 was adopted by the first Earth Summit held at Rio de Janeiro in 1992. It is a declaration of global agreement and international cooperation to combat environmental damage, poverty and disease through sustainable development, and it also provides that every local government should draw its own local Agenda 21.
