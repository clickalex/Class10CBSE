Chapter 12 · Power Sharing

Chapter 12 of 21. Two case studies and one list of four forms. The shortest route to 20 marks in Social Science starts here.

🎯 Marks lens

3–5 marks. The Belgium and Sri Lanka comparison and the forms of power sharing are the set questions.
Answers are point-based: five distinct points for a 5-mark answer, each with a heading. Long paragraphs score less.

📖 Deep concepts

1. The case of Belgium. Belgium is a small country in Europe, smaller in area than Haryana, with a population just over one crore. It has a complex ethnic composition: 59% live in the Flemish region and speak Dutch, 40% in the Wallonia region and speak French, and 1% speak German. In the capital Brussels, 80% speak French and 20% Dutch — the French-speaking minority there was relatively rich and powerful. Between 1932 and 1968 the constitution was amended four times to work out an arrangement that would enable everyone to live together: an equal number of Dutch- and French-speaking ministers in the central government; many powers given to state governments of the two regions, which are not subordinate to the central government; Brussels given a separate government with equal representation for both groups; and a third kind of government — the community government — elected by people belonging to one language group (Dutch, French or German-speaking) regardless of where they live, with power over culture, education and language.
2. The case of Sri Lanka. An island nation with about 74% Sinhala speakers and 18% Tamils, most of them concentrated in the north and east. In 1956 an act was passed to recognise Sinhala as the only official language, disregarding Tamil. The governments followed preferential policies that favoured Sinhala applicants for university positions and government employment, and the new constitution stipulated that the state shall protect and foster Buddhism. Sri Lankan Tamils demanded recognition of Tamil as an official language, regional autonomy and equal opportunity. Their demand for a separate Tamil state, Tamil Eelam, led to a civil war that began in the 1980s and continued until 2009, leaving thousands dead and damaging the social, cultural and economic life of the country.
3. Why power sharing is desirable. Prudential reasons: power sharing reduces the possibility of conflict between social groups; since social conflict often leads to violence and political instability, sharing power is a good way to ensure the stability of the political order. Imposing the will of the majority community over others may seem attractive in the short run but undermines national unity in the long run. Moral reasons: power sharing is the very spirit of democracy. A democratic government must share power with those affected by its exercise and who have to live with its effects; people have a right to be consulted on how they are to be governed. Legitimate government is one in which citizens, through participation, acquire a stake in the system.
4. Forms of power sharing. (a) Horizontal distribution — among organs of government such as the legislature, executive and judiciary, which allows one organ to check the others; this is the system of checks and balances. (b) Vertical distribution — among governments at different levels: the Union or central government, the state governments and local bodies such as municipalities and panchayats. The constitution specifies how the powers of each are to be exercised. (c) Among social groups — community government in Belgium, and reserved constituencies in assemblies and parliaments in India for weaker sections and women. (d) Among political parties, pressure groups and movements — coalition governments when no single party gets a clear majority, so two or more parties share power.
5. India's arrangements. Power sharing is built into the constitution: between the Union and the states through the Union List, State List and Concurrent List; between organs through an independent judiciary that can strike down laws that violate the constitution; through reserved seats for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and, in local bodies, one-third of seats for women; and through coalition governments at the centre and in the states.
6. Belgium versus Sri Lanka, in one comparison. Both countries faced a majority–minority split; Belgium redrew its constitution four times to give Dutch and French speakers equal representation in the central government, while Sri Lanka passed the Sinhala Only Act of 1956 and made the majority's language the state's. Belgium stayed united; Sri Lanka fought a civil war for a quarter of a century. The chapter's point is not that one country is virtuous — it is that accommodation was cheaper, and that is the argument for power sharing everywhere.
7. The two reasons power sharing is defended. The prudential reason: sharing power avoids the violence that follows when a community is permanently excluded. The moral reason: all citizens have a right to be consulted about how they are governed, which is what democracy means. Prudential arguments alone can be reversed by whoever holds power today; the moral argument is the one that makes power sharing a principle rather than a tactic.

🛠️ Method, step by step

  1. Name the country the question gives you — Belgium, Sri Lanka, India, or Northern Ireland — and its specific arrangement.
  2. Identify the form of power sharing — horizontal (legislature, executive, judiciary), vertical (union, state, local), among social groups, or among parties.
  3. Give the contrast the chapter builds: Belgium accommodated, Sri Lanka imposed, and the outcomes differed accordingly.
  4. Close with the argument for prudence — power sharing reduces conflict and is the very spirit of democracy, not a concession granted to the weak.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Belgium accommodated, Sri Lanka imposed. Those two words are the entire comparison — then add three details for each.
  • Four forms: horizontal, vertical, social groups, political parties. 'H-V-S-P'.
  • Prudential = practical benefit; Moral = it is right in itself. Always label which is which.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Giving Belgium's language shares the wrong way round. 59% Flemish (Dutch), 40% Walloon (French), 1% German.
  • Saying the Sri Lankan civil war ended in 2005. It ended in 2009.
  • Confusing horizontal (organs at the same level) with vertical (levels of government).

SHORTS12 marks

1. Why is power sharing desirable in a democracy?

Show Answer

Ans:

It ensures that no single community or group can dominate the others, reduces the fear of oppression and the chances of conflict, respects the claims of minorities and regions, and makes the government more stable, accountable and legitimate in the eyes of the people.

SHORTS23 marks

2. What is the difference between prudential and moral reasons for power sharing?

Show Answer

Ans:

Prudential reasons stress the good consequences of power sharing — it reduces conflict between social groups, ensures the stability of political order and prevents violence and political instability. Moral reasons stress the intrinsic worth of power sharing — that people have a right to be consulted on how they are to be governed, and that a legitimate government is one in which citizens acquire a stake in the system through participation. Prudential reasons are about outcomes; moral reasons are about principles.

SHORTS33 marks

3. What are the different forms of power sharing in modern democracies? Give one example of each.

Show Answer

Ans:

Horizontal distribution among organs of government — legislature, executive and judiciary, as in India, where the judiciary can review the acts of the legislature. Vertical distribution among governments at different levels — Union, state and local governments in India. Distribution among social groups — the community government in Belgium and reserved constituencies for weaker sections and women in India. Distribution among political parties — coalition governments formed by two or more parties when no single party gains a clear majority.

SHORTS43 marks

4. What is a system of checks and balances?

Show Answer

Ans:

It is the arrangement in which the organs of government — legislature, executive and judiciary — are placed at the same level but each exercises a check on the others. The legislature makes laws, the executive implements them and the judiciary can declare a law invalid if it violates the constitution. Because of these checks no organ can exercise unlimited power, and this horizontal distribution of power is a basic feature of democracy.

Show Answer

LONGL104 marks

10. Describe the different forms of power sharing in modern democracies, with an example of each.

Show Answer

Ans:

Horizontal distribution among the legislature, executive and judiciary, so that each organ checks the others — for example, the Indian Parliament makes laws while the courts can declare them unconstitutional.
Vertical division between the Union, States and local governments — federalism, as in India, Belgium and the USA.
Sharing among different social groups, such as the community government in Belgium, where Dutch-, French- and German-speaking communities have equal representation on some matters.
Sharing among political parties, pressure groups and movements through coalitions and competitive politics, so that power is not concentrated in one party.

LONGL115 marks

11. Describe the major steps taken towards power sharing in Belgium.

Show Answer

Ans:

Equal representation in the central government: the constitution prescribes an equal number of Dutch-speaking and French-speaking ministers, so no single community can take decisions unilaterally.
Devolution to the regions: many powers of the central government were given to the state governments of the Flemish and Walloon regions, and these regional governments are not subordinate to the central government.
Brussels: the capital was given a separate government with equal representation for both the French- and Dutch-speaking groups.
Community government: a third kind of government was set up, elected by people belonging to one language group — Dutch, French or German-speaking — irrespective of where they live, with power over cultural, educational and language-related issues.
This arrangement, worked out between 1932 and 1968 through four constitutional amendments, prevented civic strife and the division of the country on linguistic lines.

LONGL125 marks

12. What were the majoritarian measures adopted by the Sri Lankan government? What were their consequences?

Show Answer

Ans:

In 1956 an act was passed to recognise Sinhala as the only official language, disregarding Tamil.
Successive governments followed preferential policies that favoured Sinhala applicants for university positions and government employment.
The new constitution stipulated that the state shall protect and foster Buddhism.
Consequences: the Sri Lankan Tamils felt that none of the major political parties, led by the Buddhist Sinhala leaders, was sensitive to their language and culture. Their demand for recognition of Tamil as an official language, regional autonomy and equal opportunity was repeatedly denied, and the demand for a separate Tamil Eelam grew. This led to a civil war that lasted until 2009, in which thousands of people were killed and the social, cultural and economic life of the country was badly affected.
