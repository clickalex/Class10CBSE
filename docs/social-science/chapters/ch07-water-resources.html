Chapter 7 · Water Resources

Chapter 7 of 20. Two sides of one argument: dams are development, and dams displace people. Learn both and you can answer any question set on this chapter.

🎯 Marks lens

3 marks. Multi-purpose river projects with their benefits and drawbacks is the set question; rainwater harvesting methods come second.
Name Indian projects and places — Bhakra Nangal, Hirakud, Sardar Sarovar, Narmada Bachao Andolan, Rajasthan's johads and khadins.

📖 Deep concepts

1. Water resources. About three-fourths of the earth's surface is covered with water, but only about 2.5% is fresh water, and nearly 70% of that is frozen in ice sheets; almost all the rest is groundwater. India ranks 133rd in the world in terms of water availability per person per annum (2010 data). Renewable water resources amount to about 1,869 cubic km. About 22% of the total water used is in the industrial sector; over 80% is used in agriculture.
2. Water scarcity. It means insufficient availability of water in terms of quantity (low rainfall, drought) and quality (polluted water). Causes: growing population, greater demand for food and cash crops, urbanisation with lifestyles that use more water, over-exploitation and unequal access between social groups. Even areas with abundant water suffer scarcity because of pollution from domestic and industrial waste, pesticides and fertilisers.
3. Multi-purpose river projects. After independence, dams were seen as the vehicles of development — Nehru called them the temples of modern India. A multi-purpose project has several integrated uses: irrigation, electricity generation, flood control, inland navigation, fish breeding, recreation and water supply. Examples: Bhakra Nangal (Satluj), Hirakud (Mahanadi), Damodar Valley Corporation, Kopili, and the Narmada and Sardar Sarovar projects on the Narmada.
4. Opposition to dams. Large dams submerge vast areas of forest and farmland and displace local communities — the Narmada Bachao Andolan and the Tehri Dam Andolan are the best-known movements. Jawaharlal Nehru's dams also created conflicts between industrialists and urban centres on one side and the agricultural population on the other; in Gujarat, the Sabarmati-basin farmers were almost rioting in 1999 when the Sardar Sarovar project gave priority to water supply for urban areas. Inter-state disputes also arise over sharing costs and benefits, as in the Krishna-Godavari dispute when Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh objected to Maharashtra's plan to divert more water at Koyna. Earthquake-induced damage, sedimentation and waterlogging are further drawbacks.
5. Rainwater harvesting — the alternative. In semi-arid Rajasthan, houses traditionally had tanks attached to the roof (tanka, sometimes as big as a room) for storing drinking water; the first spell of rain was not collected as it cleans the roof. Modern methods: rooftop rainwater harvesting (widely used in Meghalaya, and made compulsory in Tamil Nadu for all houses, with legal provisions for defaulters), gully plugs, contour bunds, check dams, percolation tanks, recharge pits and khadins and johads in agricultural fields, where rainwater is allowed to stand and moisten the soil. In the flood plains of West Bengal, inundation canals irrigate fields; in the western Himalayas, diversion channels called guls or kuls carry water to villages.
6. Bamboo drip irrigation is a two-hundred-year-old system of Meghalaya in which bamboo pipes are used to divert perennial springs on hilltops to the lower reaches by gravity — about 18–20 litres of water enters the bamboo system and reaches the plant site as 20–80 drops per minute.
7. Scarcity is often a distribution problem. India's water crisis is rarely a shortage of water in absolute terms — it is a shortage in the right place, at the right time, for the right people. Monsoon rain arrives in four months and is stored badly; groundwater is pumped out faster than it is recharged because power is subsidised; and cities pipe water over long distances to lawns while villages queue at a handpump. Saying this turns a descriptive answer into an analytical one, and it is the point most exam questions on this chapter are aimed at.

🛠️ Method, step by step

  1. State the problem in numbers if you have them — per capita availability, or the fact that demand rises while supply is fixed.
  2. Separate the causes: monsoon dependence, over-extraction of groundwater, pollution, and inequity between regions and users.
  3. Give one dam and its two sides — Bhakra-Nangal or Hirakud for irrigation and power, and the displacement and siltation that follow.
  4. Close with the alternative — rainwater harvesting, watershed management, and the 'one crop, one drop' logic of drip irrigation.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Dams: give three benefits and three drawbacks in every answer — irrigation, power, flood control against displacement, sedimentation and conflict. Full marks need both sides.
  • Rainwater harvesting = 'catch it where it falls'. Rooftop in Meghalaya and Tamil Nadu; khadins and johads in Rajasthan; guls and kuls in the Himalayas; inundation canals in West Bengal.
  • Only 2.5% of the earth's water is fresh. That single statistic anchors most scarcity answers.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Writing only the benefits of dams. The question almost always asks for drawbacks too.
  • Attributing the Tehri Dam movement to the Narmada valley. They are separate movements — Tehri is on the Bhagirathi in Uttarakhand.
  • Saying rooftop harvesting is compulsory across India. It is compulsory in Tamil Nadu, with legal provisions for defaulters.

SHORTS12 marks

1. Why is water scarcity in India often a problem of management rather than of availability?

Show Answer

Ans:

India receives plenty of monsoon rain, but most of it flows away unused. Scarcity is caused by over-extraction of groundwater, unequal access between rich and poor, pollution of rivers and tanks by domestic and industrial waste, and wasteful irrigation and domestic use — so the problem is how water is stored, distributed and used.

SHORTS23 marks

2. Why is water scarcity increasing in India?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because of a growing population, which raises the demand for water for drinking and for growing more food and cash crops; rapid urbanisation and changing lifestyles that consume more water; over-exploitation of groundwater by commercial farming; and unequal access between social groups. In many regions water is abundant but polluted by domestic and industrial waste and by pesticides and fertilisers, so it cannot be used without treatment.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Describe the traditional rainwater harvesting system of Rajasthan.

Show Answer

Ans:

In the semi-arid regions of Rajasthan, particularly in Bikaner, Phalodi and Barmer, most houses traditionally had underground tanks or tankas for storing drinking water. The tanks were built inside the house or the courtyard and were connected to the sloping roofs. The first spell of rain was not collected, as it cleans the roof and the pipes; from the second shower onward the water was stored. The stored water lasted through the dry season and was treated as pure enough for religious use.

SHORTS43 marks

4. What is rooftop rainwater harvesting and where has it been made compulsory?

Show Answer

Ans:

It is a method in which rainwater falling on rooftops is collected through pipes and stored in underground tanks or used to recharge groundwater. It is common in Meghalaya, where bamboo pipes are used, and in Tamil Nadu it has been made compulsory for all houses, with legal provisions to punish defaulters.

SHORTS73 marks

7. Mention any three problems caused by large multi-purpose river projects.

Show Answer

Ans:

Large numbers of tribal and peasant families are displaced and often inadequately resettled and rehabilitated.
Forests and biodiversity are submerged, and the flow and ecology of the river are disturbed.
Benefits are unequal: canals usually serve the richer farmers in the command area, causing waterlogging and salinity, while the poor and the downstream areas gain little.

LONGL104 marks

10. Write one important fact each about the Hirakud, Bhakra Nangal and Sardar Sarovar projects.

Show Answer

Ans:

Hirakud, on the Mahanadi in Odisha, is one of the longest dams in the world and was built mainly to control floods in the Mahanadi delta.
Bhakra Nangal, on the Sutlej, is India's highest straight gravity dam; it irrigates Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan and generates hydel power.
Sardar Sarovar, on the Narmada in Gujarat, has been at the centre of a long struggle over the displacement of adivasis and peasants, led by the Narmada Bachao Andolan.

LONGL115 marks

11. Multi-purpose river projects have several advantages but also several drawbacks. Explain.

Show Answer

Ans:

Advantages: they provide water for irrigation, generate hydroelectricity, control floods, help inland navigation, supply water to industries and cities, and provide fish breeding, recreation and landscape value. Nehru proudly called dams the temples of modern India.
Drawbacks: (i) large dams submerge forests and agricultural land and displace local communities, who are often not properly rehabilitated — the Narmada Bachao Andolan and the Tehri Dam Andolan grew out of this; (ii) they disturb the natural flow of rivers, causing poor sediment flow and excessive sedimentation at the bottom of the reservoir, which destroys habitats; (iii) they cause waterlogging and soil salinity in the command area; (iv) they trigger inter-state disputes over the sharing of costs and benefits, as between Karnataka, Maharashtra and Andhra Pradesh over the Krishna-Godavari waters; (v) they have induced earthquakes and, in the case of the Sardar Sarovar project, sparked protests when urban water supply was given priority over irrigation for farmers.
