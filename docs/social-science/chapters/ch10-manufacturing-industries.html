Chapter 10 · Manufacturing Industries

Chapter 10 of 20. Two industry families — agro-based and mineral-based — each with a location factor list. Learn cotton textiles and iron and steel properly.

📖 Deep concepts

1. Manufacturing and its importance. Manufacturing is the production of goods in large quantities after processing raw materials into more valuable products — for example, paper from wood, sugar from sugarcane, aluminium from bauxite. It contributes about 17% of GDP (of which 10% comes from mining, electricity, gas, water supply and other utilities; manufacturing alone is about 27%, and 12% comes from construction). Manufacturing is important because it modernises agriculture, reduces dependence on agricultural income by providing jobs in the secondary and tertiary sectors, eradicates unemployment and poverty, reduces regional disparities, expands trade and earns foreign exchange through exports, and brings prosperity by adding value to raw materials. Industrial pollution and the location of industries are decided by availability of raw material, cheap and skilled labour, power, market and transport.
2. Classification of industries. By source of raw material: agro-based (cotton, wool, jute, silk, sugar) and mineral-based (iron and steel, cement, aluminium). By main role: basic or key industries (which supply their products to other industries, e.g. iron and steel), and consumer industries. By capital investment: small, medium and large. By ownership: public sector (SAIL, BHEL, ONGC — owned by the government), private sector (TISCO, Bajaj Auto), joint sector (Oil India Ltd, jointly owned by the state and individuals) and cooperative sector (Amul, sugar cooperatives of Maharashtra). By weight of raw material: heavy (using bulky raw materials, e.g. iron and steel) and light. By bulk and weight of raw material and finished goods also.
3. Agro-based industries. Cotton textiles — in the early years the industry was concentrated in the cotton-growing belt of Maharashtra and Gujarat because of raw cotton, market, transport including ports, labour and moist climate. Today India exports yarn to Japan, but the weaving, knitting and processing units cannot fully use the high-quality yarn produced, and most spinning is in Maharashtra, Gujarat and Tamil Nadu while weaving is decentralised. India's production of Gandhiji's khadi, handspun and handwoven cloth, remains important; a challenge to the industry is competition from synthetic fibre. Sugar industry — India is the second largest producer of sugar and the largest producer of gur and khandsari; mills are shifting to southern and western states, especially Maharashtra, because the cane there has a higher sucrose content, a longer crushing season and the cooperatives are more successful. Jute textiles — India is the largest producer of raw jute and jute goods; most mills are in West Bengal along the banks of the Hooghly, because of proximity to jute-producing areas, inexpensive water transport, abundant water, cheap labour and the port of Kolkata. National Jute Policy 2005 aims at increasing productivity, improving quality and increasing yield per hectare.
4. Mineral-based industries. Iron and steel — a basic industry because all other industries depend on it for machinery, and a heavy industry because all its raw materials and finished products are heavy and bulky. India is the largest producer of sponge iron and ranks among the leading producers of crude steel. Chotanagpur plateau has the maximum concentration of iron and steel industries because of low cost of iron ore, high-grade raw material, cheap labour, vast growth potential in the home market and good transport. TISCO, Jamshedpur (1907, the oldest) is close to the Kolkata market; Bokaro and Bhilai (with Soviet collaboration), Durgapur and Rourkela (with German collaboration) were set up in the public sector. Problems: high cost and limited supply of coking coal, low labour productivity, irregular energy supply and poor infrastructure. Aluminium smelting — the second most important metallurgical industry; aluminium is light, resistant to corrosion and a good conductor, used in aircraft, utensils and wires; it is a smelting industry needing a regular supply of electricity and bauxite. Chemical industries contribute about 3% of GDP and are placed third in Asia; include organic (petrochemicals) and inorganic (sulphuric acid, nitric acid, caustic soda) chemicals. Fertiliser industry expanded after the Green Revolution, concentrated in Gujarat, Punjab, Haryana, UP and Tamil Nadu near natural gas reserves. Cement needs bulky raw materials — limestone, silica, alumina and gypsum — and coal, electricity and diesel; the industry has grown in Gujarat because of access to the Gulf market.
5. Information technology and electronics. The electronics industry covers a wide range from transistors to television, telephones and computers; Bengaluru has emerged as the electronic capital of India, with other important centres at Delhi, Hyderabad, Pune, Chennai, Mumbai, Mohali, Kolkata and Lucknow. Software technology parks provide a single-window service and high data communication facility to software experts. India's BPO and IT services sector has grown rapidly, and women are a large share of its workforce.
6. Industrial pollution and its control. Four types: air pollution (from chemical and paper factories, brick kilns, refineries and smelting plants burning fossil fuels), water pollution (organic and inorganic industrial wastes and effluents discharged into rivers — paper, pulp, chemical, textile and dyeing, petroleum refineries, tanneries), thermal pollution (hot water from factories drained into rivers before cooling) and noise pollution (industrial and construction activities, machinery, generators), which causes hearing impairment, psychological effects and increased heart rate. Control: treatment of industrial effluent in three stages (primary — mechanical screening and grinding; secondary — biological; tertiary — chemical and physical, before reuse), over-drawing of groundwater regulation, particulate control using electrostatic precipitators, fabric filters and inertial separators, use of oil as fuel instead of coal, fitting silencers on generators and machinery, and adopting cleaner technologies such as those promoted by NTPC, which has ISO 14001 certification.
7. Agglomeration and why it breaks down. Industries cluster because a cluster saves every firm money — shared suppliers, a trained labour force, a port, a bank. Mumbai's cotton mills, Jamshedpur's steel and the Hooghly jute belt all grew this way. The same concentration then produces its own problems: congestion, rising land rents, labour militancy and pollution, which is exactly what pushed mills out of central Mumbai and left the buildings to be redeveloped. Clusters create efficiency and then create the reasons to leave.

🛠️ Method, step by step

  1. Name the industry and its classification — agro-based, mineral-based, marine, forest-based, or on the basis of size and ownership.
  2. Give the raw material and the location logic — jute near the Hooghly, cotton in Mumbai and Ahmedabad, iron and steel near the coal-iron belt.
  3. State two problems the industry faces — power, raw material, labour unrest, outdated machinery, or pollution.
  4. Close with one remedy — modernisation, better infrastructure, stricter pollution norms, or smaller units spread through the countryside.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Iron and steel = basic + heavy + in the Chotanagpur region. Three keywords that open any 3-mark answer on steel.
  • Cotton textiles moved from the cotton belt (Maharashtra, Gujarat) to Tamil Nadu for spinning, but weaving stayed decentralised — that contrast is itself an exam answer.
  • Effluent treatment = three stages: mechanical, biological, chemical. Always give all three.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Calling iron and steel a consumer industry. It is a basic and key industry.
  • Saying Bengaluru is the cotton textile capital. It is the electronic capital of India.
  • Forgetting thermal pollution when asked to list the types of industrial pollution.

SHORTS12 marks

1. Why is the jute industry concentrated in the Hooghly basin?

Show Answer

Ans:

The Hooghly basin grows raw jute in its delta, has cheap labour from the neighbouring states of Bihar, Odisha and Uttar Pradesh, a humid climate suitable for spinning, plenty of water for retting and washing, Kolkata port for export, and the Hooghly river for transport.

SHORTS23 marks

2. Why is the iron and steel industry called a basic industry? Give three reasons.

Show Answer

Ans:

It is called a basic industry because all other industries — heavy, medium and light — depend on it for their machinery, and because production and consumption of steel are regarded as the index of a country's level of development. It is also a heavy industry, since both its raw materials and its finished products are heavy and bulky, so transport costs are a major part of its total cost.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Why has the sugar industry shifted to southern and western states, especially Maharashtra?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because the sugarcane grown there has a higher sucrose content, the cooler climate ensures a longer crushing season, and the cooperatives in these states are more successful, so the mills get a better and more regular supply of cane than in the older north Indian centres.

SHORTS43 marks

4. How has the information technology industry contributed to India's economy?

Show Answer

Ans:

It has generated large employment, especially for women, and has been a major earner of foreign exchange through the rapidly growing business process outsourcing (BPO) sector. Software technology parks provide single-window services and high data communication facilities, and Bengaluru has become the electronic capital of India. Continued growth in hardware and software depends on a skilled workforce and good connectivity.

SHORTS73 marks

7. Name the different types of industrial pollution and suggest two ways of reducing air pollution.

Show Answer

Ans:

Types: air pollution, water pollution, thermal pollution, land pollution and noise pollution.
Air pollution can be reduced by fitting electrostatic precipitators, scrubbers and filters in chimneys to remove particulates and gases.
Other measures: using cleaner fuels such as CNG and low-sulphur coal, building tall chimneys so pollutants disperse, and keeping industries away from residential areas.

LONGL104 marks

10. Explain why the cotton textile industry shifted from Maharashtra to Gujarat and Tamil Nadu.

Show Answer

Ans:

Gujarat has the nearby cotton belt, a humid coastal climate suitable for weaving, cheap land and power, a tradition of trade and enterprise, and good port facilities.
Ahmedabad grew as the ‘Manchester of India' on the basis of the spinning and weaving mills set up by Gujarati entrepreneurs.
In Tamil Nadu, cotton comes from the black soil region, cheap power is available from hydroelectricity, and labour is plentiful; the Coimbatore–Madurai belt has integrated ginning, spinning and weaving.
The Mumbai mills, meanwhile, faced high land costs, old machinery, power shortages and militant trade unionism.

LONGL115 marks

11. Describe the factors responsible for the location of the jute textile industry along the banks of the Hooghly.

Show Answer

Ans:

Proximity of the jute-producing areas — the jute belt of West Bengal, Bihar, Odisha and Assam lies close by.
Inexpensive water transport, supported by a good network of railways, roadways and waterways for the movement of jute to the mills.
Abundant water for processing raw jute, which the Hooghly supplies.
Cheap labour from West Bengal and the adjoining states of Bihar, Odisha and Uttar Pradesh.
Kolkata as a large urban centre provides banking, insurance, port facilities for export and other commercial services.
As a result India is the largest producer of raw jute and jute goods in the world, though it faces competition from synthetic substitutes and from other producers such as Bangladesh.

LONGL125 marks

12. What are the different types of industrial pollution? How can it be controlled?

Show Answer

Ans:

Types: (1) Air pollution — caused by chemical and paper factories, brick kilns, refineries and smelting plants burning fossil fuels, and by toxic gas leaks such as the Bhopal tragedy; (2) Water pollution — caused by organic and inorganic industrial wastes and effluents discharged into rivers by paper, pulp, chemical, textile and dyeing, petroleum refineries and tanneries; (3) Thermal pollution — hot water from factories and power plants drained into rivers and ponds before cooling; (4) Noise pollution — from industrial and construction activity, machinery and generators, causing hearing impairment and stress.
Control: particulate matter can be controlled by fitting electrostatic precipitators, fabric filters, scrubbers and inertial separators to smokestacks; smoke can be reduced by using oil instead of coal; water pollution can be minimised by reusing and recycling water in two or more successive stages, harvesting rainwater, and treating hot water and effluents before releasing them, in three stages — primary (mechanical), secondary (biological) and tertiary (chemical and physical); noise can be reduced by fitting silencers on generators and machinery, redesigning machines and providing workers with earplugs.
