Ch 1 · Internet Basics (10)

SHORTS12 marks

1. What is the internet? Give two of its features.

Show Answer

Ans:

The internet is a worldwide network of networks in which millions of smaller networks are interconnected, allowing any computer to communicate with any other. Two of its features are: it has no single owner or central control, running instead on shared protocols; and it provides many services at once — the World Wide Web, email, file transfer and video calls all travel over the same infrastructure.

SHORTS22 marks

2. What was ARPANET and why is it important?

Show Answer

Ans:

ARPANET (Advanced Research Projects Agency Network) was started in 1969 by the US Department of Defense's research agency. It is important because it was the first packet-switched network and the direct ancestor of today's internet — its first connection ran between UCLA and Stanford Research Institute.

SHORTS32 marks

3. What is the role of DNS?

Show Answer

Ans:

DNS (Domain Name System) translates human-readable domain names such as clickalex.github.io into the numeric IP addresses that computers actually use to find each other. Without it, users would have to memorise numbers like 140.82.121.4 to reach any website. DNS works like a distributed phone book for the internet.

SHORTS43 marks

4. Distinguish between IPv4 and IPv6.

Show Answer

Ans:

IPv4 uses 32-bit addresses, giving roughly 4.3 billion possible addresses — written as four numbers separated by dots, such as 192.168.1.5. IPv6 uses 128-bit addresses, written in hexadecimal groups such as 2001:0db8:85a3::8a2e:0370:7334. IPv6 was created because the supply of IPv4 addresses was exhausted by the growth in connected devices; it also has better built-in support for security and automatic configuration.

India got internet access on 15 August 1995, through VSNL (Videsh Sanchar Nigam Limited).

Ch 2 · Internet Services & Protocols (10)

SHORTS12 marks

1. What is the difference between HTTP and HTTPS?

Show Answer

Ans:

HTTP (HyperText Transfer Protocol) is the protocol used to fetch web pages, and it sends everything in plain text on port 80. HTTPS is the same protocol wrapped in encryption using TLS/SSL, on port 443. The practical difference is that with HTTPS a third party watching the network cannot read or alter the data — which is why banking and login pages always use it.

SHORTS22 marks

2. Explain the difference between POP3 and IMAP.

Show Answer

Ans:

Both protocols receive email, but they handle storage differently. POP3 downloads messages to your device and normally deletes them from the server, so the mail is only on that one machine. IMAP leaves messages on the server and synchronises, so the same mailbox looks identical on a phone, a laptop and a web browser. IMAP needs a constant connection and server space; POP3 works offline once downloaded.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Break down the parts of this URL: https://clickalex.github.io/IT-402-/index.html.

Show Answer

Ans:

The URL has three parts. https:// is the protocol — it says the page will be fetched securely over HTTPS on port 443. clickalex.github.io is the domain name, which DNS resolves into an IP address. /IT-402-/index.html is the path, pointing to a specific file inside the IT-402- folder on that server.

SHORTS42 marks

4. What is the difference between TCP and UDP?

Show Answer

Ans:

TCP (Transmission Control Protocol) is connection-oriented: it checks that every packet arrived and reorders them, so delivery is guaranteed but slightly slower. UDP (User Datagram Protocol) simply sends packets without checking, so it is faster but some data may be lost. TCP is used for web pages and email, where a missing piece breaks the result; UDP is used for live video, VoIP and gaming, where a small loss is better than a delay.

SHORTS51 mark

5. Give the full form of FTP and its default port.

Show Answer

Ans:

FTP stands for File Transfer Protocol, and its default port is 21. It is used to upload and download files between a client and a server.

Ch 3 · Network Types, Topologies & Devices (10)

SHORTS13 marks

1. Explain star topology with its advantages and disadvantages.

Show Answer

Ans:

In star topology every node is connected by its own cable to a central device — a hub or a switch. All communication passes through that centre. Its advantages are that a cable failure affects only the one node attached to it, nodes can be added or removed without disturbing the rest, and faults are easy to locate. Its disadvantages are that it needs more cable than a bus, and the central device is a single point of failure — if the switch dies, the whole network stops.

SHORTS23 marks

2. Differentiate between a hub, a switch and a router.

Show Answer

Ans:

A hub works at the physical layer and repeats an incoming signal out of every port, so all devices see all traffic and collisions are common. A switch works at the data-link layer, learns the MAC address on each port and forwards a frame only to the intended port, which makes it far faster and more secure. A router works at the network layer and connects different networks, using IP addresses to choose the best path between them — it is what links your LAN to the internet.

SHORTS32 marks

3. Why is optical fibre preferred over copper cable for long distances?

Show Answer

Ans:

Optical fibre carries data as pulses of light through glass, so it suffers almost no signal loss over long distances and is immune to electrical interference from motors, power lines and lightning. It also has a far higher bandwidth than copper. Its drawbacks are that it is more expensive and more fragile to install, which is why copper still dominates the last few metres.

SHORTS42 marks

4. What is the difference between a MAC address and an IP address?

Show Answer

Ans:

A MAC address is a 48-bit hardware address burnt into the network card; it never changes and identifies the physical device. An IP address is assigned logically for the network the device is on, so it changes when the device moves to a different network. MAC works within one local network, while IP works across the whole internet.

SHORTS51 mark

5. Which IEEE standard defines Wi-Fi?

Show Answer

Ans:

IEEE 802.11 defines Wi-Fi. The IEEE 802.3 standard, by contrast, defines wired Ethernet.

LONGL65 marks

6. Discuss “Network Types, Topologies & Devices” in detail. Include the idea that carries marks, one worked example or answer frame, and why students lose marks.

Networks by size. A LAN (Local Area Network) covers a room or a building — a school lab. A MAN (Metropolitan Area Network) covers a city — a cable TV network. A WAN (Wide Area Network) covers countries or the world; the internet is the largest WAN. A PAN (Personal Area Network) is the smallest, joining devices around one person, usually by Bluetooth.

Ch 4 · Mobile & Wireless Technology (9)

SHORTS13 marks

1. Compare 3G, 4G and 5G.

Show Answer

Ans:

3G made mobile internet practical, offering speeds up to about 2 Mbps, which allowed video calling and basic browsing. 4G (LTE) is an all-IP network designed around data rather than voice, reaching around 100 Mbps and making HD video streaming and VoIP everyday features. 5G targets up to 20 Gbps with latency below one millisecond; its real significance is not faster phones but support for massive numbers of connected devices — IoT sensors, autonomous vehicles and remote medical procedures.

SHORTS22 marks

2. What is the difference between Bluetooth and NFC?

Show Answer

Ans:

Bluetooth works over a range of a few metres and is used for continuous connections such as headphones, speakers and keyboards. NFC (Near Field Communication) works only within a few centimetres and is used for a single quick exchange — contactless payments, transit cards, pairing by tapping. Bluetooth needs pairing; NFC works by simply bringing two devices together.

SHORTS32 marks

3. What is a cell and what is handover?

Show Answer

Ans:

A cell is the geographic area covered by one mobile tower. A whole city is divided into many cells, which is why mobile networks are called cellular. Handover is the automatic process of moving an ongoing call or data session from one cell's tower to the next as the user travels, so the connection does not drop.

SHORTS42 marks

4. Why does infrared need line of sight but Wi-Fi does not?

Show Answer

Ans:

Infrared uses light waves, which cannot pass through solid objects, so the transmitter and receiver must see each other directly — that is why a TV remote only works when pointed at the TV. Wi-Fi uses radio waves, which pass through walls and furniture, so it works throughout a building without a direct line of sight.

Ch 5 · Web Browsers, Search & Web Concepts (10)

SHORTS12 marks

1. Differentiate between a web browser and a search engine.

Show Answer

Ans:

A web browser is software installed on your computer or phone — Chrome, Firefox, Edge or Safari — that sends requests for pages and renders the HTML it receives into the page you see. A search engine such as Google is itself a website whose job is to index other websites and return a ranked list of results for your query. You need a browser in order to open a search engine, so they work together rather than as alternatives.

SHORTS22 marks

2. What are cookies and why are they used?

Show Answer

Ans:

A cookie is a small piece of data that a website stores in the user's browser. Websites use cookies to remember something about the user between visits — whether they are logged in, which language they chose, or what is in their shopping cart. Without cookies, every page request would be treated as a brand-new visitor. Because cookies can also be used to track browsing behaviour, browsers offer private modes that discard them when the window closes.

SHORTS32 marks

3. What is caching and what is its benefit?

Show Answer

Ans:

Caching means keeping a local copy of a resource — a page, an image or a stylesheet — so that it does not have to be downloaded again. The benefits are that pages load faster and use less bandwidth, which matters most on slow or metered connections. The trade-off is that a cached copy can be out of date, which is why a forced refresh sometimes shows a different page.

SHORTS42 marks

4. How does a search engine find and rank pages?

Show Answer

Ans:

A search engine uses a crawler — also called a spider — that automatically visits web pages and follows the links on them to discover more. What it finds is stored in an index, a huge searchable catalogue. When you type a query, a ranking algorithm scores the matching pages by relevance, authority and quality, and returns them in order.

Google Chrome, Mozilla Firefox and Microsoft Edge. Apple's Safari and Opera are other common examples.

Ch 6 · HTML I — Introduction & Basic Tags (10)

SHORTS13 marks

1. Write an HTML page that displays the heading 'My School' and a paragraph about it.

Show Answer

Ans:

<html>
<head>
<title>My School</title>
</head>
<body>
<h1>My School</h1>
<p>My school is located in the centre of the city and has a large playground, a library and a computer laboratory.</p>
</body>
</html>
The <title> sits inside <head> and names the browser tab; everything visible is inside <body>.

SHORTS22 marks

2. What is the difference between a tag and an element?

Show Answer

Ans:

A tag is the marker itself, written in angle brackets — for example <p> or </p>. An element is the complete unit made of the opening tag, the content and the closing tag — <p>This is a paragraph.</p>. So tags are the parts and the element is the whole thing they enclose.

Ans:

SHORTS32 marks

3. What is the difference between <frame> and <iframe>?

Show Answer

Ans:

A <frame> can only be used inside a <frameset>, which replaces the document's <body> and splits the entire browser window into separate regions. An <iframe> (inline frame) is placed inside an ordinary page and embeds another document in a rectangular area within it, leaving the rest of the page untouched. In practice iframes are still common for maps and embedded videos, while framesets are considered outdated.

Show Answer

The <marquee> tag makes its content scroll across the page. Two of its attributes are direction, which sets the direction of movement — left, right, up or down — and scrollamount, which sets how fast it moves. A third, behavior, chooses between scroll (continuous), slide (moves once and stops) and alternate (bounces back and forth).

The <pre> tag displays text exactly as it is typed, preserving all spaces, tabs and line breaks. Ordinary HTML collapses multiple spaces into one and ignores line breaks, so <pre> is used whenever the formatting itself matters — for code samples, ASCII diagrams or poetry.

Ch 12 · Cyber Safety & Security (10)

SHORTS12 marks

1. What is the difference between cyber safety and cyber security?

Show Answer

Ans:

Cyber safety is about protecting an individual user online — using strong passwords, avoiding suspicious links, not oversharing personal information. Cyber security is the broader technical discipline of protecting computers, networks, systems and data from unauthorised access and attack, using firewalls, encryption, access control and monitoring. Safety is personal practice; security is systematic defence.

SHORTS23 marks

2. Differentiate between a virus, a worm and a trojan horse.

Show Answer

Ans:

A virus is malicious code that attaches itself to a legitimate file and spreads only when that infected file is opened or shared, so it needs a human action to move. A worm is a standalone program that copies itself across a network automatically, needing no host file and no user action, which is why worms spread so fast. A trojan horse does not spread at all — it disguises itself as a useful or attractive program so the user installs it willingly, and only then does its hidden payload run.

SHORTS32 marks

3. What is phishing? How can it be avoided?

Show Answer

Ans:

Phishing is an attack in which a fake email or website imitates a trusted organisation — a bank, a school or a popular service — and tricks the user into entering passwords, card numbers or other sensitive data. It can be avoided by checking the sender's actual address rather than the display name, never clicking links in unsolicited messages, typing the website address yourself instead, looking for HTTPS before entering credentials, and enabling two-factor authentication so a stolen password alone is not enough.

SHORTS43 marks

4. List five safety measures for using the internet safely.

Show Answer

Ans:

  • Use a strong, unique password for each account and never reuse one; a password manager helps.
  • Enable two-factor authentication so a stolen password is not enough on its own.
  • Keep the operating system, browser and antivirus software updated, since updates close known security holes.
  • Never open attachments or follow links from unknown senders, and verify the address of any site asking for payment.
  • Back up important files regularly, and avoid sharing personal details, photographs or location publicly.
SHORTS51 mark

5. What is ransomware?

Show Answer

Ans:

Ransomware is a type of malware that encrypts the victim's files so they cannot be opened, and then demands payment — usually in cryptocurrency — in exchange for the decryption key. Regular offline backups are the main defence, because they let the victim restore the data without paying.

SHORTS13 marks

1. What is IPR? Name and explain its four main types.

Show Answer

Ans:

IPR (Intellectual Property Rights) are the legal rights granted to the creator of an original work, allowing them to control and benefit from its use and stopping others from copying it without permission. The four main types are copyright, which protects original literary, artistic, musical and software works; patent, which protects an invention or process for a fixed period; trademark, which protects a brand name, logo or slogan; and trade secret, which protects confidential business information such as a formula or a method.

SHORTS22 marks

2. What is plagiarism and how can it be avoided?

Show Answer

Ans:

Plagiarism is presenting someone else's words, ideas, data or code as if they were your own, without giving credit. It is an ethical offence even when no money changes hands. It is avoided by always citing the source, using quotation marks around copied text, paraphrasing genuinely rather than swapping a few words, and keeping a record of every source consulted while working.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Differentiate between free and open source software and proprietary software, with examples.

Show Answer

Ans:

FOSS makes the source code available, so anyone may use, study, modify and redistribute the program; examples are Linux, Firefox and LibreOffice. Here 'free' refers to freedom rather than price. Proprietary software keeps its source code secret and grants only a licence to use it, forbidding copying, modification or redistribution; examples are Microsoft Windows and Adobe Photoshop. The practical difference is that with FOSS you can fix or extend the software yourself, while with proprietary software you depend entirely on the vendor.

SHORTS42 marks

4. What is software piracy?

Show Answer

Ans:

Software piracy is the unauthorised copying, distribution or use of software without a valid licence. It includes installing one licensed copy on many machines, downloading cracked versions, and selling counterfeit discs. It deprives developers of income and often exposes the user to malware, since pirated copies are frequently bundled with it.

SHORTS52 marks

5. Distinguish between freeware and shareware.

Show Answer

Ans:

Freeware can be used free of charge indefinitely, but its source code is usually closed and it cannot be modified or sold — an example is Skype. Shareware is distributed free only as a trial, either for a limited period or with reduced features, and the user must pay to continue using it after the trial ends.

Ch 14 · The IT Act, Digital Footprint & e-Waste (10)

SHORTS13 marks

1. What is the IT Act, 2000? State any three of its key provisions.

Show Answer

Ans:

The Information Technology Act, 2000 is India's principal legislation governing electronic commerce and cybercrime, amended in 2008. Three key provisions are: it gives legal recognition to electronic records and digital signatures, so an online document or contract is as valid as a paper one; it defines cyber offences and their punishments, such as tampering with source documents under Section 65 and hacking under Section 66; and it provides a legal framework for electronic governance and secure electronic transactions, including penalties for identity theft under Section 66C and for publishing obscene material under Section 67.

SHORTS23 marks

2. What is a digital footprint? Explain its two types.

Show Answer

Ans:

A digital footprint is the trail of data a person leaves behind while using the internet — accounts, posts, comments, photographs, search history and the metadata attached to them. It has two types. An active footprint is created deliberately, when you post on social media, fill in a form or upload a photograph. A passive footprint is collected without your direct action, such as your IP address, approximate location, browsing history and cookies. Both matter because the internet rarely forgets — content can be copied and screenshotted, and colleges and employers increasingly check what is publicly visible.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Why is e-waste hazardous and how should it be managed?

Show Answer

Ans:

e-Waste is hazardous because discarded electronics contain toxic substances such as lead, mercury, cadmium and brominated flame retardants. When dumped in ordinary landfills these chemicals leach into soil and groundwater, and burning the waste releases poisonous fumes that harm both workers and the surrounding population. At the same time e-waste contains recoverable gold, silver and copper, so throwing it away wastes valuable material. It should be managed through the 3R approach — reduce, reuse and recycle — by repairing and passing on working devices where possible and handing the rest to authorised e-waste recyclers or collection centres rather than to regular waste collection.

SHORTS42 marks

4. What is meant by digital citizenship?

Show Answer

Ans:

Digital citizenship is the responsible, respectful and safe use of technology. A good digital citizen protects their own and other people's privacy, respects intellectual property by not copying or pirating, communicates politely without abusing or bullying others, checks facts before sharing information, and reports harmful content instead of spreading it.
