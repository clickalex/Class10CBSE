Chapter 12 · Netiquette, E-Commerce & Digital Divide

Chapter 12 of 13. Unit III is about responsible behaviour: how to act, how to transact, and who the internet still leaves out.

Open this chapter’s questions + MCQs →

🎯 Marks lens 📖 Deep concepts 🧮 Formulas 🛠️ Steps 🧠 Tricks ⚠️ Mistakes ❓ Exam Q&A ✍️ Task

🎯 Marks lens

List netiquette rules; explain e-commerce privacy, fraud and secure transmission; define the digital divide and freedom of information.
Unit III is only 10 marks of short answers — learn each idea as a crisp definition plus two examples.

📖 Deep concepts

1. Netiquette. Netiquette (network + etiquette) is the code of good manners for the internet: write polite messages, respect other people’s time and privacy, do not type in ALL CAPS (it reads as shouting), do not forward rumours or spam, and give credit instead of copying. Schools test it as a short list of do’s and don’ts.
2. E-commerce. E-commerce means buying, selling, banking and paying over the internet — e-shopping, e-banking, e-reservation, online bill payment. It is fast and available round the clock, but every transaction moves money and personal data, so privacy and fraud are the two big risks.
3. Privacy and fraud. Privacy means your personal data (name, address, card number, OTP) must stay with the parties you trust — never share passwords or OTPs, and check what an app collects before installing it. Fraud is cheating online: fake shops that take payment and never deliver, phishing messages that steal passwords, and lottery/prize scams. The defence is simple — shop on known sites, look for https://, and never pay in advance to strangers.
4. Secure data transmission. Data is protected in transit by encryption (scrambling it so only the receiver can read it), by https:// (HTTP over a secure channel — the padlock in the browser), by OTPs (one-time passwords that prove it is really you), and by firewalls and updated software on both ends. http:// without the s sends everything as readable text.
5. Freedom of information. Freedom of information is the principle that citizens have the right to access information — governments publish records, results and services online (e-governance) so that people can see, question and use them. It sits opposite censorship: an open internet lets ideas and facts flow freely.
6. Digital divide. The digital divide is the gap between people who have access to computers, smartphones and the internet — and those who do not, because of poverty, remote villages, age, language or disability. Consequences: missed schooling (online classes), missed services (banking, certificates) and missed opportunities. It is bridged by cheap devices, mobile networks in villages, public Wi-Fi and services in regional languages.

🧮 Formulas to memorise

  • Netiquette: polite words, no CAPS, no spam, respect privacy, give credit
  • E-commerce risks: privacy (guard OTPs/passwords) + fraud (fake shops, phishing)
  • Secure transmission: encryption + https:// padlock + OTP
  • Digital divide = the connected vs the unconnected: devices, coverage, skills, language

🛠️ Method, step by step

1. Define the term in one line (netiquette / e-commerce / digital divide).
2. Add two examples or two precautions — that is where the marks sit.
3. For fraud questions: name the trick, then the defence.
4. For divide questions: name the barrier, then the bridge.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Netiquette = manners with a modem. Polite words, no CAPS, no spam, give credit.
  • Shop SAFE: Secure site, Avoid advance payment, Fraud-check the seller, Exit if it smells wrong.
  • https = HTTP + Security. No s, no shopping.
  • Divide = the offline gap. Access + skills + language decide who is in.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Calling any online cheating “hacking” — shopping fraud, phishing and fake shops each have their own name.
  • Saying http is safe — only https encrypts the data in transit.
  • Defining the digital divide as only “no internet” — devices, skills, language and cost count too.
  • Sharing an OTP “even with the bank” — no genuine caller ever asks for it.

SHORTS12 marks

1. What is netiquette? List any four netiquette rules.

Show Answer

Ans:

Netiquette (network + etiquette) is the code of polite, responsible behaviour on the internet. Four rules: (1) write courteous messages and respect others’ privacy; (2) do not type in ALL CAPS — it reads as shouting; (3) do not forward rumours, chain messages or spam; (4) give credit for others’ work instead of copying it.

SHORTS22 marks

2. What is e-commerce? Give two examples from the syllabus.

Show Answer

Ans:

E-commerce means carrying out trade — buying, selling, paying and banking — over the internet. Two syllabus examples: e-shopping (ordering goods on a website) and e-banking (transferring money or paying bills through a bank’s site or app). e-reservation and online bill payment are two more.

SHORTS32 marks

3. Why is privacy the biggest concern in e-commerce? State two precautions a buyer should take.

Show Answer

Ans:

Every e-commerce transaction moves money plus personal data (name, address, card number, OTP), which thieves can misuse. Two precautions: (1) shop only on known, reputed sites showing https:// and the padlock; (2) never share passwords or OTPs with anyone, and check what data an app collects before installing it.

SHORTS42 marks

4. What is the digital divide?

Show Answer

Ans:

The digital divide is the gap between people who have access to computers, smartphones and the internet — and those who do not. It is caused by poverty, remote villages with no network, lack of digital skills, age, disability and language barriers, and it locks the excluded out of online schooling, banking and services.

SHORTS53 marks

5. Explain online fraud in e-commerce with two examples. How can a buyer avoid each?

Show Answer

Ans:

Online fraud is cheating a buyer or seller over the internet. Example 1: fake shops that take payment and never deliver — avoid by buying only from known sites and checking reviews. Example 2: phishing messages that pose as a bank and steal passwords — avoid by never clicking such links and never sharing OTPs.

SHORTS63 marks

6. How is data kept secure during transmission? Explain any three methods.

Show Answer

Ans:

Three methods: (1) Encryption — scrambling the data so only the intended receiver can read it. (2) https:// — HTTP over a secure channel (the browser padlock); plain http sends everything as readable text. (3) OTPs — one-time passwords sent to the buyer’s phone that prove the transaction is really theirs.

SHORTS73 marks

7. What is freedom of information? How does e-governance support it?

Show Answer

Ans:

Freedom of information is the principle that citizens have the right to access information held by public authorities. E-governance supports it by putting records, results, forms and services online — certificates, exam results, land records — so people can see, question and use them without middlemen or secrecy.

SHORTS83 marks

8. State three consequences of the digital divide and three ways to bridge it.

Show Answer

Ans:

Consequences: students miss online classes, citizens miss digital banking and certificates, and job-seekers miss online opportunities. Bridging it: (1) cheap smartphones and low-cost data; (2) mobile networks and public Wi-Fi in villages; (3) services and training in regional languages so language is no barrier.

APPLICATIONA93 marks

9. A friend forwards every “forward to 10 people” message, types angry replies in CAPS, and posts a classmate’s photo without asking. Which three netiquette rules is being broken, and what should be done instead?

Show Answer

Ans:

Chain forwards break the no spam/rumours rule — verify before forwarding, or delete.
ALL-CAPS replies break the no shouting rule — use normal case and polite words.
Posting the photo breaks respect for privacy — always take permission before sharing someone’s picture or number.

COMPETENCYC104 marks

10. Read and answer: Meera finds a phone 40% cheaper on an unknown site. The site uses http://, asks for full payment by bank transfer, and a “seller” on chat asks for her OTP to “confirm the order”. (a) Name three fraud/danger signals. (b) What should Meera do?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) An unknown site with an unbelievable discount; http:// with no padlock, so data travels unencrypted; and advance bank transfer plus an OTP demand — no genuine seller ever asks for an OTP.
(b) Close the site, buy only from a reputed https:// shop, never pay strangers in advance, and never share the OTP with anyone.

COMPETENCYC114 marks

11. Read and answer: A village gets a mobile tower but students still cannot attend online classes: families share one old phone, the signal is weak indoors, and the learning app is English-only. (a) Which problem is this an example of? (b) Identify three barriers shown here. (c) Suggest one fix for each.

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) This is the digital divide — access on paper, exclusion in practice.
(b) Shared/old devices, weak connectivity, and a language barrier.
(c) Device: a shared tablet corner at school; connectivity: classes or downloads from a spot with signal; language: apps and help in the regional language.

LONGL125 marks

12. Responsible use of the internet needs good manners, safe transactions and access for all. Discuss netiquette, e-commerce safety and the digital divide.

Show Answer

Ans:

Netiquette — polite messages, no CAPS shouting, no spam or rumours, respect privacy, give credit.
E-commerce safety — shop on reputed https:// sites, guard privacy (no OTP/password sharing), recognise fraud (fake shops, phishing, prize scams), and rely on secure transmission (encryption, OTPs).
Digital divide — the gap between the connected and the unconnected, caused by cost, coverage, skills and language; bridged by cheap devices, village networks, public Wi-Fi and regional-language services.
Together the three ideas make one point: use the net politely, transact on it safely, and carry everyone along.

✍️ Hands-on task

Write a 6-line “safe shopping checklist” for a grandparent: https, known shop, no OTP sharing, no advance to strangers, check reviews, keep receipts.
