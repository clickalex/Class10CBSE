English Language & Literature — Quick revision

One screen per chapter: formulas and the points that actually get asked. Expand what you need, skip what you know.

Ch 1 A Letter to God
  • Lencho: simple farmer, hailstorm destroys the corn, writes to God for 100 pesos.
  • Postmaster collects 70 pesos, signs the letter 'God'.
  • Lencho is not surprised by the letter, only angry at the shortfall.
  • Two ironies: the rain that destroyed, and the helpers called crooks.
Ch 2 Nelson Mandela: Long Walk to Freedom
  • 10 May 1994, Union Buildings, Pretoria; three anthems; end of apartheid.
  • Courage = triumph over fear.
  • Twin obligations: family and people; apartheid made both impossible.
  • Freedom is indivisible; freedom means living in a way that respects and enhances the freedom of others.
Ch 3 Two Stories about Flying
  • His First Flight: O'Flaherty; 24 hours on the ledge; hunger and the sight of fish break the fear.
  • The Black Aeroplane: Forsyth; storm over France; instruments fail; a black plane with no lights leads him out.
  • The control centre says no other plane was in the sky.
  • Common theme: fear is conquered only by taking the leap.
Ch 4 From the Diary of Anne Frank
  • Anne, 13, Amsterdam; family goes into hiding in July 1942; grandmother died January 1942.
  • No true friend, so the diary 'Kitty' replaces one; 'paper has more patience than people'.
  • Three essays: A Chatterbox → An Incorrigible Chatterbox → Quack, Quack, Quack, Said Mistress Chatterbox.
  • Mr Keesing becomes 'the nicest teacher' after reading the poem.
Ch 5 Glimpses of India
  • Goa: pader, kabai, jang-jhang-jhang staff, bol for marriage, bolinhas for Christmas; bakers look fat and jolly.
  • Coorg: smallest district of Karnataka, coffee fragrance, martial men, firearms without licence, Kuppia coat, Kaveri origin, Brahmagiri hills, Bylakutte monastery.
  • Assam: eighty crore cups daily, Chinese legend 2700 BC, Bodhidharma, finest tea from the second flush (May–July).
Ch 6 Mijbil the Otter
  • 1956, Basra; two Arabs bring the otter; named Mijbil; species named after Maxwell.
  • Loves water, marbles, ping-pong balls and rubber fruit; develops compulsive daily rituals.
  • Escapes on the plane, tears the lining, returns to nuzzle Maxwell.
  • Londoners guess seal, squirrel, walrus, hippo, beaver, bear cub, leopard, brontosaurus.
Ch 7 Madam Rides the Bus
  • Valli, eight years and some months; watches the bus from her front doorway.
  • Six miles, thirty paise each way, forty-five minutes; she saves by refusing all treats.
  • Travels alone on the one o'clock bus; the conductor calls her 'madam'.
  • The cow alive on the way out, dead on the way back — her first meeting with death.
  • She tells no one; her mother thinks she was asleep.
Ch 8 The Sermon at Benares
  • Siddhartha Gautama; four sights at 25; enlightenment under the Bo tree; first sermon at Sarnath near Benares.
  • Kisa Gotami: mustard seed from a house where no one had died — impossible, so she learns death is universal.
  • 'Draw out the arrow of lamentation, complaint and grief.'
  • Similes: the ripe fruit that may fall; the potter's vessels that end broken.
Ch 9 The Proposal
  • A one-act play by Anton Chekhov; three characters: Chubukov, Lomov (35) and Natalya (25).
  • Quarrel 1: the Oxen Meadows. Quarrel 2: Squeezer versus Guess.
  • The proposal is agreed in the middle of an argument; love is never mentioned.
  • Lomov is a hypochondriac; Chubukov is short-tempered and money-minded; Natalya is quarrelsome.
  • Satire on the landowning class, for whom property matters more than feeling.
Ch 10 Dust of Snow
  • Robert Frost, eight lines, rhyme abab cdcd.
  • A crow shakes dust of snow from a hemlock tree onto the poet.
  • It changes his mood and saves part of the day he had rued.
  • Crow and hemlock are symbols of sorrow, yet they bring joy.
Ch 11 Fire and Ice
  • Robert Frost, nine lines, rhyme aba abc bcb.
  • Fire = desire; ice = hatred.
  • The poet leans toward fire but says ice would also suffice.
  • Central idea: both desire and hate can destroy the world.
Ch 12 A Tiger in the Zoo
  • Leslie Norris; two settings, cage and wild.
  • Cage: few steps, concrete cell, bars, ignores visitors, stares at the brilliant stars.
  • Wild: shadow, long grass, watering hole, plump deer, snarling around houses.
  • 'Quiet rage' = oxymoron; 'brilliant' repeated to link the tiger and the stars.
Ch 13 How to Tell Wild Animals
  • Carolyn Wells, rhyme ababcc, light verse.
  • Asian lion roars; Bengal tiger has black stripes; leopard has spots and leaps.
  • The bear hugs; the crocodile weeps; the hyena smiles; the chameleon has no wings or ears.
  • The humour: identification is only certain when the animal attacks you.
Ch 14 The Ball Poem
  • John Berryman, free verse.
  • The boy loses his ball in the harbour; a dime could buy another.
  • The poet does not intrude, because the boy must learn loss himself.
  • 'Money is external'; 'balls will be lost always'.
  • The first loss is the beginning of growing up.
Ch 15 Amanda!
  • Robin Klein; two voices — instructions outside, daydreams in brackets.
  • Three fantasies: orphan roaming the street, Rapunzel in her tower, mermaid in the languid emerald sea.
  • Each fantasy is an escape from control and a wish for silence and freedom.
  • The final accusation of sulking shows the adult has not understood.
Ch 16 The Trees
  • Adrienne Rich, free verse.
  • The trees are inside the house; the forest outside is empty — no birds, no insects, no shade.
  • Roots disengage from the veranda floor; leaves strain towards the glass; boughs shuffle like discharged patients.
  • The moon breaks like a mirror; its pieces flash in the tallest oak.
  • Message: nature has been imprisoned by human beings, but it is moving back to the forest.
Ch 17 Fog
  • Carl Sandburg, six lines, free verse.
  • Fog comes on little cat feet, sits on silent haunches, then moves on.
  • Metaphor, not simile — no 'like' or 'as'.
  • Central idea: the quiet, mysterious, unhurried movement of fog.
Ch 18 The Tale of Custard the Dragon
  • Ogden Nash, ballad form, rhyme aabb.
  • Belinda, Ink (black kitten), Blink (grey mouse), Mustard (yellow dog), Custard (dragon).
  • All boast; Custard alone asks for a nice safe cage.
  • The pirate arrives: Mustard flees, Ink trickles, Blink hides; Custard eats the pirate.
  • Moral: bravery is shown in deeds, not in words — and a coward can still act bravely.
Ch 19 For Anne Gregory
  • W. B. Yeats; a dialogue between the poet and Anne Gregory.
  • 'Honey-coloured ramparts at your ear' = a metaphor for her yellow hair.
  • Anne offers to dye her hair brown, black or carrot to prove her point.
  • The poet quotes a religious text: only God can love you for yourself alone.
  • Theme: outer beauty against inner worth; true love is divine.
Ch 20 A Triumph of Surgery
  • James Herriot; Tricki the Pomeranian; Mrs Pumphrey overfeeds him.
  • The vet gives no medicine — water, fasting, then exercise and normal food.
  • Tricki recovers in a fortnight; Mrs Pumphrey calls it a triumph of surgery.
  • Theme: love without discipline harms; remove the cause rather than prescribe.
Ch 21 The Thief's Story
  • Ruskin Bond; the thief is 15 and calls himself Hari Singh, changing the name monthly.
  • Anil, a kind struggling writer, teaches him to write his name and sentences.
  • He steals ₹600, cannot board the train, and the notes get ruined in the rain.
  • He returns because Anil would grieve the loss of trust, not of money.
  • Anil knows, says nothing, and pays him regularly — a second chance.
Ch 22 The Midnight Visitor
  • Robert Arthur; Ausable the fat, unglamorous agent; Max the armed rival.
  • Ausable invents a balcony and a police call in seconds.
  • Max jumps backwards through the window and falls to his death.
  • The knock was a waiter with a drink — the comic reveal.
  • Theme: presence of mind beats weapons; appearances mislead.
Ch 23 A Question of Trust
  • Victor Canning; Horace Danby, a respectable locksmith who robbed once a year.
  • Target: Shotover Grange, about £15,000 of jewels.
  • A young woman in red pretends to be the lady of the house and gets him to open the safe.
  • He forgets his gloves and is arrested two days later on fingerprints.
  • Theme: honour among thieves does not exist.
Ch 24 Footprints without Feet
  • H. G. Wells; Griffin makes himself invisible with rare drugs.
  • He burns his landlord's house, robs stores, and flees to Iping.
  • At the inn: an empty room, a hopping hat, a living chair; he becomes headless.
  • Constable Jaffers is beaten by an invisible man and knocked unconscious.
  • Theme: brilliant science without conscience makes a criminal.
Ch 25 The Making of a Scientist
  • Richard Ebright, Reading, Pennsylvania; father died when he was in third grade.
  • His mother encouraged him, bought equipment and gave him The Travels of Monarch X.
  • He found the twelve gold spots produce a hormone for full development.
  • Teacher Weiherer taught him to compete to do the best job he could.
  • A scientist needs a first-rate mind, curiosity and the will to win for the right reasons.
Ch 26 The Necklace
  • Guy de Maupassant; Matilda Loisel, a clerk's wife, dreams of luxury.
  • The Ministry ball: 400 francs for a dress, a borrowed diamond necklace.
  • The necklace is lost; a replacement costs 40,000 francs; ten years to repay.
  • Matilda does heavy housework; her husband works evenings and copies documents.
  • Ending: 'mine was only paste' — the necklace was worth at most 500 francs.
Ch 27 Bholi
  • K. A. Abbas; Bholi (Sulekha), the numberdar's fourth daughter, disfigured by smallpox.
  • Neglected and mocked, she is sent to school after the tehsildar's suggestion.
  • Her kind teacher gives her confidence; she learns to read and speaks without a stammer.
  • Bishamber Nath demands ₹5,000 as dowry after seeing her face.
  • Bholi throws the marigold garland into the fire and refuses; she will teach at the school.
Ch 28 The Book That Saved the Earth
  • Claire Boiko; a play set in the twenty-fifth century, the Era of the Book.
  • Think-Tank: vain, boastful, stupid; Noodle: clever and modest.
  • The crew reads Mother Goose: silver bells and cockle shells read as metals and explosives.
  • Think-Tank panics, orders retreat, and flees to Alpha Centauri.
  • Ending: Mars and Earth become friends; Mars is renamed New Earth.
Ch 29 Reading: Unseen and Case-based Passages
  • Two passages: discursive 400–450 words; case-based 200–250 words with a chart.
  • All questions objective: inference, analysis, interpretation, evaluation, vocabulary.
  • Method: questions first, one fast read, locate the line, eliminate two options.
  • Ninety seconds maximum per question.
Ch 30 Note Making and Summarising
  • Title centred at the top; decimal numbering 1, 1.1, 1.1.1 with consistent indentation.
  • Headings are short phrases, not sentences; nothing from outside the passage.
  • Key to abbreviations at the end — a separate mark.
  • Summary: about 80 words, your own words, complete sentences, no opinion.
Ch 31 Grammar in Context
  • Tenses follow the time marker; since and for take perfect tenses.
  • Modals: can ability, may permission, must necessity, should advice.
  • The verb agrees with the subject, not the nearest noun; neither/either take the nearer subject.
  • Reported speech: shift tense, change pronoun, change time and place words, and restore statement word order in questions.
  • Determiners: some in affirmatives, any in negatives and questions; few with countable, little with uncountable.
Ch 32 Formal Letter Writing
  • Sender's address (no name) → date in full → receiver's designation and address → one-line subject → Sir/Madam → three-paragraph body → Yours faithfully → signature, name, designation.
  • Paragraph 1: purpose. Paragraph 2: details. Paragraph 3: what you want done.
  • 100–120 words, no contractions, no informal language.
  • Format marks are separate — do not lose them.
Ch 33 Analytical Paragraph
  • Open: what the data shows, the period and the units.
  • Middle: compare the two or three significant figures, using phrases of comparison and trend.
  • Close: state the trend or an inference.
  • 100–120 words, no opinion, no invented data.
  • Phrases: rose steadily, fell sharply, peaked in, accounted for, twice as much as.
