Ch 1 · A Letter to God (12)

SHORTS13 marks

1. Why did Lencho say the post office employees were a bunch of crooks?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because he had asked God for one hundred pesos and received only seventy. His faith was so absolute that he could not believe God had made a mistake or had sent less, so he concluded that the post office employees must have taken the remaining thirty pesos. The irony is that these were the very men who had collected the money for him out of charity.

SHORTS23 marks

2. What made the postmaster decide to help Lencho?

Show Answer

Ans:

He was moved by the farmer's unshakable faith in God. He did not want to shake that faith, so he collected money from his employees and friends, gave part of his own salary, and sent it to Lencho in a letter signed 'God'.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Was Lencho surprised to find a letter with money in it? What made him angry?

Show Answer

Ans:

No, he was not surprised at all — he had expected an answer from God, and his confidence in God was so complete that receiving a reply seemed natural. What made him angry was that the envelope contained only seventy pesos instead of the hundred he had asked for. He was certain that God could not have made a mistake, so he concluded that the post office employees had taken the rest.

SHORTS63 marks

6. Bring out the irony in the ending of ‘A Letter to God'.

Show Answer

Ans:

Lencho is certain that God sent him the money and cannot imagine that human beings could be so generous. He writes a second letter to complain and calls the post office employees ‘a bunch of crooks' because thirty pesos were missing. The irony is that the very people who helped him are the ones he distrusts: his faith in God survives, while his faith in human beings is destroyed.

APPLICATIONA73 marks

7. “The postmaster was a fat, jolly fellow.” How does his character contrast with Lencho's, and why does the contrast matter to the story?

Show Answer

Ans:

Lencho is intense, single-minded and absolute in his faith; the postmaster is warm, practical and humane — he helps without needing to be believed in.
The contrast creates the story's deepest irony: the believer cannot recognise human kindness, while the kind man asks for no recognition at all.
Without this contrast the ending would be merely sad; with it, it becomes a comment on faith that blinds as well as sustains.

LONGL104 marks

10. Describe Lencho's character and say what his faith tells us about him.

Show Answer

Ans:

Lencho is a poor but hard-working farmer who knows his field like a child and never stops hoping for a good crop.
His faith is complete and childlike: after the hailstorm destroys everything, he writes to God for a hundred pesos with the same confidence with which he sows his field.
The same faith makes him naive — he cannot imagine that the postmaster and his staff collected the money themselves.
The story uses this innocence to show both the strength of faith and the loneliness of the poor, who trust God more than the people around them.

LONGL115 marks

11. 'The story is built around two ironies.' Explain with reference to the text.

Show Answer

Ans:

The first irony is in the weather: Lencho's field needed rain, and rain did come — but it turned into a hailstorm that destroyed the entire crop. The very thing that promised a good harvest took it away.
The second irony is in the ending: the post office employees, moved by Lencho's faith, collected seventy pesos and sent them to him. Lencho, finding the amount short, wrote back accusing those same men of stealing the money and calling them 'a bunch of crooks'. The helpers become the accused thieves, and the man who never doubted God doubts the honesty of human beings.

Ch 2 · Nelson Mandela: Long Walk to Freedom (12)

SHORTS13 marks

1. What does courage mean to Mandela?

Show Answer

Ans:

For Mandela, courage is not the absence of fear but the triumph over it. The brave man is not the one who does not feel afraid, but the one who conquers that fear. He says he felt fear more times than he can remember, and it was this repeated conquest of fear that made him bold.

SHORTS23 marks

2. What 'twin obligations' does Mandela mention?

Show Answer

Ans:

The first obligation is to his family — his parents, his wife and children. The second is to his people, his community and his country. In a civil and humane society each person can fulfil both, but in South Africa under apartheid, a man like Mandela who tried to live as a human being was punished and isolated, and could not discharge either obligation.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Why did such a large number of international leaders attend the inauguration? What did it signify?

Show Answer

Ans:

It signified the recognition by the international community of the birth of a free, democratic, non-racial South Africa, and a common victory for justice, for peace and for human dignity. The presence of leaders of many nations showed that the end of apartheid was not only a South African event but a triumph of values shared by the whole world.

SHORTS43 marks

4. Why does Mandela say that the oppressor is also not free?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because the oppressor is a prisoner of hatred, locked behind the bars of prejudice and narrow-mindedness. A man who takes away another man's freedom is himself deprived of his own humanity, so the oppressor and the oppressed are both robbed of what makes them human.

Show Answer

LONGL115 marks

11. How did Mandela's understanding of freedom change with age and experience?

Show Answer

Ans:

As a boy in the villages of Umtata, freedom meant being able to run in the fields near his mother's hut, to swim in the stream, to roast mealies under the stars and to ride the broad backs of slow-moving bulls. It was the freedom of a child, and it seemed boundless.
As a student he wanted the transitory freedoms of staying out at night, reading what he pleased and going where he liked.
As a young man he yearned for the honourable freedoms — of achieving his potential, of earning his keep, of marrying and having a family, of living lawfully in a law-abiding society.
Later he realised that these were only illusory, because he was not free and neither were his people. He came to understand that freedom is indivisible: the chains on any one of his people were chains on all of them. He saw that the oppressed and the oppressor alike are robbed of their humanity, and that freedom is not merely to throw off one's chains but to live in a way that respects and enhances the freedom of others.

Ch 3 · Two Stories about Flying (12)

SHORTS13 marks

1. Why was the young seagull afraid to fly? What compelled him to finally make the flight?

Show Answer

Ans:

He was afraid because he felt certain that his wings would never support him, and because his siblings had already flown while he was left behind on the ledge. What compelled him to fly was hunger: when his mother tore a piece of fish and stood on the ledge, the sight of the food maddened him, and he dived at it. In that dive he fell outward into space and, flapping his wings, found that he could fly.

SHORTS23 marks

2. Describe the help the pilot received during the storm in 'The Black Aeroplane'.

Show Answer

Ans:

When his instruments failed and he ran low on fuel, he saw a black aeroplane with no lights on its wings. Its pilot waved to him and led him through the storm until the narrator came out of the clouds and could see the runway. When he landed and asked the woman in the control centre about the other pilot, she told him there had been no other aeroplane in the sky that night, and no other plane on her radar.

SHORTS33 marks

3. How did the seagull's family react when he flew for the first time?

Show Answer

Ans:

They screamed around him, praising him and offering him scraps of fish. They landed ahead of him on the water, beckoning him to join them, and when he finally sank tired onto the sea and floated, they praised him again — his first flight was complete.

Show Answer

Ans:

His family taunted and cajoled him, but he was too afraid to leave the ledge.
When his mother flew past him with a piece of fish in her beak and stopped just out of reach, the pang of hunger was stronger than his fear.
He dived at the fish, lost his balance, and his wings spread and lifted him automatically.
His fear lasted only a moment; once in the air he was flying, and his parents and brothers praised him.

APPLICATIONA73 marks

7. Compare the mother's role in ‘His First Flight’ with the black aeroplane pilot's role. How is each a guide who appears at the right moment?

Show Answer

Ans:

The mother tempts the seagull with fish and then stops short, forcing him to cross the gap between fear and flight — she guides by testing.
The unknown pilot appears when the narrator's instruments fail and his fuel runs low, leading him through the storm to safety — he guides by rescuing.
Both appear at the exact moment when the frightened one must move forward, and both vanish once courage takes over.

LONGL115 marks

11. 'Both the stories are about overcoming fear.' Justify with reference to the two stories.

Show Answer

Ans:

In His First Flight, the young seagull is paralysed by fear while his brothers and sister fly. His parents scold him and his siblings taunt him, but fear keeps him on the ledge for twenty-four hours. Only extreme hunger, and the sight of his mother with a piece of fish, drive him to dive — and in that desperate moment he discovers that his wings do support him. His fear is overcome by necessity and by the example of his family.
In The Black Aeroplane, the narrator is an experienced pilot, but the storm terrifies him: the compass and radio fail, he cannot see anything, and he has only a few minutes of fuel left. His fear is overcome by following the mysterious black aeroplane, whose pilot leads him out of the storm and vanishes. He never finds out who helped him, which suggests that the courage came from within.
In both stories, fear is conquered only by taking the leap — the seagull by diving, the pilot by following into the dark — and in both the reward is a safe arrival.

Show Answer

5. What is the structure of an analytical paragraph?

Show Answer

Ans:

Begin by naming the data: what the chart or table shows, over what period and for whom.
Then give the main trend or the largest and smallest categories, using the figures in the data.
Next explain the reasons or the pattern behind the figures, comparing categories where useful.
End with a conclusion or a suggestion, and keep the whole paragraph to about 100 to 120 words.
