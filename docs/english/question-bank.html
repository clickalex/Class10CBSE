Ch 1 · A Letter to God (12)

Save this chapter: Text (.txt) ·

SHORTS13 marks

1. Why did Lencho say the post office employees were a bunch of crooks?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because he had asked God for one hundred pesos and received only seventy. His faith was so absolute that he could not believe God had made a mistake or had sent less, so he concluded that the post office employees must have taken the remaining thirty pesos. The irony is that these were the very men who had collected the money for him out of charity.

SHORTS23 marks

2. What made the postmaster decide to help Lencho?

Show Answer

Ans:

He was moved by the farmer's unshakable faith in God. He did not want to shake that faith, so he collected money from his employees and friends, gave part of his own salary, and sent it to Lencho in a letter signed 'God'.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Was Lencho surprised to find a letter with money in it? What made him angry?

Show Answer

Ans:

No, he was not surprised at all — he had expected an answer from God, and his confidence in God was so complete that receiving a reply seemed natural. What made him angry was that the envelope contained only seventy pesos instead of the hundred he had asked for. He was certain that God could not have made a mistake, so he concluded that the post office employees had taken the rest.

APPLICATIONA43 marks

4. A student writes: “Lencho received one hundred pesos from God and thanked Him.” Correct this using the text.

Show Answer

Ans:

Lencho asked for a hundred pesos but received only seventy; the money had been collected by the postmaster from his employees and friends, plus part of his own salary.
He was not surprised to get a reply, since he was sure God would answer, but he became angry when he counted the money.
He then wrote a second letter asking God to send the rest, and not by post, because the post-office employees were “a bunch of crooks”.

APPLICATIONA53 marks

5. Lencho never thanks the postmaster or his helpers. Is he being fair to them? Give two reasons.

Show Answer

Ans:

No. The postmaster and his employees gave their own money to help a stranger, and Lencho accuses them of stealing the missing thirty pesos.
His faith in God is so complete that he cannot imagine ordinary people being generous, so he credits God alone and suspects men.
His mistake comes from innocence, not cruelty; this is what makes the ending ironic.

SHORTS63 marks

6. Bring out the irony in the ending of ‘A Letter to God'.

Show Answer

Ans:

Lencho is certain that God sent him the money and cannot imagine that human beings could be so generous. He writes a second letter to complain and calls the post office employees ‘a bunch of crooks' because thirty pesos were missing. The irony is that the very people who helped him are the ones he distrusts: his faith in God survives, while his faith in human beings is destroyed.

APPLICATIONA73 marks

7. “The postmaster was a fat, jolly fellow.” How does his character contrast with Lencho's, and why does the contrast matter to the story?

Show Answer

Ans:

Lencho is intense, single-minded and absolute in his faith; the postmaster is warm, practical and humane — he helps without needing to be believed in.
The contrast creates the story's deepest irony: the believer cannot recognise human kindness, while the kind man asks for no recognition at all.
Without this contrast the ending would be merely sad; with it, it becomes a comment on faith that blinds as well as sustains.

COMPETENCYC84 marks

8. Read and answer: After a gentle rain, large hailstones began to fall on Lencho’s valley. He said they looked like new coins. Soon the field was white, as if covered with salt, and he said a plague of locusts would have left more than the hail had. (a) What did the hailstones first look like to Lencho? (b) What do the white field and the locusts tell us about the damage? (c) Where did Lencho turn for hope, and why?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) They looked like new silver coins, which at first seemed like a blessing.
(b) The crop was completely destroyed, even worse than a plague of locusts would have left it, so the family faced hunger for the year.
(c) To God, because his faith was unshakable and he believed that no one else could help.

COMPETENCYC94 marks

9. Read and answer: The postmaster read Lencho’s letter to God. (a) How did he react at first? (b) What changed his attitude? (c) What did he do to help, and how did he hide it?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) He laughed.
(b) He became serious when he realised how deep the writer’s faith was; he did not want to shake it.
(c) He collected money from his employees and friends and gave part of his own salary, and sent it in an envelope with a letter signed “God”.

LONGL104 marks

10. Describe Lencho's character and say what his faith tells us about him.

Show Answer

Ans:

Lencho is a poor but hard-working farmer who knows his field like a child and never stops hoping for a good crop.
His faith is complete and childlike: after the hailstorm destroys everything, he writes to God for a hundred pesos with the same confidence with which he sows his field.
The same faith makes him naive — he cannot imagine that the postmaster and his staff collected the money themselves.
The story uses this innocence to show both the strength of faith and the loneliness of the poor, who trust God more than the people around them.

LONGL115 marks

11. 'The story is built around two ironies.' Explain with reference to the text.

Show Answer

Ans:

The first irony is in the weather: Lencho's field needed rain, and rain did come — but it turned into a hailstorm that destroyed the entire crop. The very thing that promised a good harvest took it away.
The second irony is in the ending: the post office employees, moved by Lencho's faith, collected seventy pesos and sent them to him. Lencho, finding the amount short, wrote back accusing those same men of stealing the money and calling them 'a bunch of crooks'. The helpers become the accused thieves, and the man who never doubted God doubts the honesty of human beings.

LONGL125 marks

12. What does “A Letter to God” suggest about faith and human kindness? Discuss with reference to Lencho and the postmaster.

Show Answer

Ans:

Lencho’s faith is complete: after the hail he writes to God as confidently as he sowed his field.
The postmaster represents human goodness: he acts out of kindness and asks for no thanks.
The irony is that Lencho keeps his faith in God but loses his faith in people, accusing the helpers of cheating him.
The story shows that faith is admirable, yet it can blind a person to the real kindness around him.
It also suggests that God’s help can reach us through human hands.

Ch 2 · Nelson Mandela: Long Walk to Freedom (12)

Save this chapter: Text (.txt) ·

SHORTS13 marks

1. What does courage mean to Mandela?

Show Answer

Ans:

For Mandela, courage is not the absence of fear but the triumph over it. The brave man is not the one who does not feel afraid, but the one who conquers that fear. He says he felt fear more times than he can remember, and it was this repeated conquest of fear that made him bold.

SHORTS23 marks

2. What 'twin obligations' does Mandela mention?

Show Answer

Ans:

The first obligation is to his family — his parents, his wife and children. The second is to his people, his community and his country. In a civil and humane society each person can fulfil both, but in South Africa under apartheid, a man like Mandela who tried to live as a human being was punished and isolated, and could not discharge either obligation.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Why did such a large number of international leaders attend the inauguration? What did it signify?

Show Answer

Ans:

It signified the recognition by the international community of the birth of a free, democratic, non-racial South Africa, and a common victory for justice, for peace and for human dignity. The presence of leaders of many nations showed that the end of apartheid was not only a South African event but a triumph of values shared by the whole world.

SHORTS43 marks

4. Why does Mandela say that the oppressor is also not free?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because the oppressor is a prisoner of hatred, locked behind the bars of prejudice and narrow-mindedness. A man who takes away another man's freedom is himself deprived of his own humanity, so the oppressor and the oppressed are both robbed of what makes them human.

APPLICATIONA53 marks

5. A student writes that Mandela’s inauguration took place on 10 May 1995 and that apartheid simply meant keeping races apart. Correct both statements.

Show Answer

Ans:

The inauguration took place on 10 May 1994, in the amphitheatre formed by the Union Buildings in Pretoria.
Apartheid was a system of racial discrimination: laws separated people by race and denied the black majority their freedom and rights.
Under it, a man who tried to live as a free human being could be punished and isolated.

APPLICATIONA63 marks

6. Mandela says, “Courage was not the absence of fear, but the triumph over it.” Give an example from school life that fits this idea and explain why.

Show Answer

Ans:

Courage means acting rightly in spite of feeling afraid.
Example: a student who is terrified of public speaking, yet takes part in a debate. She is brave because she feels the fear and still goes ahead.
In the same way, Mandela felt fear many times but conquered it, and that is what made him brave.

SHORTS73 marks

7. What does Mandela mean when he says that freedom is indivisible?

Show Answer

Ans:

He means that no one is truly free while others are unfree: the oppressor is as much a prisoner of hatred as the oppressed is of chains. His own hunger for freedom for his people was what made him lose the freedom to live with his family, and this cannot be separated from the freedom of every South African, white or black.

COMPETENCYC84 marks

8. Read and answer: In his speech Mandela said, “Never, never and never again shall it be that this beautiful land will again experience the oppression of one by another.” (a) On what occasion did he say this? (b) What is the effect of repeating “never”? (c) What does “the oppression of one by another” refer to?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) At his inauguration as the first black President of South Africa, on 10 May 1994.
(b) The repetition adds force and shows a firm, solemn promise that the past will not return.
(c) The system of apartheid, in which one racial group dominated and oppressed the others.

COMPETENCYC94 marks

9. Read and answer: Mandela remembers his boyhood as a time when he was “born free”: running in the fields near his mother’s hut, swimming in a clear stream and roasting mealies under the stars. Later he calls this freedom an illusion. (a) Name two of the freedoms of his boyhood. (b) Why does he call them an illusion? (c) What did he begin to hunger for as a young man?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) Any two: running in the fields, swimming in the stream, roasting mealies, riding the backs of bulls.
(b) He found that, as a black South African, his basic freedom had already been taken away by the laws of apartheid.
(c) The freedom of his people to live with dignity and to achieve their potential.

LONGL104 marks

10. Describe the inauguration of 10 May 1994 and the obligations Mandela spoke of.

Show Answer

Ans:

The ceremony took place in the amphitheatre of the Union Buildings in Pretoria, watched by leaders and citizens of many countries and by a crowd of South Africans.
Mandela was sworn in as the first black President of a democratic South Africa, with Thabo Mbeki and de Klerk as his deputies, and the old and new national anthems were sung.
He spoke of the courage of the people, of the end of white rule and of the birth of a society in which all would be equal.
He said the obligation of the new government was to free the people from poverty, deprivation, suffering, gender and other discrimination, so that the dignity of every person is respected.

LONGL115 marks

11. How did Mandela's understanding of freedom change with age and experience?

Show Answer

Ans:

As a boy in the villages of Umtata, freedom meant being able to run in the fields near his mother's hut, to swim in the stream, to roast mealies under the stars and to ride the broad backs of slow-moving bulls. It was the freedom of a child, and it seemed boundless.
As a student he wanted the transitory freedoms of staying out at night, reading what he pleased and going where he liked.
As a young man he yearned for the honourable freedoms — of achieving his potential, of earning his keep, of marrying and having a family, of living lawfully in a law-abiding society.
Later he realised that these were only illusory, because he was not free and neither were his people. He came to understand that freedom is indivisible: the chains on any one of his people were chains on all of them. He saw that the oppressed and the oppressor alike are robbed of their humanity, and that freedom is not merely to throw off one's chains but to live in a way that respects and enhances the freedom of others.

LONGL125 marks

12. What kind of South Africa did Mandela promise at his inauguration, and what values did he stress?

Show Answer

Ans:

He promised a society in which all South Africans, black and white, could walk tall without fear, assured of their human dignity: a “rainbow nation”.
He said that the new government would never allow one group to oppress another again.
He paid tribute to the courage of ordinary people who had resisted apartheid.
He spoke of the twin obligations to family and to one’s people, and said freedom is indivisible: the oppressor and the oppressed must both be freed.
His speech joined the personal and the national, showing that freedom, to be real, must be shared by everyone.

Ch 3 · Two Stories about Flying (12)

Save this chapter: Text (.txt) ·

SHORTS13 marks

1. Why was the young seagull afraid to fly? What compelled him to finally make the flight?

Show Answer

Ans:

He was afraid because he felt certain that his wings would never support him, and because his siblings had already flown while he was left behind on the ledge. What compelled him to fly was hunger: when his mother tore a piece of fish and stood on the ledge, the sight of the food maddened him, and he dived at it. In that dive he fell outward into space and, flapping his wings, found that he could fly.

SHORTS23 marks

2. Describe the help the pilot received during the storm in 'The Black Aeroplane'.

Show Answer

Ans:

When his instruments failed and he ran low on fuel, he saw a black aeroplane with no lights on its wings. Its pilot waved to him and led him through the storm until the narrator came out of the clouds and could see the runway. When he landed and asked the woman in the control centre about the other pilot, she told him there had been no other aeroplane in the sky that night, and no other plane on her radar.

SHORTS33 marks

3. How did the seagull's family react when he flew for the first time?

Show Answer

Ans:

They screamed around him, praising him and offering him scraps of fish. They landed ahead of him on the water, beckoning him to join them, and when he finally sank tired onto the sea and floated, they praised him again — his first flight was complete.

APPLICATIONA43 marks

4. A student writes that the young seagull flew because his father threatened him. Correct this with evidence from the story.

Show Answer

Ans:

It was hunger, not a threat, that made him fly.
His parents and brothers had coaxed and taunted him, but he stayed frozen on the ledge.
When his mother flew by with a piece of fish and stopped just out of reach, he was so hungry that he dived at it, and in the dive his wings opened and he found himself flying.

APPLICATIONA53 marks

5. A friend refuses to try swimming after one bad experience. Using the young seagull’s experience, give her three pieces of advice.

Show Answer

Ans:

Fear often comes from imagining that we cannot do something; the seagull was sure his wings would not hold him, yet they did.
A small push or a good reason can help us take the first step; in the story it was hunger.
Once we begin, the fear passes quickly, as it did for the seagull, so she should try again with someone supportive.

SHORTS63 marks

6. How did the young seagull finally overcome his fear of flying?

Show Answer

Ans:

His family taunted and cajoled him, but he was too afraid to leave the ledge.
When his mother flew past him with a piece of fish in her beak and stopped just out of reach, the pang of hunger was stronger than his fear.
He dived at the fish, lost his balance, and his wings spread and lifted him automatically.
His fear lasted only a moment; once in the air he was flying, and his parents and brothers praised him.

APPLICATIONA73 marks

7. Compare the mother's role in ‘His First Flight’ with the black aeroplane pilot's role. How is each a guide who appears at the right moment?

Show Answer

Ans:

The mother tempts the seagull with fish and then stops short, forcing him to cross the gap between fear and flight — she guides by testing.
The unknown pilot appears when the narrator's instruments fail and his fuel runs low, leading him through the storm to safety — he guides by rescuing.
Both appear at the exact moment when the frightened one must move forward, and both vanish once courage takes over.

COMPETENCYC84 marks

8. Read and answer: The seagull’s parents and brothers coaxed and scolded him, and his mother finally held a piece of fish out of his reach. (a) Why did the mother hold the food out of reach? (b) What effect did hunger have on the seagull? (c) Do you approve of the family’s method? Why?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) She wanted to tempt him to leave the ledge; he could get the fish only by flying.
(b) It was stronger than his fear, so he dived at the fish and began to fly.
(c) An answer with a reason: for example, it worked because gentle persuasion had failed, though it was a harsh push for a frightened young bird.

COMPETENCYC94 marks

9. Read and answer: Over the storm, the pilot’s instruments failed and his fuel ran low. Then a black aeroplane appeared in front of him. (a) How would you describe the pilot’s state of mind? (b) Which two details increase the tension? (c) What finally brought him to safety?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) Anxious and frightened, but hopeful once he saw the other plane.
(b) The failure of his instruments in the storm and the shortage of fuel.
(c) He followed the black aeroplane, which led him through the clouds until he saw the runway.

APPLICATIONA104 marks

10. Who do you think was in the black aeroplane? Give reasons for your answer.

Show Answer

Ans:

The pilot saw no lights, no other plane and no runway below, and he had lost his radio, compass and fuel gauge in the storm.
The black aeroplane appeared beside him, guided him through the clouds and then vanished before he landed.
The Paris control room reported that the radar showed an unidentified plane flying beside him the whole time.
These details suggest the helper was not an ordinary pilot — it reads like the pilot's own courage, or a guardian spirit, and the story leaves the question deliberately open.

LONGL115 marks

11. 'Both the stories are about overcoming fear.' Justify with reference to the two stories.

Show Answer

Ans:

In His First Flight, the young seagull is paralysed by fear while his brothers and sister fly. His parents scold him and his siblings taunt him, but fear keeps him on the ledge for twenty-four hours. Only extreme hunger, and the sight of his mother with a piece of fish, drive him to dive — and in that desperate moment he discovers that his wings do support him. His fear is overcome by necessity and by the example of his family.
In The Black Aeroplane, the narrator is an experienced pilot, but the storm terrifies him: the compass and radio fail, he cannot see anything, and he has only a few minutes of fuel left. His fear is overcome by following the mysterious black aeroplane, whose pilot leads him out of the storm and vanishes. He never finds out who helped him, which suggests that the courage came from within.
In both stories, fear is conquered only by taking the leap — the seagull by diving, the pilot by following into the dark — and in both the reward is a safe arrival.

LONGL125 marks

12. Draw a character sketch of the young seagull and show how he changes from the beginning of the story to the end.

Show Answer

Ans:

At first he is timid and afraid: he stays on the ledge while his brothers and sisters fly.
He is stubborn in his fear: he ignores the coaxing and scolding of his family.
He is also very hungry, and this makes him forget his fear for a moment and dive for the fish.
Once in the air he finds that his wings carry him; he becomes joyful and confident.
The change shows that fear is often only in the mind, and that an experience can end it.

Ch 4 · From the Diary of Anne Frank (12)

Save this chapter: Text (.txt) ·

SHORTS13 marks

1. Why did Anne Frank feel the need for a diary?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because although she had a loving family, friends and admirers, she had no true friend — nobody to whom she could confide her innermost thoughts. She wrote that paper has more patience than people, so she began the diary as a substitute for a real friend, and named it Kitty.

SHORTS23 marks

2. What argument did Anne make in her first essay, 'A Chatterbox'?

Show Answer

Ans:

She argued that talking is a student's trait and that she would try to keep it under control, but that she would never be able to cure herself completely of the habit, because her mother talks as much as she does. She added that there is little one can do about inherited traits — an argument which Mr Keesing took in the right spirit.

SHORTS33 marks

3. What does Anne write about her grandmother?

Show Answer

Ans:

Anne writes that nobody knows how often she thinks of her grandmother and still loves her. Her grandmother was born in 1888 and died in January 1942, and Anne says that she wrote the diary partly so that this love would not be lost. She adds that this whole diary was written in the thought of her grandmother.

APPLICATIONA43 marks

4. A student writes that Anne had no friends and named her diary “Anne”. Correct both statements.

Show Answer

Ans:

Anne had many friends and admirers, about thirty of them, but no true friend with whom she could share her deepest thoughts.
She named her diary Kitty and wrote to it as to a friend.
She felt that “paper has more patience than people”, so she could pour out her feelings in it.

APPLICATIONA53 marks

5. Many teenagers today keep a diary or a blog. Compare their reasons with Anne’s and give two similarities.

Show Answer

Ans:

Like Anne, they want a private place to express feelings they cannot share easily with others.
They also use writing to understand themselves and to record events and memories.
A difference is that Anne wrote in hiding and for herself, while a blog today is often written for an audience.

SHORTS63 marks

6. Why did Anne Frank decide to keep a diary?

Show Answer

Ans:

She had no real friend in whom she could confide; although she had many acquaintances, she could not discuss her innermost thoughts with them.
A diary cannot talk back, and she felt that paper has more patience than people.
She wanted to write out her deepest thoughts and feelings, and the diary became the friend she called Kitty.

SHORTS73 marks

7. What was Anne's argument in her third essay, and why did it win over Mr Keesing?

Show Answer

Ans:

The third essay was a poem — ‘Quack, Quack, Quack, Said Mistress Chatterbox’ — about a mother duck and a father swan whose three ducklings are bitten to death by the father for quacking too much.
The joke was aimed at Mr Keesing himself: too much punishment for too much talking. Luckily he took it the right way, laughed, read it to the class, and from then on let Anne talk — becoming, in her words, the nicest teacher.

COMPETENCYC84 marks

8. Read and answer: Anne writes, “Paper has more patience than people.” (a) Which figure of speech is used? (b) What does the sentence mean? (c) How does it explain why Anne began a diary?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) Personification (paper is given the human quality of patience); it is also a comparison.
(b) A page will listen without interrupting or judging, but people often do not have the time or patience to listen.
(c) She had no friend who would listen in that way, so she chose to write instead.

COMPETENCYC94 marks

9. Read and answer: Anne says that she has many friends and admirers, but that when she is with them, “we just can’t seem to get any closer”. (a) What does she mean by a true friend? (b) Why can she not share her feelings with her acquaintances? (c) What does she hope the diary will be?

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) Someone to whom she could confide her innermost thoughts.
(b) With them she talks only about ordinary things and the talk never becomes deeper.
(c) A friend, whom she calls Kitty, who will listen with “patience”.

APPLICATIONA104 marks

10. Describe Mr Keesing's punishment and how Anne used it to her advantage.

Show Answer

Ans:

Anne was caught talking in class, so the maths teacher Mr Keesing gave her extra homework: first an essay on ‘A Chatterbox', then ‘An Incorrigible Chatterbox', and then ‘Quack Quack Quack, Said Mistress Chatterbox'.
Anne wrote the first essay arguing that talking is a student's trait which she inherited from her mother and could never cure.
For the second she argued that she could not stop talking in a maths class because it was impossible to keep quiet.
For the third she wrote a poem about a swan with three ducklings that bit their father to death, and illustrated it; Mr Keesing was amused, read the poem to the class and stopped punishing her.

LONGL115 marks

11. Describe Mr Keesing and how his attitude towards Anne changed.

Show Answer

Ans:

Mr Keesing was Anne's mathematics teacher, an old-fashioned teacher who was annoyed by her constant talking in class. He punished her by assigning an essay titled 'A Chatterbox'.
Anne's response: she wrote three pages, admitting the habit but arguing that talking is a student's trait and that her mother talks as much as she does, so it is an inherited trait and there is little one can do about it. Mr Keesing took the joke in the right spirit.
The second and third essays: when she talked again he assigned 'An Incorrigible Chatterbox', and the third time he set 'Quack, Quack, Quack, Said Mistress Chatterbox' — this time in verse. With the help of her friend Sanne, Anne wrote a comic poem about a mother duck and a father swan whose three ducklings are bitten to death by the swan for quacking too much.
The change: Mr Keesing read the poem to his own class and then to another class, adding his own comments, and laughed at it. From then on he allowed Anne to talk in class and never gave her extra homework again. Anne calls him the nicest teacher she ever met.

LONGL125 marks

12. What picture of Anne’s personality emerges from the extract? Support your answer with four qualities.

Show Answer

Ans:

Talkative and cheerful: she is called a chatterbox and cannot stop talking in class.
Witty and creative: she turns a punishment into a poem and makes the teacher laugh.
Honest: she admits her failing and does not pretend to be different.
Affectionate and thoughtful: she writes lovingly about her grandmother and longs for a true friend.
Together they show a lively, perceptive girl who uses writing to make sense of life.

Ch 5 · Glimpses of India (12)

Save this chapter: Text (.txt) ·

SHORTS13 marks

1. Why is the baker called a friend, companion and guide in Goa?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because he was woven into the daily life of the village. Show Answer

SHORTS13 marks

1. Describe the structure of an analytical paragraph.

Show Answer

Ans:

The paragraph opens with a sentence introducing the data — what it shows, the period it covers and the units used. The middle sentences describe the main features, selecting the significant figures and comparing them. The final sentence gives a conclusion or a trend inferred from the data. The whole paragraph is within the word limit and contains no opinion.

Show Answer

Ans:

It should avoid giving personal opinions or recommendations unless the question asks for them, inventing data that is not in the chart, listing every figure instead of selecting the significant ones, and exceeding the word limit. It should describe and compare the data, not comment on it.
