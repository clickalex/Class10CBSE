Chapter 15 · Amanda!

Chapter 15 of 32. Two voices — the parent's instructions and the child's escape into fantasy.

Open this chapter’s questions + MCQs →Mock-test this chapter (timed, scored) →

🎯 Marks lens 📖 Deep concepts 🛠️ Steps 🧠 Tricks ⚠️ Mistakes ❓ Exam Q&A ✍️ Task

🎯 Marks lens

Reference-to-context extracts; a 3-mark answer on whether Amanda is an orphan or a mermaid, and on the theme of control.
Three fantasies: an orphan roaming the street, Rapunzel in a tower, and a mermaid in the sea.

📖 Deep concepts

1. The poem. The poem alternates between the instructions of an adult — presumably Amanda's mother — and Amanda's daydreams. The adult tells her not to bite her nails, not to hunch her shoulders, not to slouch, to sit up straight, to do her homework, to clean her room, to shine her shoes, not to eat chocolate because of her acne, and not to look so moody. Amanda answers each instruction with a fantasy: she is an orphan roaming the street, patterning the dust with her bare feet, silent as the sea; she is Rapunzel in a tower, whose life in a tranquil tower is rare, and who would never let down her bright hair; she is a mermaid, drifting blissfully in the languid emerald sea. The adult then asks if she is sulking, and Amanda's silence is read as resentment.
2. Central idea. The poem is about the pressure of constant adult control on a growing child. Amanda is not a disobedient girl; she is a girl who needs space to dream. The fantasy world she retreats into — silence, drifting, freedom from instruction — is her only escape. The poem criticises parents who nag so much that the child learns to shut them out.
3. Poetic devices. Alternating voices — the instructions in the outer stanzas and the daydreams in the bracketed stanzas. Simile — 'silent as the sea'. Metaphor and allusion — Rapunzel and the mermaid. Alliteration — 'bare feet', 'drifting blissfully'. Repetition — the repeated 'Amanda!'.

🛠️ Method, step by step

  1. Name the two voices: the adult nagging with instructions, and Amanda's silent, dreaming reply.
  2. List the instructions — don't bite your nails, hunch your shoulders, eat your chocolate, finish your homework.
  3. Show Amanda's escape — she dreams of being a mermaid, an orphan roaming the streets, and Rapunzel on a tower of silence.
  4. Close on the point: the nagging is called care, but it leaves the child lonely; the poem asks the reader to stop and listen.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Three fantasies in order: orphan, Rapunzel, mermaid. Each is her escape from one kind of instruction.
  • Amanda is not an orphan or a mermaid — the question asks why she imagines these, and the answer is freedom and silence.
  • The final line — the adult accusing her of sulking — shows the parent has learned nothing.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Answering that Amanda is an orphan. She only imagines herself to be one.
  • Saying she lets down her hair like Rapunzel. She says she would never let down her bright hair, because she does not want to be found.
  • Missing the two voices. The bracketed stanzas are Amanda's imagination, not her replies.

SHORTS13 marks

1. Is Amanda an orphan? Why does she say she is one?

Show Answer

Ans:

No, she is not an orphan. She imagines herself to be one because she is tired of the constant instructions and scolding. As an orphan roaming the street, patterning the dust with her bare feet, she would be free, silent and alone — free from anyone telling her what to do. The fantasy is an escape, not a statement of fact.

SHORTS23 marks

2. Why does Amanda want to be Rapunzel?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because Rapunzel lives in a tranquil tower, far away from the world, and her life there is rare and peaceful. Amanda longs for that peace and solitude, away from the nagging. But she adds that she would never let down her bright hair, because she does not want anyone to find her — she wants the tower for its silence, not for rescue.

SHORTS33 marks

3. What does the poem tell us about the relationship between Amanda and the adult?

Show Answer

Ans:

It shows a relationship of constant control and silent resistance. The adult gives instruction after instruction — about nails, posture, homework, shoes, chocolate and mood — and Amanda withdraws into her imagination each time. The adult interprets this silence as sulking and asks if she is moody, which shows that the adult has not understood that the child needs space to dream. The poem criticises a way of bringing up children that leaves no room for them.

SHORTS63 marks

6. What does Amanda imagine herself to be, and why?

Show Answer

Ans:

She imagines herself as a mermaid drifting blissfully on the lapping waves of a languid emerald sea.
She imagines herself as an orphan, roaming barefoot and free in the dust and gold of the street, with no one to scold her.
She also dreams of being Rapunzel, living in a tower where no one can find her and life is ‘tranquil and rare, unmolested'.
Each daydream is an escape from the constant instruction and nagging she hears at home, so the poem is really about a child's need for freedom.

SHORTS73 marks

7. Why does Amanda imagine being a mermaid ‘in the languid, emerald sea’? What is she escaping from?

Show Answer

Ans:

The mermaid drifts alone in a green, slow sea where ‘the only sound is silence’ — the opposite of her life of constant instructions: do not bite your nails, sit straight, stop sulking. She is escaping from adult control into a world where nobody corrects her and she can simply drift free. Each fantasy is a wish for silence and freedom.

LONGL104 marks

10. How does the poem show the conflict between a child's freedom and adult instruction?

Show Answer

Ans:

Each stanza opens with a command — don't bite your nails, don't hunch your shoulders, don't eat chocolate, finish your homework, tidy your room.
Between the commands Amanda drifts into silence and dream, which is her only defence.
The adults believe they are shaping a good child; the child experiences only constant correction and the loss of her own thoughts.
The poem suggests that endless instruction produces withdrawal rather than goodness, and ends with the warning that if she is sulked at all day she will stop loving herself.

LONGL114 marks

11. The poem ends with Amanda being accused of sulking. Why does this ending prove that the adult has understood nothing?

Show Answer

Ans:

Amanda is silent not because she sulks but because she has withdrawn into daydreams — her only private space.
The adult reads this silence as disobedience (‘you are sulking’) and adds one more instruction to the list.
The ending thus completes the circle: nagging produces withdrawal, withdrawal is punished with more nagging. The adult hears the silence but never asks what it means.
