Chapter 17 · Fog

Chapter 17 of 32. The shortest poem in the syllabus and the most frequently asked. Six lines, one metaphor.

Open this chapter’s questions + MCQs →

🎯 Marks lens 📖 Deep concepts 🛠️ Steps 🧠 Tricks ⚠️ Mistakes ❓ Exam Q&A ✍️ Task

🎯 Marks lens

Reference-to-context extracts; a 3-mark answer on the central metaphor and on whether the poet actually says the fog is a cat.
The fog comes on little cat feet, sits looking over harbour and city, then moves on.

📖 Deep concepts

1. The poem. The fog comes / on little cat feet. / It sits looking / over harbour and city / on silent haunches / and then moves on. Six lines, no rhyme.
2. Central idea. The poet compares the arrival and movement of fog to the quiet, soft, unannounced coming and going of a cat. The fog covers the city and the harbour silently, looks down on them, and then drifts away without warning. The comparison captures the mysterious and gentle quality of fog.
3. Poetic devices. Metaphor — the fog is compared to a cat throughout, but the word 'like' is never used, so it is a metaphor and not a simile. Personification — the fog sits on silent haunches and looks. Imagery — the silent, soft movement of the fog over the harbour and city. Free verse — no rhyme scheme or metre.
4. How the cat comparison is carried. Nothing in the poem says the fog is like a cat; instead every verb is one a cat could own — it comes on little cat feet, it sits, it looks, it moves on. Because the comparison is never announced, the reader accepts it before noticing it. This is the single most-rewarded observation in an answer on this poem, and it explains why the poem needs no adjectives at all.
5. Form, silence and the city. Sandburg writes in free verse — two quatrains, no rhyme scheme, no regular metre — and the looseness matches a fog that obeys nothing. The setting is a working harbour city, and the point is the contrast: a city built on noise and schedules is stopped, briefly, by something silent. The fog leaves as quietly as it came, and the poem suggests that the pressure of human hurry is the real interruption, not the weather.

🛠️ Method, step by step

  1. Say immediately that the fog is compared to a cat — that single comparison is the poem's whole structure.
  2. Describe the arrival: 'It sits looking over harbour and city / on silent haunches and then moves on.'
  3. Point out the deliberate stillness — no roar, no announcement; the fog simply comes, looks and leaves.
  4. Close on Carl Sandburg's method: free verse, no rhyme, two short stanzas, and a comparison that is never stated as a simile but carried through every verb.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • It is a metaphor, not a simile. The poet never uses 'like' or 'as' — that distinction is a marked point.
  • 'Little cat feet' = silent, soft, unhurried. Three adjectives answer most questions.
  • The fog 'moves on' without warning, as a cat does.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Saying the poet says the fog is a cat. He compares it to a cat; the fog is not literally a cat.
  • Calling it a simile. There is no 'like' or 'as'.
  • Adding lines. The poem is six lines and nothing more.

SHORTS23 marks

2. What is the central idea of the poem?

Show Answer

Ans:

The central idea is that fog, like a cat, arrives silently and without warning, covers the city and the harbour, looks down on them, and then moves away. The comparison brings out the mysterious, gentle and unobtrusive nature of fog, and shows how a simple natural event can be described vividly in a few lines.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Identify the figure of speech used in 'The fog comes on little cat feet'.

Show Answer

Ans:

It is a metaphor, since the fog is directly compared to a cat without the use of 'like' or 'as'. There is also personification, because the fog is given the cat's action of walking on little feet and sitting on silent haunches.

SHORTS63 marks

6. How does Carl Sandburg describe the fog?

Show Answer

Ans:

The fog comes ‘on little cat feet': silently, slowly and almost without anyone noticing.
It sits looking over harbour and city on silent haunches, like a cat perched and watching.
Then it moves on; it does not announce itself, and it does not stay.
The six-line poem turns an ordinary weather event into a living creature.

SHORTS73 marks

7. The fog ‘sits looking over harbour and city’ and then ‘moves on’. What does this arrival and departure suggest about fog?

Show Answer

Ans:

It suggests that fog comes quietly, stays a while like a watchful visitor, and leaves on its own — no one can summon or dismiss it. The sitting shows its stillness and patience; the moving on shows its mystery and freedom. Fog, like a cat, arrives and departs exactly as it pleases.

LONGL104 marks

10. What does the comparison with a cat achieve in the poem?

Show Answer

Ans:

A cat is quiet, self-possessed and indifferent to human plans, which is exactly how fog behaves in a city.
The comparison explains why fog is noticed only when it has already arrived, and why it leaves without ceremony.
The image also softens what is usually an inconvenience: the fog becomes playful rather than threatening.
By using a domestic animal, Sandburg makes the natural phenomenon familiar, which is typical of his plain, imagist style.

APPLICATIONA114 marks

11. Why is the fog compared to a cat rather than a dog? Give two qualities the fog shares with a cat.

Show Answer

Ans:

First, silence — a cat walks on soft feet and a dog barks; fog arrives noiselessly ‘on little cat feet’.
Second, independence — a cat sits where it likes and moves on when it likes; fog settles over the harbour and city and leaves without explanation.
A dog would suggest noise and obedience, the opposite of fog's quiet mystery.
