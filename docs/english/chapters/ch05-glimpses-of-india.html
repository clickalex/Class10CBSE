📖 Deep concepts

1. A Baker from Goa (Lucio Rodrigues). In the old days of Portuguese rule, bakers — known as pader — were an important part of Goan life. Their arrival was announced by the 'jang, jhang, jhang' of the bamboo staff they carried, one hand on the basket of loaves and the other banging the staff on the ground. Children ran to meet them, not for the bread (which the maid-servant bought) but to choose the bread bangles, or sometimes the sweet specially-made loaves. The baker always came twice a day — once early in the morning and again when he returned after emptying his basket. Baking was a profitable profession; the baker and his family never starved, and they always looked fat and jolly — even today, anyone with a jackfruit-like physique is compared to a baker. Bread is essential: bol (sweet bread) for marriage gifts, bolinhas and coconut cookies for Christmas, sandwiches for a daughter's engagement, and pao for the feast. The baker wore a peculiar dress, the kabai, a single-piece long frock reaching down to the knees; in the writer's childhood the bakers wore a shirt and trousers shorter than full-length ones. Monthly accounts were recorded on the wall in pencil, and the furnaces still exist.
2. Coorg (Lokesh Abrol). Coorg, or Kodagu, is the smallest district of Karnataka, midway between Mysore and the coastal town of Mangalore — a piece of heaven that must have drifted from the kingdom of god. It is a rolling hillside with a permanent fragrance of coffee, coffee estates and spice plantations, colonial bungalows under treetops, and forests. The season of joy is from September to March. The people of Coorg are a proud race of martial men, beautiful women and wild creatures; a popular theory traces their descent to Alexander's army, which moved south along the coast and settled there, marrying among the locals. Another theory claims Arab origin, since the Kuppia, the long black coat with an embroidered waist-belt worn by the Kodavus, resembles the kuffia worn by the Arabs and the Kurds. Coorgis are the most hospitable people in India, and are the only people in India permitted to carry firearms without a licence. The Kaveri river originates from the hills and forests of Coorg; Mahaseer, a large freshwater fish, is found there, and kingfishers, squirrels, langurs and wild elephants can be seen. The climb to the Brahmagiri hills gives a view of the whole misty landscape of Coorg, and a walk to the sixty-acre Buddhist monastery at Bylakuppe is a surprise.
3. Tea from Assam (Arup Kumar Datta). Rajvir, a boy from Assam, travels to Delhi with Pranjol, whose father manages the Dhekiajuli Tea Estate. On the way they stop at a tea stall, where Rajvir hears that eighty crore cups of tea are drunk every day throughout the world. Pranjol's father meets them at Mariani junction and drives them through the Dhekiajuli estate, where acres of neatly pruned bushes stretch to the horizon with shade trees. Pranjol's mother welcomes them with tea and snacks. In the garden, the workers with bamboo baskets on their backs pluck the newly sprouted leaves. Pranjol's mother says that the Chinese legend says tea was first drunk in China around 2700 BC, when some leaves from a burning twig fell into boiling water and gave a delicious drink; Marco Polo and Bodhidharma are also linked to tea legends. The second-sprouting period, from May to July, yields the finest tea. Tea was first brought to India from China by the British, and India now produces some of the finest tea in the world.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Pader, kabai, bol, bolinhas, pao — five Goan words that anchor the Goa extract.
  • Coorg = coffee fragrance + martial men + firearms without licence + Kuppia coat. Four markers, one paragraph.
  • Eighty crore cups a day is the number asked most often in the Assam extract.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Saying Coorg is in Kerala. It is Kodavu, in Karnataka, the smallest district, between Mysore and Mangalore.
  • Confusing bol (sweet bread for marriage gifts) with bolinhas (coconut cookies for Christmas).
  • Giving the tea date as 2700 AD. The Chinese legend places it around 2700 BC.

SHORTS13 marks

1. Why is the baker called a friend, companion and guide in Goa?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because he was woven into the daily life of the village. His arrival, announced by the jangling of his bamboo staff, was a fixed event twice a day, and children ran to meet him to choose their bread bangles. He was present at every celebration — bol for marriages, bolinhas and coconut cookies for Christmas, sandwiches for engagements — so his presence marked the family's important occasions.

LONGL35 marks

3. Describe the Coorg landscape and its attractions.

Show Answer

Ans:

Coorg, or Kodagu, is the smallest district of Karnataka, lying midway between Mysore and the coastal town of Mangalore. It is a rolling hillside with a permanent fragrance of fresh coffee, coffee estates and spice plantations, colonial bungalows tucked under treetops, and dense forests.
The best season is from September to March, when the weather is perfect, the air breathes of invigorating coffee, and the rain showers keep the hills fresh.
Wildlife: the Kaveri river, which takes its birth from the hills and forests of Coorg, is home to Mahaseer, a large freshwater fish; kingfishers, squirrels and langurs can be seen, and wild elephants bathe in the river.
Attractions: the climb to the Brahmagiri hills gives a panoramic view of the entire misty landscape of Coorg; a walk into the sixty-acre Buddhist monastery at Bylakutte, hidden among the coffee plantations, is a surprise; and there are rappelling, river rafting, canoeing and rock climbing for the adventurous.

SHORTS43 marks

4. How does the legend of tea connect China and India?

Show Answer

Ans:

According to a Chinese legend, tea was first drunk in China around 2700 BC, when a few leaves from a twig burning under a vessel of boiling water fell into it and gave a delicious drink. An Indian legend says that Bodhidharma, an ancient Buddhist ascetic, cut off his eyelids because he felt sleepy during meditation, and ten tea plants grew out of the eyelids; the leaves of these plants, when put in hot water and drunk, banished sleep. Tea came to Europe only in the sixteenth century, and was drunk more as medicine than as a beverage.
