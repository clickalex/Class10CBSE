Chapter 24 · Footprints without Feet

Chapter 24 of 32. A science-fiction story with a moral: brilliant science without conscience makes a criminal.

Open this chapter’s questions + MCQs →Mock-test this chapter (timed, scored) →

🎯 Marks lens 📖 Deep concepts 🛠️ Steps 🧠 Tricks ⚠️ Mistakes ❓ Exam Q&A ✍️ Task

🎯 Marks lens

Short answers and a long answer on whether Griffin was a brilliant scientist or a lawless person.
Griffin, and the three places he goes: London, Iping, and back to London.

📖 Deep concepts

1. Summary. Griffin was a brilliant scientist who had carried out experiment after experiment to prove that the human body could become invisible, and finally swallowed certain rare drugs, so that his body became as transparent as a sheet of glass, though as hard to see. His landlord disliked him and was trying to eject him, so in revenge Griffin set fire to the house and, to escape without being seen, removed his clothes. He became a homeless wanderer without clothes, without money and quite invisible. He went to a big London store, where he put on clothes, ate and slept, and then had to strip again before the store opened. He went to a theatrical company in Drury Lane, bandaged his face to hide his invisibility, robbed the shopkeeper there, and escaped to Iping village by train. He booked two rooms at the inn and paid the landlord, Mr Hall, in advance. There he experimented further, and strange things began to happen: the landlady Mrs Hall found the scientist's door open and his room empty, then a chair became alive and pushed her and her husband out of the room and slammed the door. When the landlord asked for rent, Griffin threw off his bandages and whiskers, and became a headless man. The village constable, Mr Jaffers, came to arrest him; Griffin removed his clothes one by one, and Jaffers found himself hitting a man he could not see, and was knocked unconscious. Griffin escaped again, a lawless man wandering without clothes, money, home or friends.
2. Characters. Griffin — a brilliant but lawless scientist; his discovery is remarkable, but he uses it for revenge and theft. Mr and Mrs Hall — the innkeepers at Iping. Mr Jaffers — the village constable.
3. Theme. Scientific brilliance without moral responsibility produces a danger to society; and a person who lives outside the law ends up entirely alone.

🛠️ Method, step by step

  1. State the discovery — Griffin, a scientist who finds the drug that makes the body transparent and tests it on himself.
  2. Trace the consequences in order: the landlord, the escape, the theatrical shop for bandages, and the arrival at Iping.
  3. Explain what goes wrong — the thefts, the clergyman's house, and the village that finally turns on him.
  4. Close with Kemp's house — the invisible man is shot by a constable, and his body becomes visible as he dies.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Two words describe him: brilliant and lawless. Every long answer on Griffin needs both.
  • He became invisible by swallowing rare drugs, then had to remove his clothes to stay unseen.
  • The chair scene in Mrs Hall's room is the most frequently extracted passage.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Saying Griffin was invisible by birth. He made himself invisible by swallowing rare drugs.
  • Placing the inn in London. It is in Iping, a village.
  • Forgetting that he set fire to the landlord's house — the first evidence of his lawlessness.

Show Answer

SHORTS23 marks

2. Describe what happened at the inn in Iping.

Show Answer

Ans:

Griffin booked two rooms and paid Mr Hall in advance, but behaved strangely, refusing company and shutting himself in. When Mrs Hall went to his room she found the door open and the room empty, with the clothes he had been wearing scattered about. Suddenly a hat hopped off the bedpost and struck her face, and then a chair became alive, pushed both her and her husband out of the room and slammed and locked the door behind them. Mrs Hall was convinced the room was haunted by spirits.

SHORTS53 marks

5. Why does Griffin fail as an invisible man?

Show Answer

Ans:

He discovers how to make the human body transparent, but he does not discover how to make it visible again, and he cannot reverse his experiment.
Invisibility leaves him without clothes, food and shelter; he is hungry, cold and hunted everywhere.
His behavior turns violent: he sets fire to his landlord's house, assaults people who get in his way and steals to survive.
Finally he is attacked by the crowd in the inn, and his cloak is torn off, revealing an empty space where a man should be.

SHORTS63 marks

6. Why did Griffin burn his landlord's house? What does this first crime tell us about him?

Show Answer

Ans:

Griffin burnt the house in revenge because his landlord tried to eject him. This first crime tells us that Griffin answers every obstacle with destruction — he has brilliance without conscience. The pattern never changes: robbery, assault and finally beating Constable Jaffers follow from the same lawless temper.

LONGL94 marks

9. Describe Griffin's character and what the story says about the misuse of science.

Show Answer

Ans:

Griffin is a brilliant scientist, but he is selfish, violent and dishonest: he uses his discovery to rob, to revenge himself and to terrify ordinary people.
He is disowned by his father, who had given him the money, and he is prepared to hurt anyone who obstructs him.
The story suggests that science without moral responsibility destroys the scientist himself: Griffin ends up hunted, hungry and alone.
Wells thus warns that a discovery used for private gain instead of public good turns its maker into a criminal and a victim.

APPLICATIONA104 marks

10. Invisibility should have made Griffin powerful, but it made him helpless. Explain this paradox with two examples from the story.

Show Answer

Ans:

First, at the inn in Iping, invisibility strands him: without clothes he is naked and freezing, with clothes he is visible and suspicious — the ‘headless’ reveal terrifies everyone and brings the police.
Second, he cannot eat, sleep or move unseen in a watched village; every theft leaves footprints, literally and figuratively.
The paradox is that invisibility removes his disguise but not his needs — science without planning is a trap, not a power.

LONGL115 marks

11. Was Griffin a brilliant scientist or a lawless person? Justify your answer.

Show Answer

Ans:

He was certainly a brilliant scientist. He carried out experiment after experiment until he proved that the human body could be made invisible, and succeeded in swallowing drugs that made his body as transparent as glass. It is a discovery of extraordinary difficulty, and it shows real scientific ability and persistence.
But he was also lawless. His first use of the discovery was revenge: he set fire to his landlord's house because the man disliked him and was trying to eject him. He then robbed a shop in London, stole clothes from a big store, robbed a shopkeeper at Drury Lane, and attacked the village constable Mr Jaffers at Iping.
Conclusion: scientific brilliance without moral responsibility is dangerous. Griffin used a remarkable discovery for theft, revenge and violence, and ended up a hunted, homeless wanderer with no friends. So while his ability as a scientist cannot be denied, he was, in his behaviour, a lawless person.
