Chapter 32 · Analytical Paragraph

Chapter 32 of 32. Ten marks for describing data you are given. Nothing has to be invented — the answer is in front of you.

Open this chapter’s questions + MCQs →Mock-test this chapter (timed, scored) →

🎯 Marks lens 📖 Deep concepts 🛠️ Steps 🧠 Tricks ⚠️ Mistakes ❓ Exam Q&A ✍️ Task

🎯 Marks lens

10 marks, 100–120 words. Based on a chart, graph, table, data set, outline or set of clues.
The marks go to accurate description, comparison and a concluding inference — not to opinions.

📖 Deep concepts

1. The structure. First sentence: introduce the data — what the chart shows, what period it covers and what units are used. Middle sentences: describe the main features, comparing and contrasting the significant figures. Last sentence: give a conclusion or a trend inferred from the data. Do not list every number — select the two or three that matter.
2. The language of description. Comparison: higher than, lower than, twice as much as, three times the figure for. Trend: rose steadily, fell sharply, remained constant, fluctuated, peaked in, reached a low of. Proportion: accounted for, made up, constituted, represented. Use these phrases rather than repeating 'it increased'.
3. What not to do. Do not give your opinion or a recommendation unless the question asks for one. Do not invent data that is not in the chart. Do not exceed the word limit. Do not write a story — this is a description, not a narrative.
4. Worked example. Given a table showing the percentage of households with internet access in 2010, 2015, 2020 and 2025, a good paragraph would open: 'The table shows the percentage of households with internet access over a fifteen-year period from 2010 to 2025.' It would then compare the figures, noting the sharpest rise and any period of stagnation, and close with the overall trend and its implication.

🛠️ Method, step by step

  1. Note the data cue in the question — it will ask you to analyse a chart, a table, a set of facts or a short extract.
  2. Open with one sentence that states what the data shows overall, not with the numbers themselves.
  3. Develop two or three points with the figures that support them, and say what they mean rather than repeating them.
  4. Close with a conclusion or trend — the likely direction, the reason, or the implication — and stay inside the word limit.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Open with what the chart shows, then compare, then conclude. Three moves, one paragraph.
  • Pick three figures, not ten. Examiners reward selection, not transcription.
  • Learn six comparison phrases and use them. Repeating 'it increased' loses language marks.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Copying every number from the table.
  • Adding an opinion or a recommendation that was not asked for.
  • Exceeding 120 words.
  • Writing 'the chart is very interesting' — description, not comment, is what is rewarded.

SHORTS23 marks

2. What should an analytical paragraph avoid?

Show Answer

Ans:

It should avoid giving personal opinions or recommendations unless the question asks for them, inventing data that is not in the chart, listing every figure instead of selecting the significant ones, and exceeding the word limit. It should describe and compare the data, not comment on it.

SHORTS53 marks

5. What is the structure of an analytical paragraph?

Show Answer

Ans:

Begin by naming the data: what the chart or table shows, over what period and for whom.
Then give the main trend or the largest and smallest categories, using the figures in the data.
Next explain the reasons or the pattern behind the figures, comparing categories where useful.
End with a conclusion or a suggestion, and keep the whole paragraph to about 100 to 120 words.

SHORTS63 marks

6. Which phrases of comparison and trend should an analytical paragraph use? Give four examples with the situation for each.

Show Answer

Ans:

‘Rose steadily’ — for a gradual increase over time, as in literacy rates climbing year by year.
‘Fell sharply’ — for a sudden drop, as in accidents after speed cameras were installed.
‘Peaked in’ — for the highest point, as in sales peaking in December.
‘Twice as much as’ — for direct comparison, as in bus users being twice as many as car users.

APPLICATIONA94 marks

9. A survey of 200 students of a school showed these preferences for co-curricular activities: Sports 80, Music 50, Art 40, Debate 30. Write an analytical paragraph.

Show Answer

Ans:

A survey of 200 students shows clear preferences among the four co-curricular activities offered by the school.
Sports is the most popular choice, preferred by 80 students, or 40 per cent of those surveyed, while music is second with 50 students, or 25 per cent.
Art is chosen by 40 students (20 per cent) and debate by only 30 (15 per cent), which together account for just over a third of the students.
The pattern suggests that physical activity attracts the largest number of students, while activities that require performance in front of an audience, such as debate, attract the fewest; the school could encourage the less popular activities by giving them more time, trained coaches and recognition.

APPLICATIONA104 marks

10. A student writes: ‘I think the chart is wrong and the school should buy more buses.’ Identify what is wrong with this sentence for an analytical paragraph.

Show Answer

Ans:

First, ‘I think the chart is wrong’ disputes the given data — the data must be accepted and analysed, never challenged.
Second, ‘the school should buy more buses’ is a personal opinion and a suggestion — analytical paragraphs report trends and inferences, not opinions.
The correct close would state what the data shows, for example that bus use dominates and is still rising.

LONGL115 marks

11. Write an analytical paragraph on a bar chart showing the mode of transport used by students: bus 45%, cycle 25%, walk 20%, private car 10%.

Show Answer

Ans:

The bar chart shows how students travel to school, expressed as a percentage of the total number of students.
The most common mode of transport is the bus, used by 45 per cent of students — almost half. Cycling is the second most popular, at 25 per cent, while 20 per cent of students walk to school. Only 10 per cent travel by private car, the least common mode.
Taken together, the figures show that the great majority of students — 90 per cent — use public or non-motorised transport, and that reliance on private cars is minimal. This suggests that improving bus services and cycle lanes would benefit far more students than any measure aimed at car users.
