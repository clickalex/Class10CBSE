Chapter 19 · For Anne Gregory

Chapter 19 of 33. A conversation in verse. Three speakers, one question: can anyone love you for yourself alone?

🎯 Marks lens

Reference-to-context extracts; a 3-mark answer on the central question and on the religious man's answer.
Know the poet (W. B. Yeats), the yellow hair, the rampart and the honey-coloured ramparts at your ear.

📖 Deep concepts

1. The poem. The poet asks Anne Gregory: never shall a young man, thrown into despair by those great honey-coloured ramparts at your ear, love you for yourself alone, and not your yellow hair. Anne replies that she can dye her hair brown, or black, or carrot, and that young men could then love her for herself alone and not her yellow hair. The poet answers that an old religious man told him last night that he had found a text to prove that only God, my dear, could love you for yourself alone, and not your yellow hair.
2. Central idea. Physical beauty attracts people, and it is hard for a young man to love a girl for her inner self alone when her outer beauty is so striking. Anne believes she can prove him wrong by changing her hair colour, but the poet's answer — quoting a religious text — is that only God can love someone for themselves alone. The poem is a meditation on the difference between physical attraction and true love.
3. Poetic devices. Dialogue — the poem is a conversation between the poet, Anne and (quoted) the religious man. Metaphor — 'great honey-coloured ramparts at your ear', where her hair is compared to the ramparts of a fort. Enjambment — the lines run on. Rhyme — a regular scheme with alternating rhymes. Irony — Anne's confident answer is answered by a text she cannot argue with.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • 'Ramparts' = the walls of a fort. Her hair is compared to a fort's wall — a metaphor, and a marked figure of speech.
  • Only God can love a person for themselves alone — that is the poem's conclusion.
  • Anne's answer is practical (dye the hair); the poet's is philosophical (a religious text). Contrast them.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Saying the poet answers Anne directly. He quotes an old religious man and a text.
  • Calling the ramparts a simile. There is no 'like' or 'as' — it is a metaphor.
  • Missing the conclusion: only God can love for oneself alone.

SHORTS13 marks

1. What does the poet mean by 'honey-coloured ramparts at your ear'?

Show Answer

Ans:

He is comparing Anne's yellow hair to the ramparts, or high walls, of a fort. The hair hangs around her ears like a wall that young men cannot cross, and it throws them into despair because they cannot help being attracted to her beauty. It is a metaphor, since no 'like' or 'as' is used.

SHORTS23 marks

2. What is Anne Gregory's reply, and does it answer the poet's point?

Show Answer

Ans:

Anne replies that she can dye her hair another colour — brown, or black, or carrot — so that young men could love her for herself alone and not for her yellow hair. She believes that by removing the attraction of her hair she would prove the poet wrong. But the poet answers that according to a religious text only God can love a person for themselves alone, so her plan would not change the fact that human love is always mixed with physical attraction.

SHORTS33 marks

3. What is the central idea of the poem?

Show Answer

Ans:

The central idea is that physical beauty draws people, and that it is almost impossible for a human being to love another for their inner self alone. Anne thinks that by dyeing her hair she could prove otherwise, but the poet quotes a religious text to show that only God is capable of such selfless love. The poem sets outer beauty against inner worth and suggests that true, disinterested love is divine rather than human.
