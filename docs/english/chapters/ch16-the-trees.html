Chapter 16 · The Trees

Chapter 16 of 33. An extended metaphor. The trees are prisoners moving out at night, and the moon breaks.

Open this chapter’s questions + MCQs →

🎯 Marks lens 📖 Deep concepts 🧠 Tricks ⚠️ Mistakes ❓ Exam Q&A ✍️ Task

🎯 Marks lens

Reference-to-context extracts, usually the first or the third stanza; a 5-mark answer on the message about nature.
Know the poet (Adrienne Rich), the boughs like newly discharged patients, and the moon breaking like a mirror.

📖 Deep concepts

1. The poem. The trees are inside the house, and the forest outside is empty — no birds sit, no insects hide, no sun bury its feet in shadow, and there is nothing there all day. The trees that had been inside are moving out: their roots work all night to disengage themselves from the cracks in the veranda floor, their leaves strain towards the glass, and their small twigs stiff with exertion. The long-cramped boughs shuffling under the roof are like newly discharged patients, half dazed, moving to the clinic doors. The poet sits inside with the doors open, writing long letters, and hardly mentions the departure of the forest. At night the trees stumble forward, the moon is broken like a mirror, and its pieces flash now in the crown of the tallest oak.
2. Central idea. The poem is a protest against the way human beings have cut down forests and confined nature to pots, gardens and drawing rooms. The trees struggle to break free and return to the forest. The poet also suggests that nature will not stay imprisoned — it moves out, slowly and surely, and human beings are left watching, unable to stop it.
3. Poetic devices. Personification — the trees work, strain, shuffle and stumble, as if they were human. Simile — the boughs are like newly discharged patients, half dazed. Metaphor — the moon broken like a mirror. Imagery — the roots in the cracks, the leaves straining at the glass, the pieces of moon in the oak. Free verse — no rhyme, giving the poem a quiet, observant tone.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • The trees are prisoners; the forest is empty. That reversal is the whole poem.
  • Boughs = newly discharged patients and moon = a broken mirror are the two figures of speech to name.
  • The poet writes long letters and hardly mentions the departure — say why: the event is too large for words.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Saying the forest is full and the trees are outside. It is the opposite: the forest is empty and the trees are inside.
  • Missing the simile of the patients — it is the most frequently asked figure of speech.
  • Treating the poem literally as being about houseplants rather than as a comment on deforestation.

SHORTS23 marks

2. Explain the comparison of the boughs to newly discharged patients.

Show Answer

Ans:

The boughs have been cramped under the roof of the house for a long time, and as they move out they shuffle like patients who have just been discharged from a clinic — half dazed, unsteady and unused to the open air. The simile shows how long the trees have been imprisoned and how difficult it is for them to become free again.

SHORTS53 marks

5. Describe the movement of the trees out of the house.

Show Answer

Ans:

The trees inside the house strain at the windows and the doors, and their roots work all night to disengage themselves from the cracks in the veranda floor.
Their leaves move towards the glass, and the small twigs stiffen with exertion while the long, broken branches are relieved of the weight of the roof.
The poet says she sits inside, and the doors open and the huge trees move out, like newly discharged patients half-dazed, moving to the clinic doors.
They leave behind the broken glass, the smell of leaves and lichen, and the moonlight that had been trapped in the house.

LONGL84 marks

8. What is the poet's message about nature in ‘The Trees'?

Show Answer

Ans:

Nature cannot be kept indoors for decoration; the trees inside the house are as uncomfortable as caged animals.
Their escape is an assertion that the forest, not the drawing room, is where they belong, and that nature will reclaim what has been taken from it.
Adrienne Rich also suggests that the unnatural division between the human world and the natural world cannot last.
By ending with the empty forest waiting to be filled, she hints that the natural world is diminished by our attempts to possess it.

LONGL95 marks

9. What message does the poem convey about human beings and nature?

Show Answer

Ans:

The poem shows that human beings have removed nature from its natural home and confined it to houses, verandas and gardens, so that the forest outside is left empty and lifeless. Without trees there are no birds, no insects and no shade.
But the trees do not accept this imprisonment. All night their roots work to disengage themselves from the cracks in the veranda floor, their leaves strain towards the glass, and their twigs stiffen with exertion. Slowly, painfully, they move out and stumble forward into the forest.
The poet watches them from inside, writing long letters and hardly mentioning their departure — which suggests that human beings know what they have done and prefer not to speak of it.
The breaking of the moon like a mirror, its pieces flashing in the crown of the tallest oak, marks the moment the trees return to the wild. The message is that nature cannot be permanently confined, and that human beings who destroy forests are left with an empty world — but nature itself will find its way back.
