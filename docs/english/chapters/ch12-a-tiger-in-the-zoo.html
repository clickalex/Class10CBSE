Chapter 12 · A Tiger in the Zoo

Chapter 12 of 33. Two settings, one contrast: the cage and the wild. The pairs of images are what is examined.

🎯 Marks lens

Reference-to-context extracts, usually the first stanza (the cage) or the last (the stars).
Know the poet (Leslie Norris), the watering hole, the plump deer, and the contrast between the tiger's rage and his quiet steps.

📖 Deep concepts

1. The poem. The tiger in the zoo stalks the few steps of his cage in quiet rage; his strength is locked in a cage, so he ignores visitors. The poet then imagines where the tiger should be — lurking in the shadow, sliding through long grass near the watering hole where plump deer pass. He should be lurking in shadow, sliding through long grass near the water hole, where plump deer pass. In the last stanza the tiger is locked in a concrete cell, his strength behind bars, and he stares with his brilliant eyes at the brilliant stars.
2. Central idea. The poem is a protest against keeping wild animals in captivity. In the zoo the tiger is confined to a few steps, his strength useless, his rage quiet because there is nothing he can do. In the wild he would be a hunter, terrifying the village at the jungle's edge. The last image — the tiger staring at the stars from a concrete cell — sums up the tragedy: he is alive but not free.
3. Poetic devices. Contrast — the cage and the forest. Alliteration — 'plump deer', 'brilliant eyes', 'brilliant stars', 'quiet rage'. Repetition — 'brilliant' is repeated to link the tiger's eyes and the stars. Personification — the tiger's rage is described as if it were a companion. Imagery — vivid visual images of both settings.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Two settings, two lists. Cage: few steps, concrete cell, bars, visitors, stare at the stars. Forest: shadow, long grass, watering hole, plump deer, terrorise the village.
  • 'Quiet rage' is an oxymoron — the rage is there but cannot be expressed. Examiners look for that term.
  • The repeated word 'brilliant' links the tiger to the freedom of the sky.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Saying the tiger attacks the visitors. He ignores them.
  • Describing the deer as a herd. The poem says plump deer.
  • Missing the last line entirely — the tiger stares at the brilliant stars, which is the poem's central image.

SHORTS13 marks

1. How does the poet contrast the tiger in the cage with the tiger in the wild?

Show Answer

Ans:

In the cage the tiger stalks only the few steps of his enclosure, in quiet rage, with his strength locked behind bars and his attention turned away from the visitors. In the wild he would lurk in shadow, slide through the long grass near the watering hole where plump deer pass, and snarl around houses at the jungle's edge, baring his white fangs and claws and terrorising the village. The contrast is between useless confinement and natural power.

SHORTS23 marks

2. Explain the meaning of 'quiet rage'.

Show Answer

Ans:

It is an oxymoron. The tiger's rage is intense but silent, because in the cage there is nothing he can do about it. He cannot hunt, cannot roam and cannot express his anger, so the rage stays inside him and becomes quiet. The phrase captures the whole tragedy of captivity in two words.

SHORTS33 marks

3. What is the significance of the last stanza?

Show Answer

Ans:

The tiger is locked in a concrete cell, his strength behind bars, and he stalks the length of his cage ignoring visitors. At night he stares with his brilliant eyes at the brilliant stars. The repetition of 'brilliant' links the tiger to the vast, free sky that he can only look at. The stanza shows that captivity has taken away everything except the longing for freedom.
