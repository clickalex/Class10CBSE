Chapter 23 · A Question of Trust

Chapter 23 of 33. A burglar out-burgled. The woman in red is one of the great reveals in the supplementary reader.

Open this chapter’s questions + MCQs →

🎯 Marks lens 📖 Deep concepts 🛠️ Steps 🧠 Tricks ⚠️ Mistakes ❓ Exam Q&A ✍️ Task

🎯 Marks lens

Short answers and a value-based question on whether the lady was right to trick Horace.
Horace Danby, Shotover Grange, and the woman in red are the key names.

📖 Deep concepts

1. Summary. Horace Danby was a good and respectable man, about fifty and unmarried, who made locks and was successful enough to have two people help him. But he was not completely honest: he robbed a safe every year, planning each robbery carefully, so that the money lasted twelve months. He robbed only rich people, and never robbed anyone poor. This year he had planned to rob the safe at Shotover Grange, which contained about fifteen thousand pounds' worth of jewels. He had studied the house for two weeks and knew that the family was in London. He entered, cut the wires of the burglar alarm, and found the safe — but it had a lock he had not seen before. As he worked, a young woman dressed in red appeared, saying she had come home just in time. She spoke as the lady of the house, said her husband was away and that the jewels were needed for a party that night, and asked Horace to open the safe for her, saying she had forgotten the number. She also threatened to call the police. Horace, afraid of prison, opened the safe and gave her the jewels, and left without his gloves. Two days later he was arrested, because he had left his fingerprints all over the room. In prison he is the assistant librarian, and he is angry when anyone mentions honour among thieves.
2. Characters. Horace Danby — respectable, careful, a lover of rare and expensive books, and a thief only once a year. The young woman in red — calm, confident, quick-thinking, and herself a burglar who had been waiting for exactly this opportunity.
3. Theme. Honour among thieves does not exist; and overconfidence makes even a careful man careless.

🛠️ Method, step by step

  1. Open with Horace Danby — a respectful, successful locksmith with a criminal hobby, planning the robbery at Shotover Grange.
  2. Give the plan and the execution: the dogs shut away, the safe in the drawing room, the very good lock that takes him too long.
  3. Show how the woman in red outwits him — she borrows the safe's keys, and he leaves his fingerprints for the police.
  4. Close with the irony: the jewellery is recovered from a theft that was never reported, and Horace is left wondering whether she was the family or a cleverer thief.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • He forgot his gloves because he believed her story — that detail explains the arrest.
  • The woman's trick worked because she pretended to be in trouble herself and asked for help.
  • The last line — 'honour among thieves' — is the standard closing point of any long answer.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Saying the woman was the owner's wife. She was another thief, who had planned to rob the same house.
  • Saying Horace was arrested at the house. He was arrested two days later, because of the fingerprints.
  • Describing Horace as a habitual criminal. He robbed once a year and was otherwise respectable.

SHORTS23 marks

2. Why was Horace arrested?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because he had left his fingerprints all over the room. When the young woman told him her story and asked him to open the safe, he was so relieved and so eager to escape that he forgot to put on his gloves. Two days later the police arrested him, using the fingerprints as evidence.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Do you think the young lady was right in tricking Horace? Give reasons.

Show Answer

Ans:

No. She used another person's crime to cover her own, and she let an innocent-seeming man take the blame. Although Horace was himself a thief, she could have simply left the house instead of framing him. Her action shows that 'honour among thieves' does not exist — she was willing to send him to prison to protect herself and keep the jewels.

SHORTS63 marks

6. How is Horace Danby trapped by the young woman at Shotover Grange?

Show Answer

Ans:

She finds him unlocking the door, and calmly claims to be the lady of the house who has forgotten her jewels upstairs.
Horace helps her open the door, and she watches him take the jewels from the safe and lets him go with them.
Because she saw him handle the safe, her fingerprints are not needed: he has left a clean set of prints all over the house.
The police find his fingerprints and arrest him, while the real thief, who had read the family's letters in the hall, escapes.

SHORTS73 marks

7. Why did Horace Danby rob only once a year, and what did he do with the money?

Show Answer

Ans:

Horace was otherwise a good, respectable locksmith who loved rare and expensive books but could not afford them honestly. So he robbed one safe a year — carefully planned, always enough to last twelve months — and spent the money secretly through an agent on first editions for his library. Crime, for him, was a yearly subscription to his hobby.

LONGL104 marks

10. What is the irony in ‘A Question of Trust'?

Show Answer

Ans:

Horace Danby is a respectable locksmith and a successful crook who plans his robberies carefully for a year.
['The young woman who tricks him is not a lady but a thief who does no planning at all: she simply reads the letters in the hall, walks in and walks out with the jewels.', 'Horace is caught by his own fingerprints while she leaves none, because she wore gloves and was never seen as a thief.', "The title's irony is double: Horace trusted the wrong person, and the police trust only the evidence, so the guiltiest person goes free."]

APPLICATIONA114 marks

11. ‘Honour among thieves does not exist.’ How does the ending prove this? Do you feel any sympathy for Horace?

Show Answer

Ans:

The young woman in red is herself a thief; she uses Horace to open the safe, takes the jewels, and leaves him to be caught by his own fingerprints. One thief betrays another without a second thought.
Some sympathy is natural — Horace is polite, careful and robbed by a cleverer criminal — but the story withholds full pity: he chose crime freely, and a thief complaining of being robbed is the joke the title promises.
