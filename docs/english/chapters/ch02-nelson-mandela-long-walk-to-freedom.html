Chapter 2 · Nelson Mandela: Long Walk to Freedom

🎯 Marks lens

Reference-to-context extracts and one 5-mark long answer on the meaning of freedom or on courage.
10 May 1994 — the inauguration at the Union Buildings amphitheatre in Pretoria — is the anchor date.
Know the three national anthems, the meaning of apartheid, and the two obligations.

📖 Deep concepts

1. Summary. On 10 May 1994, Nelson Mandela was sworn in as South Africa's first black President at the Union Buildings amphitheatre in Pretoria, before an international gathering of leaders. The day marked the end of apartheid — a political system that separated people according to race — and the birth of a new, democratic, non-racial nation. Three national anthems were sung: 'Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika' and 'Morena' by the blacks, 'Die Stem' the old anthem of the Republic, and the new combined anthem. Mandela reflects on the day: a beautiful autumn day, but for the whites a symbol of victory and for the blacks a symbol of a new beginning. He recalls his own journey from a frightened young man to a bold one, and thanks the international presence, which he sees as a common victory for justice, peace and human dignity.
2. Courage and oppression. Mandela says that he learned that courage was not the absence of fear but the triumph over it — the brave man is not he who does not feel afraid, but he who conquers that fear. Decades of oppression made him hunger for freedom, not just for himself but for all his people. He says that the oppressed and the oppressor alike are robbed of their humanity: the oppressor is a prisoner of hatred, locked behind the bars of prejudice and narrow-mindedness, and is not truly free; the oppressed, robbed of his dignity, is also not free.
3. The twin obligations. Every man has two obligations: the first to his family — his parents, his wife and children; the second to his people, his community and his country. In a civil and humane society each man is able to fulfil both. In South Africa under apartheid, a man like Mandela who tried to live as a human being was punished and isolated, and could not fulfil either obligation.
4. The meaning of freedom. As a boy, freedom meant running in the fields, swimming in the stream and roasting mealies under the stars. As a student, it meant staying out at night, reading what he pleased and going where he liked — transitory freedoms. As a young man he yearned for the honourable freedoms of achieving his potential, of earning his keep, of marrying and having a family. Only later did he understand that freedom is indivisible — the chains on any one of his people were chains on all of them. He says that freedom was not just to throw off one's chains, but to live in a way that respects and enhances the freedom of others. His long walk is not yet ended.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Courage = triumph over fear, not absence of fear. That sentence is asked repeatedly and must be quoted nearly word-for-word.
  • Two obligations: family and people. In apartheid South Africa, a man could not discharge both.
  • Freedom changed meaning with age: boyhood = physical freedom, youth = honourable freedoms, later = freedom of the whole people.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Giving the inauguration date as 10 May 1995. It is 1994.
  • Saying South Africa has two national anthems. Three were sung that day.
  • Defining apartheid as segregation only. The textbook calls it a political system that separates people according to race.

❓ Exam Q&A

3 marks What does courage mean to Mandela?

For Mandela, courage is not the absence of fear but the triumph over it. The brave man is not the one who does not feel afraid, but the one who conquers that fear. He says he felt fear more times than he can remember, and it was this repeated conquest of fear that made him bold.

3 marks What 'twin obligations' does Mandela mention?

The first obligation is to his family — his parents, his wife and children. The second is to his people, his community and his country. In a civil and humane society each person can fulfil both, but in South Africa under apartheid, a man like Mandela who tried to live as a human being was punished and isolated, and could not discharge either obligation.

5 marks How did Mandela's understanding of freedom change with age and experience?

As a boy in the villages of Umtata, freedom meant being able to run in the fields near his mother's hut, to swim in the stream, to roast mealies under the stars and to ride the broad backs of slow-moving bulls. It was the freedom of a child, and it seemed boundless.
As a student he wanted the transitory freedoms of staying out at night, reading what he pleased and going where he liked.
As a young man he yearned for the honourable freedoms — of achieving his potential, of earning his keep, of marrying and having a family, of living lawfully in a law-abiding society.
Later he realised that these were only illusory, because he was not free and neither were his people. He came to understand that freedom is indivisible: the chains on any one of his people were chains on all of them. He saw that the oppressed and the oppressor alike are robbed of their humanity, and that freedom is not merely to throw off one's chains but to live in a way that respects and enhances the freedom of others.

3 marks Why did such a large number of international leaders attend the inauguration? What did it signify?

It signified the recognition by the international community of the birth of a free, democratic, non-racial South Africa, and a common victory for justice, for peace and for human dignity. The presence of leaders of many nations showed that the end of apartheid was not only a South African event but a triumph of values shared by the whole world.

3 marks Why does Mandela say that the oppressor is also not free?

Because the oppressor is a prisoner of hatred, locked behind the bars of prejudice and narrow-mindedness. A man who takes away another man's freedom is himself deprived of his own humanity, so the oppressor and the oppressed are both robbed of what makes them human.

✍️ Hands-on task

Write the four stages of Mandela's idea of freedom as four headed sentences (boyhood, student, young man, mature). Practise it once from memory — this is a guaranteed long answer and the headings alone carry two marks.
