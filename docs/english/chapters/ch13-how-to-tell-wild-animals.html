Chapter 13 · How to Tell Wild Animals

Chapter 13 of 33. A comic poem with a grim joke in every stanza. Learn the six animals and how each 'identifies' you.

🎯 Marks lens

The six animals: Asian lion, Bengal tiger, leopard, bear, crocodile, hyena — and the chameleon at the end.

📖 Deep concepts

1. The poem. The poet gives an amusing method of telling one wild animal from another — by letting each of them attack you. If a large tawny beast in the east roars at you so that you feel you will die, it is an Asian lion. If the beast has a hide with black stripes on a yellow ground and it only needs to roar to know it is a Bengal tiger, and if it eats you, you can be quite sure it is a tiger. If a creature walks towards your home, and it has a hide with spots that pepper it, and it leaps on you, it is a leopard — and it will do no good to roar with pain; it will only leap again. If a creature in your yard hugs you very, very hard, be sure it is a bear; if you have any doubts, it gives you just one more caress. If a creature has no ears but has eyes, and it is a crocodile — you will know it by its eyes and by its tears, for a crocodile weeps before it eats its prey. A hyena comes with merry smiles, but if they weep, it is the crocodile. The last stanza describes the chameleon, a small lizard-like creature with no wings and no ears, which, if there is nothing on the tree, is the chameleon you see.
2. Central idea. The poem is a piece of light verse that makes fun of field guides which claim to make identification easy. The humour lies in the grim practicality of the advice: the only sure way to identify a wild animal is to be attacked by it, and by then it is too late. It is also a gentle satire on human confidence in knowing and naming the natural world.
3. Poetic devices. Humour and irony — the 'identification' is fatal. Rhyme scheme — ababcc, a light and bouncy pattern. Alliteration — 'Bengal tiger', 'a novice might nonplus'. Repetition — the repeated pattern of each stanza. Enjambment — lines run on without a pause, giving the poem its speed.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Six animals in order: Asian lion, Bengal tiger, leopard, bear, crocodile, hyena — then the chameleon.
  • Bear = hug; crocodile = tears; hyena = smile. Three pairings that answer most extract questions.
  • The joke is that the reader is the bait — say so in any question about the humour.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Confusing the hyena's smile with the crocodile's tears. The hyena comes with merry smiles; the crocodile weeps.
  • Naming the leopard's markings as stripes. It has spots that pepper its hide; stripes belong to the Bengal tiger.
  • Treating the poem as serious natural history.

SHORTS23 marks

2. What is the humour in the poem?

Show Answer

Ans:

The humour comes from the poet's pretended practical advice: the only reliable way to identify a wild animal is to be attacked by it, and by the time you are certain, you are being eaten. The advice is useless to the reader, who is meant to be the bait. The poet also plays with the animals' characteristics — the bear's hug, the crocodile's tears, the hyena's smile — turning well-known sayings into a comic method of identification.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Explain the lines about the crocodile and the hyena.

Show Answer

Ans:

The poet plays on two popular beliefs. A crocodile is said to weep before it eats its prey, hence 'crocodile tears'; so if a creature weeps, it is a crocodile. A hyena is supposed to laugh, so the hyena 'comes with merry smiles'. By contrasting weeping with smiling, the poet gives the reader an amusing rule for telling the two apart — a rule that would only be tested at the cost of one's life.
