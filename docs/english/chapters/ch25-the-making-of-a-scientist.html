Chapter 25 · The Making of a Scientist

Chapter 25 of 33. A true account of how a scientist is made — curiosity, competition and a supportive parent.

Open this chapter’s questions + MCQs →

🎯 Marks lens 📖 Deep concepts 🛠️ Steps 🧠 Tricks ⚠️ Mistakes ❓ Exam Q&A ✍️ Task

🎯 Marks lens

Short answers and a long answer on the qualities that make a scientist.
Richard Ebright, the book The Travels of Monarch X, and the twelve tiny gold spots on a monarch pupa are the key details.

📖 Deep concepts

1. Summary. Richard Ebright grew up in Reading, Pennsylvania, an only child whose father died when he was in third grade. His mother was his only companion and encouraged his learning: she took him on trips, bought him scientific equipment and gave him challenges. She gave him a children's book, The Travels of Monarch X, which opened the world of science to him; it was written by James R. Wong, and it described the migration of monarch butterflies to Central America. The book led him to raise butterflies in his basement, to tag them, and to write to the author, who sent him more suggestions. In the seventh grade he entered a county science fair with a project on monarch pupae, showing that the twelve tiny gold spots on a monarch pupa were not ornamental but were used to produce a hormone necessary for the butterfly's full development — he won, and was taken to the National Insect Collection. Later he discovered the purpose of the gold spots, worked on how cells work, and read the chemical structure of a hormone, which he and his college roommate James R. Wong described in a paper published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. He also became a champion debater and public speaker, a good canoeist and an expert photographer of nature and scientific exhibits. His teacher, Richard A. Weiherer, opened his mind to ideas and taught him to compete — not for the sake of winning, but to do the best job he could. Ebright says that his mother taught him that he must work hard to succeed, and that his interest in science came from her encouragement.
2. Characters. Richard Ebright — curious, persistent, competitive and well organised. His mother — the person who encouraged him, bought him equipment and gave him work to do. Richard A. Weiherer — his teacher, who opened his mind and taught him healthy competition. James R. Wong — the author of the book and later his co-researcher.
3. Theme. A scientist is made by curiosity, a competitive spirit in the right sense, and the will to do the best work — plus someone who encourages them.

🛠️ Method, step by step

  1. Name the subject and the method the chapter follows — Richard Ebright's life from butterfly collector to molecular biologist.
  2. List the turning points in order: the Book of Butterflies, the science fairs, the tagged butterflies, and the work on the monarch's chemical.
  3. Show the people around him — his mother, who bought the telescope and the book, and Dr Urquhart, who set him the real problem.
  4. Close with what makes a scientist: a first-rate mind, curiosity, and the will to win for the right reasons.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Three qualities: curiosity, competitive spirit, first-rate mind. Quote the textbook phrase if you can.
  • The twelve gold spots produce a hormone — that discovery is the turning point of his career.
  • His mother is the single most important influence; do not leave her out.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Saying his teacher encouraged him to compete in order to win. Weiherer taught him to compete to do the best job he could.
  • Forgetting the mother. She is the answer to almost every 'who influenced him' question.
  • Saying the gold spots were decorative. They produce a hormone necessary for full development.

1. How did Richard Ebright's mother help him in his work?

Show Answer

Ans:

She was his only companion after his father's death, and she encouraged his learning in every way. She took him on trips, bought him telescopes, microscopes, cameras and other scientific equipment, and always found work for him to do. Most importantly she gave him the children's book The Travels of Monarch X, which opened the world of science to him and started him on his work with butterflies.

SHORTS23 marks

2. What did Ebright discover about the twelve gold spots on a monarch pupa?

Show Answer

Ans:

He found that the twelve tiny gold spots on a monarch pupa were not merely ornamental, as was thought, but produced a hormone necessary for the full development of the butterfly. He demonstrated this by removing the spots and showing the effect, and the discovery won him the county science fair and a place at the National Insect Collection.

SHORTS33 marks

3. What qualities go into the making of a scientist?

Show Answer

Ans:

According to the chapter, three things are needed: a first-rate mind, curiosity, and the will to win for the right reasons — not merely to win, but to do the best job one can. Richard Ebright had all three, and he also had the advantage of a mother who encouraged him and a teacher who opened his mind to ideas and taught him to compete in a healthy way.

SHORTS63 marks

6. What did Richard Ebright learn from his monarch butterfly research?

Show Answer

Ans:

He began by tagging monarch butterflies to track their migration to Mexico and beyond.
He discovered what caused a viral disease that kills nearly all monarchs, and later, with a friend, he found that the gold spots on the pupa are not decorative.
The spots produce a hormone needed for the full development of the butterfly.
This work won him the Searle scholarship, the Schering Plough award and a place in the Intel Science Talent Search.

SHORTS73 marks

7. How did Ebright's failure at the county science fair in seventh grade help him later?

Show Answer

Ans:

Young Ebright exhibited slides of frog tissues — neat work, but only display, not experiment — and won nothing. The failure taught him what real science needs: a question, an experiment and an answer. Thereafter he did genuine investigations, starting with the viral disease of monarch caterpillars, and began winning every fair he entered.

LONGL104 marks

10. What qualities made Richard Ebright a scientist?

Show Answer

Ans:

He had first-rate curiosity and a mind that never stopped: he collected butterflies, rocks, fossils and coins from childhood.
He was competitive in the best sense — when he lost a county science fair, he resolved to do a real experiment the next year.
He was a hard worker and a careful observer, and he had the drive to write to a top scientist, which led to work at a laboratory.
Most of all, he had a mother who encouraged him, teachers and mentors who took him seriously, and the imagination to ask why.

APPLICATIONA114 marks

11. ‘A scientist needs a first-rate mind, curiosity and the will to win for the right reasons.’ Show how Ebright's life illustrates each of these three.

Show Answer

Ans:

A first-rate mind — he designed real experiments, like proving the gold spots produce a hormone, and published in a scientific journal while still at school.
Curiosity — from collecting butterflies and rocks as a child to chasing the question of cell life, he never stopped asking why.
The will to win for the right reasons — his teacher Weiherer taught him to compete to do the best job he could, not merely to beat others.
