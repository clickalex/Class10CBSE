MCQs · Drill

MCQM11 mark

1. 'The Trees' is written by: (a) Robin Klein (b) Adrienne Rich (c) Leslie Norris (d) Carolyn Wells

Show Answer

Ans:

(b) Adrienne Rich.

MCQM21 mark

2. The long-cramped boughs are compared to: (a) prisoners escaping (b) birds returning (c) newly discharged patients (d) children running

Show Answer

Ans:

(c) newly discharged patients, half dazed, moving to the clinic doors.

MCQM31 mark

3. In the last stanza, the moon is described as: (a) full and bright (b) broken like a mirror (c) hidden behind clouds (d) setting

Show Answer

Ans:

(b) broken like a mirror, its pieces flashing in the crown of the tallest oak.

MCQM41 mark

4. What do the branches press toward as the trees struggle to leave the house? (a) The fireplace (b) The ceiling fan (c) The glass (d) A bookshelf

Show Answer

Ans:

(c) The glass. The branches strain toward the window as the trees seek a way out.

MCQM51 mark

5. When are the trees' roots described as working to free themselves? (a) All night (b) At noon (c) At sunrise only (d) During the autumn harvest

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) All night. The poem gives the roots a sustained, determined effort to disengage from the floor.

MCQM61 mark

6. In ‘The Trees', the trees move out because: (a) they are being cut (b) the house is too small (c) winter is coming (d) the forest is empty and they belong there

Show Answer

Ans:

(d) the forest is empty and they belong there. Their natural home has been emptied of trees.

MCQM71 mark

7. The poet of ‘The Trees' is: (a) Robin Klein (b) Leslie Norris (c) Carolyn Wells (d) Adrienne Rich

Show Answer

Ans:

(d) Adrienne Rich. The American poet often wrote about freedom and the natural world.

MCQM81 mark

8. The roots of the trees try to disengage themselves from: (a) cracks in the veranda floor (b) the forest soil (c) flower pots (d) the garden path

Show Answer

Ans:

(a) cracks in the veranda floor. Even the house floor cannot hold the roots back.

Written questions · Write

SHORTS13 marks

1. Why does the poet say the forest outside is empty?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because all the trees have been brought inside — into houses, verandas and pots — so there are none left in the forest. With no trees there are no birds to sit, no insects to hide and no sun to bury its feet in the shadow of leaves. The forest is empty all day, which shows how completely human beings have taken nature away from it.

SHORTS23 marks

2. Explain the comparison of the boughs to newly discharged patients.

Show Answer

Ans:

The boughs have been cramped under the roof of the house for a long time, and as they move out they shuffle like patients who have just been discharged from a clinic — half dazed, unsteady and unused to the open air. The simile shows how long the trees have been imprisoned and how difficult it is for them to become free again.

APPLICATIONA33 marks

3. A reader says “The Trees” is only a description of houseplants being moved outdoors. Explain how the poem's images make the movement mean more than a gardening task.

Show Answer

Ans:

The trees are given purposeful, almost human actions: roots struggle free and branches reach toward the open space.
The forest outside is described as empty while the trees crowd the house, reversing the natural order.
Their movement can therefore suggest nature reclaiming freedom after being confined by people, not merely a change of pots.

APPLICATIONA43 marks

4. A school is planning to remove several trees from a paved courtyard. Use the poem's movement from house to forest to explain why the trees should not be treated as decoration alone.

Show Answer

Ans:

The poem imagines trees as living beings whose roots and branches need room beyond the human space that contains them.
In the poem, the empty forest waits for the trees, suggesting that nature has its own place and cannot be reduced to indoor ornament.

SHORTS53 marks

5. Describe the movement of the trees out of the house.

Show Answer

Ans:

The trees inside the house strain at the windows and the doors, and their roots work all night to disengage themselves from the cracks in the veranda floor.
Their leaves move towards the glass, and the small twigs stiffen with exertion while the long, broken branches are relieved of the weight of the roof.
The poet says she sits inside, and the doors open and the huge trees move out, like newly discharged patients half-dazed, moving to the clinic doors.
They leave behind the broken glass, the smell of leaves and lichen, and the moonlight that had been trapped in the house.

SHORTS63 marks

6. What happens to the moon in the last stanza, and what effect does this image create?

Show Answer

Ans:

The moon ‘breaks like a mirror’ and its pieces flash in the crown of the tallest oak. The image suggests a world cracking open as the trees return to the forest — even the sky cannot stay whole. It is the poem's most dramatic picture of nature breaking out of the prison human beings built for it.

COMPETENCYC74 marks

7. Read and answer: The roots strain to free themselves from the floor, while branches press toward the glass. By night, the trees begin to move out of the house; the forest outside is empty and waiting. (a) What conflict is suggested by the roots and branches? (b) Why is the empty forest significant? (c) What larger idea can the movement represent?

Show Answer

Ans:

The roots and branches struggle against the restrictions of the house and glass; the trees are trying to return to open space.
The forest is their natural home, but it has been emptied while the trees are kept inside human spaces.
The movement can symbolise nature resisting confinement and reclaiming a place from which human activity has displaced it.

COMPETENCYC84 marks

8. Read and answer: The trees leave the house at night. In the morning, the speaker's long letters are finished and the forest is no longer empty. The moon appears broken in the glass. (a) What change has taken place outside? (b) What might the broken-mirror image suggest? (c) Why does the speaker notice the trees' departure through the house?

Show Answer

Ans:

The trees have moved from the house back toward the forest, so the formerly empty forest is being filled again.
The broken-mirror image makes the moon seem fragmented and reflects the disturbance or transformation as the trees escape.
The speaker is inside observing the change; the glass and open spaces mark the boundary between human control and the natural world.

LONGL94 marks

9. What is the poet's message about nature in ‘The Trees'?

Show Answer

Ans:

Nature cannot be kept indoors for decoration; the trees inside the house are as uncomfortable as caged animals.
Their escape is an assertion that the forest, not the drawing room, is where they belong, and that nature will reclaim what has been taken from it.
Adrienne Rich also suggests that the unnatural division between the human world and the natural world cannot last.
By ending with the empty forest waiting to be filled, she hints that the natural world is diminished by our attempts to possess it.

APPLICATIONA104 marks

10. ‘The night is fresh, the whole moon shines in a sky still open.’ How does this opening contrast with the empty forest, and what does the contrast suggest?

Show Answer

Ans:

The sky is fresh and open while the forest is empty — no birds, no insects, no shade — because the trees are still locked inside the house.
The contrast suggests that one part of nature is waiting: the sky is ready, the forest is ready, only the trees' return is missing.
It frames the whole poem as a homecoming — nature is about to become whole again.

LONGL115 marks

11. What message does the poem convey about human beings and nature?

Show Answer

Ans:

The poem shows that human beings have removed nature from its natural home and confined it to houses, verandas and gardens, so that the forest outside is left empty and lifeless. Without trees there are no birds, no insects and no shade.
But the trees do not accept this imprisonment. All night their roots work to disengage themselves from the cracks in the veranda floor, their leaves strain towards the glass, and their twigs stiffen with exertion. Slowly, painfully, they move out and stumble forward into the forest.
The poet watches them from inside, writing long letters and hardly mentioning their departure — which suggests that human beings know what they have done and prefer not to speak of it.
The breaking of the moon like a mirror, its pieces flashing in the crown of the tallest oak, marks the moment the trees return to the wild. The message is that nature cannot be permanently confined, and that human beings who destroy forests are left with an empty world — but nature itself will find its way back.

LONGL125 marks

12. How does Adrienne Rich use the trees' movement to question the human attempt to confine nature?

Show Answer

Ans:

At the beginning, trees occupy the house while the forest outside is empty, an unsettling reversal of where they belong.
Roots strain against the floor and branches reach toward the glass, giving the trees a strong desire to escape the limits people have imposed.
Their night-time movement restores them to the forest. The poem treats this not as a tidy relocation but as nature's return to an open space.
The speaker's position inside the house and the broken image of the moon in the glass underline the separation between human enclosure and the freedom the trees seek.
