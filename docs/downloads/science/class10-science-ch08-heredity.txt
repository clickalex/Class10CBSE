A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (14)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 1 mark] What is the sex chromosome combination in a human male, and what is the functional unit of inheritance?
    Ans: XY; the gene (a segment of DNA on a chromosome).

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] A man with blood group A marries a woman with blood group O and their daughter has blood group O. Is this enough to tell which trait is dominant? Why?
    Ans: No. From one family we cannot tell. If A were dominant, the father could be heterozygous (IAi) and the daughter could inherit i from both parents; but a single observation does not establish dominance, which requires studying many such crosses.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] Distinguish between acquired and inherited traits, with one example of each.
    Ans: An acquired trait develops during an organism's lifetime due to experience or environment and is not passed on — for example, a wrestler's well-developed muscles or a pierced ear. An inherited trait is present from birth and is passed on through the DNA of germ cells — for example, the colour of eyes or free ear lobes.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] Distinguish between genotype and phenotype with an example.
    Ans: Genotype is the genetic constitution of an organism (for example Tt), while phenotype is the observable character it produces (for example a tall plant). Because the tall allele is dominant, the genotypes TT and Tt both give the tall phenotype.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] Explain Mendel's monohybrid cross with a Punnett square.
    Ans:
      Pure tall (TT) × pure dwarf (tt). Gametes: T and t. F1: all Tt — tall, because tall is dominant and dwarf is recessive.
      Selfing F1 (Tt × Tt) gives gametes T and t from each parent. Punnett square → TT, Tt, Tt, tt.
      Phenotype: 3 tall : 1 dwarf. Genotype: 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt. The dwarf character was only masked in F1, not lost.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] How is the sex of a child determined in human beings? Show diagrammatically.
    Ans:
      Mother (XX) produces only X-bearing eggs; father (XY) produces X-bearing and Y-bearing sperms in equal numbers.
      X egg + X sperm → XX = girl; X egg + Y sperm → XY = boy.
      The probability of either is 50%, so the sex of the child is decided by chance and not by either parent's wish or action.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] In a cross between two F₁ pea plants (Tt × Tt), a student writes: “The ratio of tall to dwarf plants is 1 : 2 : 1.” What is the mistake? Write the correct phenotypic and genotypic ratios.
    Ans:
      The ratio 1 : 2 : 1 is the genotypic ratio (TT : Tt : tt), not the ratio of tall to dwarf plants.
      Both TT and Tt plants look tall, so the phenotypic ratio is tall : dwarf = 3 : 1.
      Genotype is the gene combination (TT, Tt, tt); phenotype is the visible trait (tall or dwarf).

Q8. [Application · 3 marks] In humans, free earlobes (F) are dominant over attached earlobes (f). A man with free earlobes, whose mother had attached earlobes, marries a woman with attached earlobes. What kinds of earlobes can their children have, and in what proportion?
    Ans:
      The man’s mother was ff, so he received f from her; he has free earlobes, so he is Ff. The woman is ff.
      Cross Ff × ff: gametes F, f from the man and only f from the woman.
      Children: 1 Ff : 1 ff, i.e. 50% free earlobes and 50% attached earlobes.

Q9. [Short · 3 marks] A tall pea plant (Tt) is crossed with a dwarf plant (tt). What proportion of the progeny will be tall?
    Ans:
      The tall parent produces two kinds of gametes, T and t; the dwarf parent produces only t.
      Combining them gives Tt, Tt, tt and tt.
      So half the progeny are Tt (tall) and half are tt (dwarf) — a 1 : 1 ratio. This cross with a recessive parent is called a test cross.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:

    A population of red beetles lives on green bushes, and crows easily spot and eat the red beetles. In one reproductive event, a variation appears and a green beetle is born.

    (a) Why is the green beetle more likely to survive?
    (b) What happens to the colour of the beetle population over several generations?
    (c) Does this variation help the individual only, or the species? Explain.
    Ans:
      (a) Its green colour camouflages it against the green leaves, so crows find it harder to see.
      (b) Green beetles survive and breed, passing the colour to their offspring, so their number grows each generation while the red beetles decline.
      (c) It helps the species: because the variation is inherited, it spreads and increases the chance that the population survives the crows.

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:

    A student crosses pure tall pea plants with pure dwarf pea plants. All F₁ plants are tall. When the F₁ plants are self-pollinated, the F₂ generation has 600 tall and 200 dwarf plants.

    (a) What is the ratio of tall to dwarf plants in F₂?
    (b) Which trait is recessive, and why was it not seen in F₁?
    (c) What does the result show about how traits are inherited?
    Ans:
      (a) 600 : 200 = 3 : 1.
      (b) Dwarfness is recessive. In F₁ every plant is Tt, and the dominant allele T masks t, so all look tall.
      (c) Each parent passes one copy of the factor for a trait; the factors do not blend, and the recessive trait reappears in F₂ when two copies of it come together.

Q12. [Application · 4 marks] A family blames the mother whenever a daughter is born. Using what you know about sex determination, write the reply you would give them.
    Ans:
      Human females have two X chromosomes, so every egg carries an X chromosome.
      Human males have one X and one Y, so half their sperm carry X and half carry Y.
      An X-bearing sperm fertilising the egg gives XX, a girl; a Y-bearing sperm gives XY, a boy. The egg always contributes an X, so it is the sperm — that is, the father — that decides the sex of the child.
      Repeated daughters in a family are a matter of chance, exactly like repeated tails when a coin is tossed; there is no biological basis for blaming the mother.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] Describe Mendel's dihybrid cross and state the conclusion drawn from it.
    Ans:
      He crossed pea plants with round-yellow seeds (RRYY) with plants having wrinkled-green seeds (rryy).
      F1: all plants had round-yellow seeds (RrYy) — round and yellow are dominant.
      On self-pollinating F1, F2 gave four phenotypes: 9 round-yellow : 3 round-green : 3 wrinkled-yellow : 1 wrinkled-green (16 combinations in the 4×4 Punnett square).
      Conclusion: the two pairs of characters are inherited independently of each other, which is why new combinations (round-green and wrinkled-green) appear in F2 that were absent in the parents. This is the law of independent assortment.

Q14. [Long · 5 marks] How do variations arise in sexually reproducing organisms? Explain with examples why variation is important for the survival of a species.
    Ans:
      Copying of DNA during reproduction is never perfectly accurate, so small differences (variations) arise between parent and offspring.
      In sexual reproduction the DNA of two different parents is combined, which produces much more variation than copying one parent alone.
      Some variations help an organism in a changed environment. For example, when a heat wave kills most bacteria in a pond, the few heat-tolerant ones survive and multiply.
      Similarly, a green beetle on green bushes escapes crows and leaves more offspring than a red beetle.
      A population with no variation could be wiped out by one change in its surroundings, so variation gives a species a chance to survive.

B. MCQs (8)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. In a monohybrid cross between a tall (TT) and a dwarf (tt) pea plant, the F2 phenotypic ratio is: (a) 1:1 (b) 9:3:3:1 (c) 3:1 (d) 1:2:1
    Ans: (c) 3 tall : 1 dwarf. 1:2:1 is the genotypic ratio of the same cross.

M2. The sex of a human child is determined by: (a) the father's sperm (b) the mother's egg (c) both parents equally (d) the diet of the mother
    Ans: (a) the father's sperm. Eggs always carry X; a sperm carrying Y produces a boy and one carrying X produces a girl.

M3. A cross between F1 hybrid tall plants (Tt × Tt) gives dwarf plants in F2. The expected ratio of tall to dwarf is: (a) 1:3 (b) 3:1 (c) 1:1 (d) 2:2
    Ans: (b) 3:1 — 25% of F2 are homozygous dwarf (tt).

M4. In a cross between two plants with genotype Tt × Tt, the proportion of the progeny with genotype Tt is: (a) 1/4 (b) 3/4 (c) 1 (d) 1/2
    Ans: (d) 1/2. The Punnett square gives TT : Tt : tt = 1 : 2 : 1, so Tt makes up 2 of 4, i.e. 1/2.

M5. Mendel chose the garden pea for his experiments mainly because it: (a) has easily visible contrasting traits and a short life cycle (b) has no variation among its plants (c) cannot be self-pollinated (d) produces only one seed per plant
    Ans: (a) has easily visible contrasting traits and a short life cycle. Pea plants have clear contrasting traits (tall/dwarf, round/wrinkled), grow quickly, and can be both self- and cross-pollinated.

M6. The phenotypic ratio obtained in a dihybrid cross between two F₁ hybrids is: (a) 3 : 1  (b) 9 : 3 : 3 : 1  (c) 1 : 1  (d) 1 : 2 : 1
    Ans: (b) 9 : 3 : 3 : 1. Mendel obtained this for two independently assorting traits, such as seed shape and seed colour.

M7. Which of these traits can be inherited? (a) the cut tail of a mouse  (b) large muscles built by exercise  (c) eye colour  (d) a scar
    Ans: (c) eye colour. Only changes in the DNA of reproductive cells are passed to the next generation; acquired changes in body cells are not.

M8. The number of chromosomes in a human zygote is: (a) 23  (b) 92  (c) 22  (d) 46
    Ans: (d) 46. Each gamete carries 23 chromosomes and their fusion restores the 23 pairs.
