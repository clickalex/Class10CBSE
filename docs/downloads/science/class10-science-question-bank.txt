CHAPTER 1 · Chemical Reactions and Equations
Unit: I · Chemical Substances

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 1 mark] Why must a chemical equation be balanced?
    Ans: Because of the law of conservation of mass — the number of atoms of each element must be the same on both sides, since atoms are neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Balance: HNO3 + Ca(OH)2 → Ca(NO3)2 + H2O
    Ans: 2HNO3 + Ca(OH)2 → Ca(NO3)2 + 2H2O. This is also a neutralisation, hence a double displacement reaction.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] What is a precipitation reaction? Give one example with a balanced equation.
    Ans: A precipitation reaction forms an insoluble solid (the precipitate) when two aqueous solutions are mixed. Example: Na₂SO₄(aq) + BaCl₂(aq) → BaSO₄(s)↓ + 2NaCl(aq), where the white precipitate is barium sulphate.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] Why is nitrogen flushed into bags of potato chips?
    Ans: Nitrogen is an unreactive (inert) gas. It displaces oxygen from the packet, so the fats and oils cannot be oxidised, which prevents rancidity and keeps the chips from changing smell and taste.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] Riya is revising “Chemical Reactions and Equations” (Balancing, the five reaction types, corrosion and rancidity). A classmate lost marks by doing this: Changing a subscript to balance (H2O → H2O2). Only the coefficient in front of a formula may be changed. Apply the correct method and rewrite the scoring answer.
    Ans:
      Correct method: Balancing order: start with the element that appears in the largest number of atoms, leave free elements (O2, H2) for last, then re-check oxygen.
      Why the mistake costs marks: Changing a subscript to balance (H2O → H2O2). Only the coefficient in front of a formula may be changed.
      Write the definition or formula first, then the working or example.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] Amit is given this exam-style task from “Chemical Reactions and Equations”: “Why must a chemical equation be balanced?” Treat it as an application question. Identify the idea, then write the working in points.
    Ans:
      Idea: Balancing, the five reaction types, corrosion and rancidity.
      Because of the law of conservation of mass — the number of atoms of each element must be the same on both sides, since atoms are neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Balance this equation and name the type of reaction: FeSO₄(s) → Fe₂O₃(s) + SO₂(g) + SO₃(g).
    Ans:
      Balanced: 2FeSO₄(s) → Fe₂O₃(s) + SO₂(g) + SO₃(g).
      It is a thermal decomposition reaction — one substance breaks into three on heating.
      It is also a redox reaction: Fe²⁺ is oxidised while part of the sulphur is reduced.

Q8. [Long · 4 marks] In the reaction ZnO + C → Zn + CO, identify the substance oxidised and the substance reduced.
    Ans: Carbon is oxidised (it gains oxygen to form CO) and zinc oxide is reduced (it loses oxygen to form Zn). Carbon is the reducing agent; ZnO is the oxidising agent.

Q11. [Application · 4 marks] Describe an activity to show that a chemical reaction is accompanied by the evolution of a gas, and how you would identify the gas.
    Ans:
      Take a few zinc granules in a conical flask and add dilute sulphuric acid; fit a cork with a delivery tube.
      Bubbles appear on the surface of the zinc, showing that a gas is being evolved.
      Bring a burning splint near the mouth of the tube: the gas burns with a pop sound, which identifies it as hydrogen.
      Equation: Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂↑.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] A white salt on heating decomposes to give brown fumes and a residue. Name the salt and write the equation.
    Ans: Lead nitrate. The brown fumes are nitrogen dioxide. 2Pb(NO3)2 → 2PbO + 4NO2↑ + O2↑ — a thermal decomposition reaction.

B. MCQs (11)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. The reaction Fe2O3 + 2Al → Al2O3 + 2Fe is an example of: (a) combination (b) decomposition (c) displacement (d) double displacement
    Ans: (c) displacement. Aluminium, being more reactive, displaces iron from its oxide. It is also a redox and a highly exothermic (thermite) reaction.

M2. Which of the following is an endothermic process? (a) respiration (b) decomposition of vegetable matter into compost (c) process of decomposition of ferrous sulphate (d) quicklime + water
    Ans: (c). Decomposition always needs energy, so heating ferrous sulphate is endothermic. The other three release heat.

M3. The green coating on copper and the black coating on silver are respectively: (a) basic copper carbonate and Ag2S (b) CuO and Ag2O (c) CuCO3 and AgS2 (d) Cu(OH)2 and AgO
    Ans: (a). Copper forms basic copper carbonate CuCO3·Cu(OH)2 (green); silver forms silver sulphide Ag2S (black).

M4. The most exam-ready way to revise “Chemical Reactions and Equations” is: (a) read the heading only  (b) learn the key idea plus one example  (c) skip written practice  (d) memorise a random extra topic
    Ans: (b) learn the key idea plus one example. The chapter is Balancing, the five reaction types, corrosion and rancidity.

M5. A 3-mark answer on “Chemical Reactions and Equations” should: (a) be one sentence  (b) start with the idea, then a reason or method  (c) copy the whole chapter  (d) leave the example out
    Ans: (b) start with the idea, then a reason or method. Step marks follow that frame.

M6. Which of these is a combination reaction? (a) CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂  (b) 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂  (c) Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu  (d) AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃
    Ans: (a) CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂. Two reactants combine to form a single product; (b) is decomposition, (c) displacement and (d) double displacement.

M7. The brown fumes evolved when lead nitrate is heated are of: (a) NO₂  (b) O₂  (c) NO  (d) PbO
    Ans: (a) NO₂. 2Pb(NO₃)₂ → 2PbO + 4NO₂ + O₂; nitrogen dioxide is brown, the residue lead oxide is yellow.

M8. Rancidity of fat-containing food is prevented by: (a) storing it in the open  (b) adding antioxidants and flushing with nitrogen  (c) adding oxidising agents  (d) heating it repeatedly
    Ans: (b) adding antioxidants and flushing with nitrogen. Both slow down the oxidation of fats that causes the stale smell and taste.

M9. When the equation Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂ is balanced, the coefficient of steam (H₂O) is: (a) 2  (b) 3  (c) 4  (d) 6
    Ans: (c) 4. 3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂ — three iron atoms need four oxygen atoms, which come from four water molecules.

M10. A shiny brown element X becomes black when heated in air. X and the black compound are: (a) Cu and CuO  (b) Fe and Fe₂O₃  (c) Ag and Ag₂S  (d) Al and Al₂O₃
    Ans: (a) Cu and CuO. Copper is reddish-brown and forms black copper(II) oxide on heating: 2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO.

M11. The reaction 2AgBr → 2Ag + Br₂ used in black-and-white photography is a: (a) thermal decomposition  (b) photochemical decomposition  (c) displacement  (d) combination reaction
    Ans: (b) photochemical decomposition. Light, not heat, splits silver bromide into silver and bromine; the grey silver darkens the film.

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] Why does dry HCl gas not change the colour of dry litmus paper?
    Ans: Because acidic behaviour requires the formation of H+(aq) ions, which needs water. Dry HCl gas has no water, so it does not ionise and cannot affect dry litmus.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] A solution turns red litmus blue. Its pH is likely to be: 1, 4, 7 or 10. Justify.
    Ans: 10. Turning red litmus blue shows the solution is basic, and only pH 10 among the options is on the basic side of 7.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] Fresh milk has a pH of 6. Explain why the pH falls as it turns into curd.
    Ans: Bacteria convert lactose into lactic acid. The acid increases the H+ ion concentration, so the pH drops below 6 and the milk proteins coagulate into curd.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] What happens when a base reacts with a non-metal oxide? Give an equation and state what it tells you about non-metal oxides.
    Ans: A salt and water are formed, just as in neutralisation: Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O. It shows that non-metal oxides are acidic in nature.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] Why is plaster of Paris written as CaSO4·½H2O? Is it possible to have half a water molecule?
    Ans: The formula is written per formula unit of CaSO4. One molecule of water is shared between two CaSO4 units, so each unit effectively holds half a water molecule. Heating gypsum at 373 K drives out 1½ molecules of water.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] A milkman adds a very small amount of baking soda to fresh milk. Why does the milk take longer to set as curd?
    Ans: The baking soda makes the milk slightly alkaline, so the lactic acid produced by bacteria is first neutralised before the pH can fall low enough to coagulate the casein. Setting is therefore delayed.

      Why the mistake costs marks: Saying 'an acid gives H+ ions' without (aq). Ionisation needs water — dry HCl is not acidic.

Q9. [Short · 3 marks] How is bleaching powder prepared? Write the equation and two uses.

      Uses: bleaching cotton and linen in the textile industry and wood pulp in paper factories.

Q12. [Application · 4 marks] Three unlabelled bottles contain an acidic, a basic and a neutral solution. How would you identify all three using only red and blue litmus paper?
    Ans:
      Dip a strip of red litmus into each solution: the one that turns it blue is the basic solution.
      Now dip a strip of blue litmus into the two remaining solutions: the one that turns it red is the acidic solution.
      The solution that changes neither paper is neutral (pH ≈ 7).
      Note that a single indicator is not enough — you must use both papers, because neutral and acidic solutions both leave red litmus unchanged.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] Explain the chlor-alkali process with an equation, and give two uses each of the products.
    Ans:

      Sodium hydroxide — making soaps and detergents, and in the paper industry.
      Chlorine — disinfecting drinking water and making bleaching powder/PVC.
      Hydrogen — as a fuel and in the manufacture of ammonia and margarine (hydrogenation of oils).

B. MCQs (11)

M1. A solution reacts with crushed egg-shell to give a gas that turns lime water milky. The solution contains: (a) NaCl (b) HCl (c) LiCl (d) KCl
    Ans: (b) HCl. Egg-shell is CaCO3; CaCO3 + 2HCl → CaCl2 + H2O + CO2. The CO2 turns lime water milky.

M2. Which one is a strong base? (a) NH4OH (b) Mg(OH)2 (c) NaOH (d) Cu(OH)2
    Ans: (c) NaOH. Alkali metal hydroxides are strong bases and ionise completely; the others are weak.

M3. The pH of gastric juice released during digestion is: (a) less than 7 (b) more than 7 (c) equal to 7 (d) equal to 0
    Ans: (a) less than 7. The stomach secretes hydrochloric acid, which keeps the gastric juice acidic (roughly pH 1.2–3) so that pepsin can act.

M4. The most exam-ready way to revise “Acids, Bases and Salts” is: (a) read the heading only  (b) learn the key idea plus one example  (c) skip written practice  (d) memorise a random extra topic
    Ans: (b) learn the key idea plus one example. The chapter is pH scale, indicators, and the four commercially important salts.

M5. A 3-mark answer on “Acids, Bases and Salts” should: (a) be one sentence  (b) start with the idea, then a reason or method  (c) copy the whole chapter  (d) leave the example out
    Ans: (b) start with the idea, then a reason or method. Step marks follow that frame.

M6. A solution turns colourless phenolphthalein pink. Its pH is likely to be: (a) 2  (b) 5  (c) 7  (d) 10
    Ans: (d) 10. Phenolphthalein turns pink only in a basic solution, whose pH is above 7.

M7. The salt used to remove permanent hardness of water is: (a) sodium chloride  (b) sodium carbonate  (c) calcium sulphate  (d) ammonium chloride
    Ans: (b) sodium carbonate. Washing soda precipitates out the dissolved calcium and magnesium salts that cause hardness.

M8. Dilute acid added to an active metal produces: (a) oxygen  (b) hydrogen  (c) carbon dioxide  (d) nitrogen
    Ans: (b) hydrogen. Metal + dilute acid → salt + H₂, which burns with a pop sound.

M9. The chemical formula of Plaster of Paris is: (a) CaSO₄·2H₂O  (b) CaSO₄·½H₂O  (c) CaSO₄  (d) CaCO₃
    Ans: (b) CaSO₄·½H₂O. Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) heated to 373 K loses three-quarters of its water of crystallisation to give the hemihydrate.

M10. Baking soda is: (a) Na₂CO₃·10H₂O  (b) NaHCO₃  (c) NaOH  (d) Na₂CO₃
    Ans: (b) NaHCO₃. Sodium hydrogencarbonate; it releases CO₂ on heating or with acid, which makes cakes rise and antacids fizz.

M11. Bleaching powder is manufactured by passing chlorine gas over: (a) dry slaked lime  (b) quicklime  (c) limestone  (d) gypsum
    Ans: (a) dry slaked lime. Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂ → CaOCl₂ + H₂O. Quicklime is CaO, limestone is CaCO₃, gypsum is a sulphate.

Q1. [Short · 1 mark] Name a metal that is liquid at room temperature and a non-metal that is lustrous.
    Ans: Mercury (metal, liquid at room temperature) and iodine (non-metal, lustrous).

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Why is sodium kept immersed in kerosene oil?
    Ans: Sodium is so reactive that it reacts vigorously with oxygen, moisture and carbon dioxide in air, catching fire. Kerosene cuts off air and moisture, so the metal stays unreacted.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] Why does aluminium not corrode easily even though it is a reactive metal?
    Ans: It reacts with oxygen to form a thin, tightly adhering layer of aluminium oxide on its surface. This layer prevents further reaction with air or moisture, so the metal below is protected.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] Give a reason why ionic compounds have high melting and boiling points.
    Ans: Oppositely charged ions in an ionic compound are held together by strong electrostatic forces in a crystal lattice, and a large amount of energy is needed to break these forces, so melting and boiling points are high.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] Why are food cans coated with tin and not with zinc?
    Ans: Zinc is more reactive than tin and would itself react with the acidic contents of the food, contaminating it. Tin is less reactive, so it protects the can without reacting.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] Show the formation of MgCl2 by the transfer of electrons, and state two properties of the compound formed.
    Ans:
      Mg (2,8,2) loses 2 electrons → Mg2+; each Cl (2,8,7) gains one electron → Cl−. Mg2+ + 2Cl− → MgCl2, held by strong electrostatic force.
      Properties: it is a hard crystalline solid with a high melting point, and it conducts electricity when molten or dissolved in water.

Q9. [Short · 3 marks] How are metals low in the reactivity series extracted from their ores? Give equations.
    Ans:

M1. A copper coin placed in silver nitrate solution turns silvery and the solution turns blue. This shows copper is: (a) less reactive than silver (b) more reactive than silver (c) equally reactive (d) an inert metal
    Ans: (b) more reactive than silver. Cu + 2AgNO3 → Cu(NO3)2 + 2Ag; the blue colour is due to Cu2+ ions.

M2. Which pair is amphoteric? (a) Na2O and CaO (b) Al2O3 and ZnO (c) CO2 and SO2 (d) CuO and FeO
    Ans: (b) Al2O3 and ZnO — they react with both acids and bases to form salt and water.

M3. The method used to extract sodium from molten NaCl is: (a) reduction with carbon (b) self reduction (c) electrolytic reduction (d) roasting
    Ans: (c) electrolytic reduction. Sodium is above carbon in the reactivity series, so carbon cannot reduce it; the molten chloride is electrolysed.

    Ans: (b) start with the idea, then a reason or method. Step marks follow that frame.

M6. The best conductor of electricity among these is: (a) copper  (b) silver  (c) aluminium  (d) iron

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] Why do covalent compounds generally have low melting and boiling points?
    Ans: Because the forces of attraction between their molecules are weak, even though the covalent bonds within a molecule are strong. Little energy is needed to separate the molecules.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Give a chemical test to distinguish between butter and cooking oil.
    Ans: The bromine water test (or the alkaline KMnO4 test). Cooking oil is unsaturated, so it decolorises bromine water/KMnO4; butter is largely saturated and does not.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] Why is a mixture of ethyne and pure oxygen used for welding instead of ethyne and air?
    Ans: In air, ethyne burns incompletely and gives a sooty flame with less heat. In pure oxygen combustion is complete, producing a clean flame at about 3000 °C (the oxy-acetylene flame), which is hot enough for welding.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] Why is methane called a saturated hydrocarbon? Describe its electron-dot structure in words.
    Ans: In methane (CH₄) all four valencies of carbon are satisfied by four single covalent bonds with hydrogen atoms, so no more atoms can be added — the molecule is saturated. In the electron-dot structure, carbon sits at the centre and shares one pair of electrons with each of the four hydrogen atoms, giving every atom a stable octet (duplet for hydrogen).

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] What is a homologous series? Write the general formula and the third member of the alkane series.
    Ans:

      Alkanes: CnH2n+2. Third member = propane, C3H8.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] Explain the cleansing action of soap with the help of a diagram description.

      The micelle is washed away with the water, taking the dirt with it; agitation helps detach the oil.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] A man with blood group A marries a woman with blood group O and their daughter has blood group O. Is this enough to tell which trait is dominant? Why?
    Ans: No. From one family we cannot tell. If A were dominant, the father could be heterozygous (IAi) and the daughter could inherit i from both parents; but a single observation does not establish dominance, which requires studying many such crosses.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] Distinguish between acquired and inherited traits, with one example of each.
    Ans: An acquired trait develops during an organism's lifetime due to experience or environment and is not passed on — for example, a wrestler's well-developed muscles or a pierced ear. An inherited trait is present from birth and is passed on through the DNA of germ cells — for example, the colour of eyes or free ear lobes.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] Distinguish between genotype and phenotype with an example.
    Ans: Genotype is the genetic constitution of an organism (for example Tt), while phenotype is the observable character it produces (for example a tall plant). Because the tall allele is dominant, the genotypes TT and Tt both give the tall phenotype.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] Explain Mendel's monohybrid cross with a Punnett square.
    Ans:
      Pure tall (TT) × pure dwarf (tt). Gametes: T and t. F1: all Tt — tall, because tall is dominant and dwarf is recessive.
      Selfing F1 (Tt × Tt) gives gametes T and t from each parent. Punnett square → TT, Tt, Tt, tt.
      Phenotype: 3 tall : 1 dwarf. Genotype: 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt. The dwarf character was only masked in F1, not lost.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] How is the sex of a child determined in human beings? Show diagrammatically.
    Ans:
      Mother (XX) produces only X-bearing eggs; father (XY) produces X-bearing and Y-bearing sperms in equal numbers.
      X egg + X sperm → XX = girl; X egg + Y sperm → XY = boy.
      The probability of either is 50%, so the sex of the child is decided by chance and not by either parent's wish or action.

A plane mirror always forms a virtual, erect image, the same size as the object, at the same distance behind the mirror, and laterally inverted.
      Marks lens: –4 marks. One ray diagram, one numerical on the mirror or lens formula, and one question on magnification or power.
      Method / memory trick: Sign convention: distances measured against the direction of the incident light (to the left of the pole) are negative. Object distance u is always negative. For a concave mirror f is negative; for a convex mirror and a convex lens f is positive; a concave lens has negative f.
      Do not: Using the mirror formula for a lens. Lens formula has −v and +u in the same form: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Why do stars twinkle but planets do not?
    Ans: Starlight passes through layers of air of changing refractive index, so its path keeps changing slightly and the apparent brightness and position fluctuate — the star twinkles. Planets are much nearer and behave as a collection of many point sources, so the fluctuations from different parts cancel out and the total light remains steady.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] Why does the sun appear reddish early in the morning?
    Ans: Because near the horizon sunlight travels through a much thicker layer of air. The shorter blue wavelengths are scattered away, and mainly the longer red wavelengths reach our eyes, so the sun looks red. At noon the path is short, little blue light is scattered, and the sun looks white.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] What is the power of accommodation of the eye?
    Ans: It is the ability of the eye lens to change its focal length — by the action of the ciliary muscles — so that both near and distant objects are focused sharply on the retina. In a normal eye the near point is about 25 cm and the far point is infinity.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] What is myopia? State its two causes and its correction.
    Ans:
      Myopia (short-sightedness) is the defect in which a person can see nearby objects clearly but cannot see distant objects distinctly; the image of a distant object forms in front of the retina.
      Causes: (i) excessive curvature of the eye lens, (ii) elongation of the eyeball.
      Correction: a concave lens of suitable power, which diverges the light so that the image is formed exactly on the retina.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] Explain the formation of a rainbow in the sky.
    Ans:

      Sunlight entering a droplet is first refracted and dispersed into its constituent colours, then undergoes total internal reflection at the inner surface of the droplet, and is refracted again as it leaves.
      Because different colours emerge at slightly different angles, the observer sees a coloured arc — a rainbow — with the sun always behind them.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Why are the coils of electric toasters and electric irons made of an alloy rather than a pure metal?

Q1. [Short · 1 mark] State Fleming's right-hand rule.
    Ans: Stretch the thumb, forefinger and middle finger of the right hand so that they are mutually perpendicular. If the forefinger points in the direction of the magnetic field and the thumb in the direction of motion of the conductor, the middle finger gives the direction of the induced current.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Why do two magnetic field lines never intersect each other?
    Ans: Because at the point of intersection there would be two directions of the magnetic field for a single point, which is impossible — a compass needle placed there could not point in two directions at once.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] Differentiate between an AC generator and a DC generator.
    Ans: An AC generator uses two complete slip rings, and the current it produces reverses direction periodically (50 Hz in India). A DC generator uses a split-ring commutator, so although the current in the coil reverses, the current in the external circuit always flows in one direction.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] State the rule used to find the direction of the magnetic field around a straight current-carrying conductor.
    Ans: Right-hand thumb rule: imagine holding the conductor in your right hand with the thumb pointing along the current; the direction in which your fingers curl around the conductor is the direction of the magnetic field lines, which are concentric circles around the wire.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] Explain the principle and working of an electric motor.
    Ans:
      Principle: a current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field experiences a force, given by Fleming's left-hand rule.
      Working: a rectangular coil ABCD is placed between the poles of a magnet. When current flows, one arm is pushed up and the other down, so the coil rotates.
      The split-ring commutator reverses the direction of current in the coil after every half rotation, so the coil keeps rotating in the same direction.
      Uses: electric fans, mixers, washing machines and pumps.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] What is electromagnetic induction? Describe an activity to demonstrate it.
    Ans:
      When the magnetic field linked with a coil changes, a current is induced in the coil — this is electromagnetic induction.
      Activity: connect a galvanometer to a coil. Push a bar magnet's north pole into the coil and the galvanometer needle deflects momentarily in one direction; pull it out and it deflects in the opposite direction; hold it still inside and there is no deflection.
      The deflection shows that a current is induced only when the magnetic flux through the coil is changing, i.e. only while the magnet is in relative motion.

Q12. [Application · 4 marks] Explain the working of an electric generator, including the role of slip rings and the commutator.
    Ans:
      A coil is rotated mechanically in a magnetic field, so the magnetic flux linked with it keeps changing and an induced current is produced (electromagnetic induction).
      The direction of the induced current reverses every half rotation, which is found by Fleming's right-hand rule.
      In an AC generator two slip rings keep the coil connected to the external circuit without reversing the connections, so the output is alternating current.
      A DC generator replaces them with a split-ring commutator, which reverses the connection to the external circuit every half turn so the output flows in one direction.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] Describe a domestic electric circuit and explain the function of the earth wire and the fuse.
    Ans:
      The supply has three wires: live (red, at about 220 V), neutral (black, at zero potential) and earth (green).
      Appliances are connected in parallel so that each receives the full supply voltage, each has its own switch, and the failure of one does not affect the others. Separate 15 A and 5 A circuits are provided for heavy and light appliances.
      Earth wire: the metallic body of an appliance is connected to a metal plate buried in the earth, so if the live wire touches the body the leakage current flows safely into the ground instead of through a person — preventing a fatal shock.
      Fuse: a short piece of wire of low melting point connected in series with the appliance. If the current exceeds the safe value, the fuse wire melts and breaks the circuit, protecting the appliance from damage due to overloading or short circuiting.

Q14. [Long · 5 marks] Discuss “Magnetic Effects of Electric Current” in detail. Include the idea that carries marks, one worked example or answer frame, and why students lose marks.
    Ans:

Two field lines never intersect (that would mean two directions of force at one point). The relative strength is shown by how close the lines are.

M6. The magnetic field lines inside a long current-carrying solenoid are: (a) concentric circles  (b) parallel straight lines  (c) diverging curves  (d) absent

Q1. [Short · 1 mark] Name the organisms that always occupy the first trophic level of a food chain, and define an ecosystem.
    Ans: Green plants and blue-green algae (producers). An ecosystem is a self-sustaining system in which all the living organisms of an area interact with each other and with the non-living (abiotic) components such as soil, water and air.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Why are some substances biodegradable and some non-biodegradable?
    Ans: Because micro-organisms such as bacteria and fungi produce enzymes that can break down only certain substances like paper, wood and vegetable waste. Substances such as plastic, glass and DDT cannot be acted upon by these enzymes, so they remain in the environment for a very long time and are called non-biodegradable.

Q3. [Application · 2 marks] Suggest two ways to reduce the problem of waste disposal.
    Ans: Segregate waste at the source and send biodegradable waste for composting or vermicomposting; and practise the 5 R's — refuse what is unnecessary, reduce consumption, reuse containers, repurpose items, and recycle paper, plastic, glass and metal.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] Distinguish between a food chain and a food web.
    Ans: A food chain is a single linear sequence showing who eats whom, for example grass → grasshopper → frog → snake. A food web is a network of many interconnected food chains in an ecosystem; because each organism can feed at more than one level, the web is far more stable than a single chain.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] In a food chain, if 10,000 joules of solar energy is available to the producers, how much energy will be available to the secondary consumer?
    Ans:
      Producers convert about 1% of the solar energy into food energy, but taking 10,000 J as the energy in the producers: primary consumer receives 10% = 1,000 J.
      Secondary consumer receives 10% of 1,000 J = 100 J.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] What is ozone? How is it formed, and why is damage to the ozone layer a cause for concern?
    Ans:
      Ozone is a molecule of oxygen with three atoms, O3, found in the upper atmosphere.
      Formation: ultraviolet radiation splits an oxygen molecule into free oxygen atoms, O2 →(UV) 2O; each free atom combines with an oxygen molecule to form ozone, O + O2 → O3.
      Concern: the ozone layer absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation from the sun. Its depletion, caused mainly by CFCs from refrigerators, air conditioners and aerosols, allows more UV to reach the earth, causing skin cancer, damage to the eyes, reduced crop yields and harm to marine organisms. The Montreal Protocol of 1987 froze CFC production to protect it.

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] Explain the role of decomposers in an ecosystem.
