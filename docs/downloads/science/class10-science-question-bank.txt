CHAPTER 1 · Chemical Reactions and Equations
Unit: I · Chemical Substances
========================================================================

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 1 mark] Why must a chemical equation be balanced?
    Ans: Because of the law of conservation of mass — the number of atoms of each element must be the same on both sides, since atoms are neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Balance: HNO3 + Ca(OH)2 → Ca(NO3)2 + H2O
    Ans: 2HNO3 + Ca(OH)2 → Ca(NO3)2 + 2H2O. This is also a neutralisation, hence a double displacement reaction.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] What is a precipitation reaction? Give one example with a balanced equation.
    Ans: A precipitation reaction forms an insoluble solid (the precipitate) when two aqueous solutions are mixed. Example: Na₂SO₄(aq) + BaCl₂(aq) → BaSO₄(s)↓ + 2NaCl(aq), where the white precipitate is barium sulphate.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] Why is nitrogen flushed into bags of potato chips?
    Ans: Nitrogen is an unreactive (inert) gas. It displaces oxygen from the packet, so the fats and oils cannot be oxidised, which prevents rancidity and keeps the chips from changing smell and taste.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] To balance H₂ + O₂ → H₂O, a student changes the formula of water to H₂O₂ and writes H₂ + O₂ → H₂O₂. Why is this wrong? Give the correct balanced equation with state symbols.
    Ans:
      Changing a subscript changes the substance: H₂O₂ is hydrogen peroxide, not water. Only the coefficient in front of a formula may be changed.
      Balanced equation: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O (4 H and 2 O atoms on each side).
      With state symbols: 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l).

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] An iron nail is dipped in blue copper sulphate solution for about 20 minutes. State what you observe, write the balanced chemical equation and name the type of reaction.
    Ans:
      The nail becomes coated with a reddish-brown deposit of copper, and the blue colour of the solution fades to light green.
      Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s).
      Iron is more reactive than copper, so it displaces copper from its salt solution: a displacement reaction.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Balance this equation and name the type of reaction: FeSO₄(s) → Fe₂O₃(s) + SO₂(g) + SO₃(g).
    Ans:
      Balanced: 2FeSO₄(s) → Fe₂O₃(s) + SO₂(g) + SO₃(g).
      It is a thermal decomposition reaction — one substance breaks into three on heating.
      It is also a redox reaction: Fe²⁺ is oxidised while part of the sulphur is reduced.

Q8. [Long · 4 marks] In the reaction ZnO + C → Zn + CO, identify the substance oxidised and the substance reduced.
    Ans: Carbon is oxidised (it gains oxygen to form CO) and zinc oxide is reduced (it loses oxygen to form Zn). Carbon is the reducing agent; ZnO is the oxidising agent.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A shopkeeper finds that a packet of chips left open for weeks smells and tastes bad, and that the iron gate outside his shop has turned reddish-brown.
    
    (a) Name the two processes.
    (b) Give the chemical formula of the reddish-brown substance on the gate.
    (c) Suggest one way to prevent each process.
    Ans:
      (a) The chips have turned rancid (oxidation of fats and oils); the gate has rusted (corrosion of iron).
      (b) Rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃·xH₂O.
      (c) Rancidity: pack the chips in airtight bags flushed with nitrogen, or add antioxidants. Rusting: paint, oil or galvanise the iron so that air and moisture cannot reach it.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A student rubs a 3 cm magnesium ribbon with sandpaper and then holds it with tongs over a flame. It burns with a dazzling white flame and leaves a white powder.
    
    (a) Why is the ribbon cleaned before burning?
    (b) Name the white powder.
    (c) Write the balanced equation and name the type of reaction.
    Ans:
      (a) Sandpaper removes the dull layer of magnesium oxide that covers the ribbon, so the pure metal can burn.
      (b) The white powder is magnesium oxide, MgO.
      (c) 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. Two substances combine to form one product, so it is a combination reaction (it is also an oxidation, and exothermic).

Q11. [Application · 4 marks] Describe an activity to show that a chemical reaction is accompanied by the evolution of a gas, and how you would identify the gas.
    Ans:
      Take a few zinc granules in a conical flask and add dilute sulphuric acid; fit a cork with a delivery tube.
      Bubbles appear on the surface of the zinc, showing that a gas is being evolved.
      Bring a burning splint near the mouth of the tube: the gas burns with a pop sound, which identifies it as hydrogen.
      Equation: Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂↑.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] A white salt on heating decomposes to give brown fumes and a residue. Name the salt and write the equation.
    Ans: Lead nitrate. The brown fumes are nitrogen dioxide. 2Pb(NO3)2 → 2PbO + 4NO2↑ + O2↑ — a thermal decomposition reaction.

B. MCQs (11)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. The reaction Fe2O3 + 2Al → Al2O3 + 2Fe is an example of: (a) combination (b) decomposition (c) double displacement (d) displacement
    Ans: (d) displacement. Aluminium, being more reactive, displaces iron from its oxide. It is also a redox and a highly exothermic (thermite) reaction.

M2. Which of the following is an endothermic process? (a) process of decomposition of ferrous sulphate (b) respiration (c) decomposition of vegetable matter into compost (d) quicklime + water
    Ans: (a). Decomposition always needs energy, so heating ferrous sulphate is endothermic. The other three release heat.

M3. The green coating on copper and the black coating on silver are respectively: (a) CuO and Ag2O (b) CuCO3 and AgS2 (c) basic copper carbonate and Ag2S (d) Cu(OH)2 and AgO
    Ans: (c). Copper forms basic copper carbonate CuCO3·Cu(OH)2 (green); silver forms silver sulphide Ag2S (black).

M4. Which of the following is a displacement reaction? (a) CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂  (b) 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂  (c) 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO  (d) Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu
    Ans: (d) Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu. Zinc, being more reactive, replaces copper from its salt solution. The others are combination, decomposition and combination reactions.

M5. Quicklime reacts vigorously with water to form slaked lime and a lot of heat. This reaction is: (a) endothermic and a combination reaction  (b) exothermic and a combination reaction  (c) exothermic and a decomposition reaction  (d) endothermic and a displacement reaction
    Ans: (b) exothermic and a combination reaction. CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + heat: two substances join to form one product, and heat is released.

M6. Which of these is a combination reaction? (a) CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂  (b) 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂  (c) Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu  (d) AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃
    Ans: (a) CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂. Two reactants combine to form a single product; (b) is decomposition, (c) displacement and (d) double displacement.

M7. The brown fumes evolved when lead nitrate is heated are of: (a) O₂  (b) NO₂  (c) NO  (d) PbO
    Ans: (b) NO₂. 2Pb(NO₃)₂ → 2PbO + 4NO₂ + O₂; nitrogen dioxide is brown, the residue lead oxide is yellow.

M8. Rancidity of fat-containing food is prevented by: (a) storing it in the open  (b) adding oxidising agents  (c) heating it repeatedly  (d) adding antioxidants and flushing with nitrogen
    Ans: (d) adding antioxidants and flushing with nitrogen. Both slow down the oxidation of fats that causes the stale smell and taste.

M9. When the equation Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂ is balanced, the coefficient of steam (H₂O) is: (a) 2  (b) 3  (c) 4  (d) 6
    Ans: (c) 4. 3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂ — three iron atoms need four oxygen atoms, which come from four water molecules.

M10. A shiny brown element X becomes black when heated in air. X and the black compound are: (a) Fe and Fe₂O₃  (b) Ag and Ag₂S  (c) Cu and CuO  (d) Al and Al₂O₃
    Ans: (c) Cu and CuO. Copper is reddish-brown and forms black copper(II) oxide on heating: 2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO.

M11. The reaction 2AgBr → 2Ag + Br₂ used in black-and-white photography is a: (a) thermal decomposition  (b) photochemical decomposition  (c) displacement  (d) combination reaction
    Ans: (b) photochemical decomposition. Light, not heat, splits silver bromide into silver and bromine; the grey silver darkens the film.

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (14)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] Why does dry HCl gas not change the colour of dry litmus paper?
    Ans: Because acidic behaviour requires the formation of H+(aq) ions, which needs water. Dry HCl gas has no water, so it does not ionise and cannot affect dry litmus.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] A solution turns red litmus blue. Its pH is likely to be: 1, 4, 7 or 10. Justify.
    Ans: 10. Turning red litmus blue shows the solution is basic, and only pH 10 among the options is on the basic side of 7.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] Fresh milk has a pH of 6. Explain why the pH falls as it turns into curd.
    Ans: Bacteria convert lactose into lactic acid. The acid increases the H+ ion concentration, so the pH drops below 6 and the milk proteins coagulate into curd.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] What happens when a base reacts with a non-metal oxide? Give an equation and state what it tells you about non-metal oxides.
    Ans: A salt and water are formed, just as in neutralisation: Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O. It shows that non-metal oxides are acidic in nature.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] Why is plaster of Paris written as CaSO4·½H2O? Is it possible to have half a water molecule?
    Ans: The formula is written per formula unit of CaSO4. One molecule of water is shared between two CaSO4 units, so each unit effectively holds half a water molecule. Heating gypsum at 373 K drives out 1½ molecules of water.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] A milkman adds a very small amount of baking soda to fresh milk. Why does the milk take longer to set as curd?
    Ans: The baking soda makes the milk slightly alkaline, so the lactic acid produced by bacteria is first neutralised before the pH can fall low enough to coagulate the casein. Setting is therefore delayed.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes: “The setting of plaster of Paris into a hard mass is only a physical change because nothing new is made.” Correct the statement with the chemical equation.
    Ans:
      Setting is a chemical change: plaster of Paris combines with water to form a new compound, gypsum.
      CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O (gypsum, a hard solid mass).
      This is why POP is stored in moisture-proof containers: damp air would make it set. Its uses include casting for fractured bones and making toys and decorative material.

Q8. [Application · 3 marks] The soil in a field has a pH of 5.2. What should the farmer add to it, and why? Name one common material.
    Ans:
      A pH of 5.2 means the soil is acidic, while most crops grow best at about pH 6.5–7.5.
      The farmer should add a base such as quicklime (CaO), slaked lime Ca(OH)₂ or chalk (CaCO₃).
      The base neutralises the excess acid (acid + base → salt + water), which raises the pH towards neutral.

Q9. [Short · 3 marks] How is bleaching powder prepared? Write the equation and two uses.
    Ans:
      Bleaching powder is prepared by the action of chlorine gas on dry slaked lime: Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂ → CaOCl₂ + H₂O.
      Uses: bleaching cotton and linen in the textile industry and wood pulp in paper factories.
      It is also used as an oxidising agent in chemical industries and to disinfect drinking water.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Four solutions P, Q, R and S have pH values 2, 6, 8 and 13 respectively.
    
    (a) Which is the strongest acid and which is the strongest base?
    (b) Which solution would you use to relieve a bee sting, and why not the strongest base?
    (c) How many times greater is the hydrogen-ion concentration of P than that of Q?
    Ans:
      (a) P (pH 2) is the strongest acid; S (pH 13) is the strongest base.
      (b) R (pH 8), a mild base like baking-soda solution, neutralises the acid (methanoic acid) of the sting. S would be too strong and would burn the skin.
      (c) Each pH unit is a factor of 10, and P and Q differ by 4 units, so P has 10⁴ = 10,000 times the hydrogen-ion concentration of Q.

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A student heats a few blue crystals of copper sulphate in a dry test tube. Water droplets appear on the cooler part of the tube and the crystals turn white. When a few drops of water are added to the white powder, the blue colour returns.
    
    (a) Write the formula of the blue crystals.
    (b) Why does the colour change on heating?
    (c) What is this water called?
    Ans:
      (a) CuSO₄·5H₂O.
      (b) Heating drives off the water held in the crystals, leaving white anhydrous copper sulphate, CuSO₄.
      (c) It is the water of crystallisation: five water molecules per formula unit. Adding water restores it, so the blue colour returns.

Q12. [Application · 4 marks] Three unlabelled bottles contain an acidic, a basic and a neutral solution. How would you identify all three using only red and blue litmus paper?
    Ans:
      Dip a strip of red litmus into each solution: the one that turns it blue is the basic solution.
      Now dip a strip of blue litmus into the two remaining solutions: the one that turns it red is the acidic solution.
      The solution that changes neither paper is neutral (pH ≈ 7).
      Note that a single indicator is not enough — you must use both papers, because neutral and acidic solutions both leave red litmus unchanged.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] Explain the chlor-alkali process with an equation, and give two uses each of the products.
    Ans:
      Electrolysis of concentrated aqueous sodium chloride (brine) gives sodium hydroxide, chlorine and hydrogen: 2NaCl(aq) + 2H2O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + Cl2(g) + H2(g).
      Sodium hydroxide — making soaps and detergents, and in the paper industry.
      Chlorine — disinfecting drinking water and making bleaching powder/PVC.
      Hydrogen — as a fuel and in the manufacture of ammonia and margarine (hydrogenation of oils).

Q14. [Long · 5 marks] How is baking soda prepared? What happens when it is heated? Write the equations and give two uses of baking soda and two uses of washing soda.
    Ans:
      Preparation: NaCl + H₂O + CO₂ + NH₃ → NH₄Cl + NaHCO₃ (sodium hydrogencarbonate, baking soda).
      On heating: 2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂. The sodium carbonate formed gives washing soda, Na₂CO₃·10H₂O, on recrystallisation.
      Uses of baking soda: an antacid; baking powder (with tartaric acid) to make cakes soft and spongy; soda-acid fire extinguishers.
      Uses of washing soda: removing permanent hardness of water; making glass, soap and paper; household cleaning.

B. MCQs (11)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. A solution reacts with crushed egg-shell to give a gas that turns lime water milky. The solution contains: (a) NaCl (b) HCl (c) LiCl (d) KCl
    Ans: (b) HCl. Egg-shell is CaCO3; CaCO3 + 2HCl → CaCl2 + H2O + CO2. The CO2 turns lime water milky.

M2. Which one is a strong base? (a) NaOH (b) NH4OH (c) Mg(OH)2 (d) Cu(OH)2
    Ans: (a) NaOH. Alkali metal hydroxides are strong bases and ionise completely; the others are weak.

M3. The pH of gastric juice released during digestion is: (a) less than 7 (b) more than 7 (c) equal to 7 (d) equal to 0
    Ans: (a) less than 7. The stomach secretes hydrochloric acid, which keeps the gastric juice acidic (roughly pH 1.2–3) so that pepsin can act.

M4. Which of the following salts gives a neutral solution (pH about 7) in water? (a) Na₂CO₃  (b) NH₄Cl  (c) NaCl  (d) CH₃COONa
    Ans: (c) NaCl. NaCl is the salt of a strong acid (HCl) and a strong base (NaOH), so its solution is neutral.

M5. When carbon dioxide is passed through lime water, it turns milky because of the formation of: (a) calcium chloride  (b) calcium carbonate  (c) calcium hydroxide  (d) calcium oxide
    Ans: (b) calcium carbonate. Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O; calcium carbonate is insoluble in water, so the lime water turns milky.

M6. A solution turns colourless phenolphthalein pink. Its pH is likely to be: (a) 2  (b) 5  (c) 7  (d) 10
    Ans: (d) 10. Phenolphthalein turns pink only in a basic solution, whose pH is above 7.

M7. The salt used to remove permanent hardness of water is: (a) sodium chloride  (b) calcium sulphate  (c) sodium carbonate  (d) ammonium chloride
    Ans: (c) sodium carbonate. Washing soda precipitates out the dissolved calcium and magnesium salts that cause hardness.

M8. Dilute acid added to an active metal produces: (a) hydrogen  (b) oxygen  (c) carbon dioxide  (d) nitrogen
    Ans: (a) hydrogen. Metal + dilute acid → salt + H₂, which burns with a pop sound.

M9. The chemical formula of Plaster of Paris is: (a) CaSO₄·2H₂O  (b) CaSO₄·½H₂O  (c) CaSO₄  (d) CaCO₃
    Ans: (b) CaSO₄·½H₂O. Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) heated to 373 K loses three-quarters of its water of crystallisation to give the hemihydrate.

M10. Baking soda is: (a) Na₂CO₃·10H₂O  (b) NaOH  (c) Na₂CO₃  (d) NaHCO₃
    Ans: (d) NaHCO₃. Sodium hydrogencarbonate; it releases CO₂ on heating or with acid, which makes cakes rise and antacids fizz.

M11. Bleaching powder is manufactured by passing chlorine gas over: (a) quicklime  (b) limestone  (c) dry slaked lime  (d) gypsum
    Ans: (c) dry slaked lime. Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂ → CaOCl₂ + H₂O. Quicklime is CaO, limestone is CaCO₃, gypsum is a sulphate.

CHAPTER 3 · Metals and Non-metals
Unit: I · Chemical Substances

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (14)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 1 mark] Name a metal that is liquid at room temperature and a non-metal that is lustrous.
    Ans: Mercury (metal, liquid at room temperature) and iodine (non-metal, lustrous).

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Why is sodium kept immersed in kerosene oil?
    Ans: Sodium is so reactive that it reacts vigorously with oxygen, moisture and carbon dioxide in air, catching fire. Kerosene cuts off air and moisture, so the metal stays unreacted.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] Why does aluminium not corrode easily even though it is a reactive metal?
    Ans: It reacts with oxygen to form a thin, tightly adhering layer of aluminium oxide on its surface. This layer prevents further reaction with air or moisture, so the metal below is protected.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] Give a reason why ionic compounds have high melting and boiling points.
    Ans: Oppositely charged ions in an ionic compound are held together by strong electrostatic forces in a crystal lattice, and a large amount of energy is needed to break these forces, so melting and boiling points are high.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] Why are food cans coated with tin and not with zinc?
    Ans: Zinc is more reactive than tin and would itself react with the acidic contents of the food, contaminating it. Tin is less reactive, so it protects the can without reacting.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] Show the formation of MgCl2 by the transfer of electrons, and state two properties of the compound formed.
    Ans:
      Mg (2,8,2) loses 2 electrons → Mg2+; each Cl (2,8,7) gains one electron → Cl−. Mg2+ + 2Cl− → MgCl2, held by strong electrostatic force.
      Properties: it is a hard crystalline solid with a high melting point, and it conducts electricity when molten or dissolved in water.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes: “Zinc carbonate is converted into zinc oxide by roasting in excess air.” Correct the statement and write the equations for converting a carbonate ore and a sulphide ore into oxides.
    Ans:
      A carbonate ore is converted into its oxide by calcination — heating strongly in a limited supply or absence of air. Roasting is used for sulphide ores, in excess air.
      Calcination: ZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂.
      Roasting: 2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂.

Q8. [Application · 3 marks] Iron nails are placed in copper sulphate solution, and a copper strip is placed in zinc sulphate solution. In which case will a reaction occur? Justify.
    Ans:
      A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its salt solution.
      Iron is more reactive than copper, so a reaction occurs: Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu.
      Copper is less reactive than zinc, so it cannot displace zinc, and there is no reaction in the second case.

Q9. [Short · 3 marks] How are metals low in the reactivity series extracted from their ores? Give equations.
    Ans:
      Metals low in the series (such as mercury and copper) are obtained by heating their ores alone — reduction with carbon is not needed.
      Cinnabar (HgS) is first roasted in air: 2HgS + 3O₂ → 2HgO + 2SO₂, then heated: 2HgO → 2Hg + O₂.
      Copper from its sulphide ore: Cu₂S + 3O₂ → 2Cu₂O + 2SO₂, then 2Cu₂O + Cu₂S → 6Cu + SO₂.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A metal M reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid, giving bubbles of a gas that burns with a pop sound. M does not react with cold water but reacts with steam to form a metal oxide and the same gas.
    
    (a) Name the gas.
    (b) Suggest a metal that could be M.
    (c) Write the equation for the reaction of M with steam.
    Ans:
      (a) Hydrogen.
      (b) Zinc (iron or aluminium would also fit).
      (c) Zn + H₂O(g) → ZnO + H₂.

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A student tests three samples. Solid sodium chloride does not conduct electricity, but molten sodium chloride and its aqueous solution do. An aqueous solution of glucose does not conduct either.
    
    (a) Why does solid NaCl not conduct?
    (b) Why does it conduct when molten or dissolved?
    (c) Why does the glucose solution not conduct?
    Ans:
      (a) In the solid, the Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are held rigidly in the crystal and cannot move.
      (b) Melting or dissolving frees the ions, and moving ions carry the current.
      (c) Glucose is a covalent compound made of neutral molecules; it forms no ions in water, so there are no charge carriers.

Q12. [Application · 4 marks] Describe the thermite reaction and give its use.
    Ans:
      The thermite reaction: Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe + heat.
      Aluminium is more reactive than iron, so it reduces iron(III) oxide to molten iron and a large amount of heat is released.
      The molten iron produced is used to weld railway tracks and cracked machine parts without dismantling them.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] Explain electrolytic refining of copper.
    Ans:
      The impure copper is made the anode and a thin strip of pure copper the cathode, both dipped in acidified copper sulphate solution.
      On passing current, copper from the anode dissolves as Cu2+ and an equal amount deposits on the cathode.
      Soluble impurities go into the solution; insoluble impurities settle below the anode as anode mud (which contains the precious metals).

Q14. [Long · 5 marks] Explain, with equations, how zinc is extracted from its sulphide ore. Why can sodium not be obtained from its oxide in the same way?
    Ans:
      Zinc blende (ZnS) is first concentrated, then converted into zinc oxide by roasting: 2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂.
      The oxide is reduced to the metal by heating with carbon: ZnO + C → Zn + CO.
      The crude zinc is purified by electrolytic refining.
      Sodium is far above carbon in the reactivity series, so carbon cannot reduce its oxide.
      Highly reactive metals are obtained by electrolysis of their molten chlorides: at the cathode Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na.

B. MCQs (11)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. A copper coin placed in silver nitrate solution turns silvery and the solution turns blue. This shows copper is: (a) less reactive than silver (b) equally reactive (c) an inert metal (d) more reactive than silver
    Ans: (d) more reactive than silver. Cu + 2AgNO3 → Cu(NO3)2 + 2Ag; the blue colour is due to Cu2+ ions.

M2. Which pair is amphoteric? (a) Na2O and CaO (b) Al2O3 and ZnO (c) CO2 and SO2 (d) CuO and FeO
    Ans: (b) Al2O3 and ZnO — they react with both acids and bases to form salt and water.

M3. The method used to extract sodium from molten NaCl is: (a) reduction with carbon (b) self reduction (c) electrolytic reduction (d) roasting
    Ans: (c) electrolytic reduction. Sodium is above carbon in the reactivity series, so carbon cannot reduce it; the molten chloride is electrolysed.

M4. Which of the following metals will displace copper from copper sulphate solution? (a) Ag  (b) Zn  (c) Hg  (d) Au
    Ans: (b) Zn. Only a metal above copper in the reactivity series can displace it; zinc is above copper, while silver, mercury and gold are below it.

M5. An element forms an oxide that is acidic in nature. The element is most likely a: (a) non-metal  (b) metal  (c) alkali metal  (d) alloy
    Ans: (a) non-metal. Non-metals form acidic (or neutral) oxides, while metals generally form basic oxides.

M6. The best conductor of electricity among these is: (a) copper  (b) silver  (c) aluminium  (d) iron
    Ans: (b) silver. Silver conducts best, then copper; silver is too costly for wiring, so copper is used.

M7. Brass is an alloy of: (a) copper and zinc  (b) copper and tin  (c) lead and tin  (d) mercury and zinc
    Ans: (a) copper and zinc. Bronze is copper and tin, solder is lead and tin, and an amalgam contains mercury.

M8. Which metal reacts vigorously with cold water? (a) iron  (b) zinc  (c) copper  (d) sodium
    Ans: (d) sodium. It reacts violently with cold water; iron and zinc react only with steam, and copper does not react at all.

M9. The correct decreasing order of reactivity is: (a) Zn > Mg > Cu  (b) Cu > Zn > Mg  (c) Zn > Cu > Mg  (d) Mg > Zn > Cu
    Ans: (d) Mg > Zn > Cu. In the reactivity series magnesium lies above zinc, and copper lies below hydrogen, so it is the least reactive of the three.

M10. Galvanisation protects iron from rusting by coating it with a thin layer of: (a) tin  (b) chromium  (c) zinc  (d) copper
    Ans: (c) zinc. Zinc is more reactive than iron, so it corrodes in preference to the iron even if the coating is scratched.

M11. Magnesium (2, 8, 2) combines with chlorine (2, 8, 7) to form an ionic compound with the formula: (a) MgCl₂  (b) MgCl  (c) Mg₂Cl  (d) MgCl₃
    Ans: (a) MgCl₂. Magnesium loses two electrons to become Mg²⁺; each chlorine atom gains one, so two Cl⁻ ions are needed.

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (14)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] Why do covalent compounds generally have low melting and boiling points?
    Ans: Because the forces of attraction between their molecules are weak, even though the covalent bonds within a molecule are strong. Little energy is needed to separate the molecules.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Give a chemical test to distinguish between butter and cooking oil.
    Ans: The bromine water test (or the alkaline KMnO4 test). Cooking oil is unsaturated, so it decolorises bromine water/KMnO4; butter is largely saturated and does not.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] Why is a mixture of ethyne and pure oxygen used for welding instead of ethyne and air?
    Ans: In air, ethyne burns incompletely and gives a sooty flame with less heat. In pure oxygen combustion is complete, producing a clean flame at about 3000 °C (the oxy-acetylene flame), which is hot enough for welding.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] Why is methane called a saturated hydrocarbon? Describe its electron-dot structure in words.
    Ans: In methane (CH₄) all four valencies of carbon are satisfied by four single covalent bonds with hydrogen atoms, so no more atoms can be added — the molecule is saturated. In the electron-dot structure, carbon sits at the centre and shares one pair of electrons with each of the four hydrogen atoms, giving every atom a stable octet (duplet for hydrogen).

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] What is a homologous series? Write the general formula and the third member of the alkane series.
    Ans:
      A homologous series is a group of compounds with the same general formula and similar chemical properties, in which successive members differ by a −CH2− unit (14 u).
      Alkanes: CnH2n+2. Third member = propane, C3H8.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] Explain the cleansing action of soap with the help of a diagram description.
    Ans:
      A soap molecule has a hydrophilic ionic head and a hydrophobic hydrocarbon tail.
      In water, the tails attach to the oily dirt while the heads remain in water, forming a spherical cluster called a micelle with the oil trapped inside.
      The micelle is washed away with the water, taking the dirt with it; agitation helps detach the oil.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes the formula of propene as C₃H₈, saying the general formula of alkenes is CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. Find the error and give the correct formula and structure of propene.
    Ans:
      Error: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ is the general formula of alkanes; alkenes have the general formula CₙH₂ₙ.
      For n = 3, propene is C₃H₆, with the structure CH₃–CH=CH₂ (one C=C double bond).
      C₃H₈ is propane, the saturated hydrocarbon with only single bonds.

Q8. [Application · 3 marks] How can you distinguish between ethanol and ethanoic acid by adding sodium hydrogencarbonate to each? Write the equation for the reaction that occurs.
    Ans:
      Add a little sodium hydrogencarbonate to a sample of each liquid.
      Ethanoic acid gives a brisk effervescence of carbon dioxide (the gas turns lime water milky); ethanol shows no reaction.
      CH₃COOH + NaHCO₃ → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂.

Q9. [Short · 3 marks] Write the equation for the combustion of ethanol and explain why ethanol burns with a clean blue flame.
    Ans:
      C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O + heat and light.
      Ethanol already contains oxygen and has a low carbon-to-hydrogen ratio, so it burns almost completely.
      Saturated hydrocarbons contain relatively more carbon and often burn incompletely, depositing unburnt carbon, which gives a sooty yellow flame.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Three compounds are: A = CH₃CH₂OH, B = CH₃COOH and C = CH₃COOCH₂CH₃.
    
    (a) Name A, B and C.
    (b) Which compound has a sweet, fruity smell, and how is it formed?
    (c) Write the equation for its formation.
    Ans:
      (a) A is ethanol, B is ethanoic acid and C is ethyl ethanoate.
      (b) C, the ester. It is formed when ethanol reacts with ethanoic acid in the presence of a few drops of concentrated sulphuric acid — an esterification reaction.
      (c) CH₃COOH + CH₃CH₂OH → CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + H₂O (conc. H₂SO₄ as catalyst).

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A chemist heats ethanol with excess concentrated sulphuric acid at 443 K and collects a colourless gas that decolourises bromine water. In another test, a small piece of sodium is dropped into ethanol; a gas bubbles out that burns with a pop sound.
    
    (a) Name the two gases.
    (b) Name the type of the first reaction.
    (c) Write the equation for the second reaction.
    Ans:
      (a) The first gas is ethene; the second is hydrogen.
      (b) The first reaction is dehydration — conc. H₂SO₄ removes a molecule of water from ethanol: CH₃CH₂OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O.
      (c) 2Na + 2CH₃CH₂OH → 2CH₃CH₂ONa + H₂ (sodium ethoxide and hydrogen).

Q12. [Application · 4 marks] Why does soap not lather well in hard water, and what is used instead?
    Ans:
      Hard water contains dissolved calcium and magnesium salts, which react with soap to form an insoluble sticky precipitate (scum).
      Soap is therefore wasted and lather forms only after all the Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions have been precipitated.
      Detergents are used instead: their molecules are sulphonate or sulphate salts whose calcium and magnesium salts stay dissolved, so they lather even in hard water.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] With equations, describe three chemical properties of ethanoic acid.
    Ans:
      With a base (neutralisation): CH3COOH + NaOH → CH3COONa + H2O.
      With a metal carbonate: 2CH3COOH + Na2CO3 → 2CH3COONa + H2O + CO2.
      With a hydrogen carbonate: CH3COOH + NaHCO3 → CH3COONa + H2O + CO2 (brisk effervescence).
      Esterification (with ethanol, conc. H2SO4): CH3COOH + C2H5OH → CH3COOC2H5 + H2O — a sweet-smelling ester used in perfumes and flavourings.

Q14. [Long · 5 marks] Why does carbon form covalent compounds? Describe the bonding in methane and in ethene with the help of electron-dot structures, in words.
    Ans:
      Carbon has four valence electrons. To gain four electrons (C⁴⁻) is difficult because six protons must hold ten electrons, and to lose four (C⁴⁺) needs a very large amount of energy. So carbon shares electrons and forms covalent bonds.
      Methane, CH₄: the carbon atom shares one electron with each of four hydrogen atoms, forming four single bonds. Carbon gets an octet and each hydrogen gets a duplet.
      Ethene, C₂H₄: each carbon shares two electrons with the other carbon (a double bond, two shared pairs) and one electron with each of two hydrogen atoms.
      Each carbon in ethene has four shared pairs around it, so both carbons have an octet.

B. MCQs (11)

M1. The number of covalent bonds in a molecule of ethane (C2H6) is: (a) 5 (b) 6 (c) 7 (d) 8
    Ans: (c) 7 — six C–H bonds plus one C–C bond.

M2. Which compound decolorises bromine water? (a) C2H4 (b) CH4 (c) C2H6 (d) C3H8
    Ans: (a) C2H4 (ethene). Only unsaturated compounds with a double bond undergo addition with bromine.

M3. Vinegar is a solution of: (a) 50–80% ethanoic acid (b) 5–8% ethanoic acid in water (c) pure ethanoic acid (d) ethanoic acid in alcohol
    Ans: (b) 5–8% ethanoic acid in water. Pure (glacial) acetic acid freezes at 290 K, which is why it is not used directly in kitchens.

M4. The next higher homologue of ethanol, C₂H₅OH, is: (a) C₃H₇OH  (b) C₃H₈  (c) CH₃OH  (d) C₂H₅COOH
    Ans: (a) C₃H₇OH. Successive members of a homologous series differ by a –CH₂– unit; ethanol → propanol (C₃H₇OH).

M5. Which of the following burns with a sooty flame? (a) methane (CH₄)  (b) ethane (C₂H₆)  (c) ethyne (C₂H₂)  (d) ethanol (C₂H₅OH)
    Ans: (c) ethyne (C₂H₂). Unsaturated hydrocarbons such as ethyne have a high proportion of carbon, which does not burn completely and forms soot.

M6. Which compound decolourises alkaline KMnO₄ (Baeyer's reagent)? (a) C₂H₆  (b) C₂H₄  (c) CH₄  (d) C₃H₈
    Ans: (b) C₂H₄. Ethene is unsaturated; only double or triple bonds add on and decolourise the reagent.

M7. The functional group present in an alcohol is: (a) –CHO  (b) –COOH  (c) –CO–  (d) –OH
    Ans: (d) –OH. –CHO is aldehyde, –COOH is carboxylic acid and –CO– is a ketone group.

M8. The gas evolved when ethanoic acid reacts with sodium carbonate is: (a) H₂  (b) O₂  (c) CO  (d) CO₂
    Ans: (d) CO₂. 2CH₃COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂; the gas turns lime water milky.

M9. The IUPAC name of CH₃–CH₂–CHO is: (a) propanal  (b) propanol  (c) propanone  (d) propanoic acid
    Ans: (a) propanal. Three carbons (prop-) with an aldehyde group –CHO (-al). Propanone is a ketone, propanol an alcohol, propanoic acid a carboxylic acid.

M10. Which pair belongs to the same homologous series? (a) CH₄ and C₂H₄  (b) C₂H₄ and C₂H₂  (c) CH₃OH and CH₃COOH  (d) C₂H₆ and C₃H₈
    Ans: (d) C₂H₆ and C₃H₈. Both are alkanes (CₙH₂ₙ₊₂) differing by one –CH₂– unit. The other pairs have different functional groups or bond types.

M11. Which hydrocarbon undergoes an addition reaction with hydrogen in the presence of a nickel catalyst? (a) C₃H₈  (b) C₂H₆  (c) C₂H₄  (d) C₄H₁₀
    Ans: (c) C₂H₄. Only an unsaturated hydrocarbon (ethene, with a C=C double bond) can add hydrogen; the other three are saturated alkanes.

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (14)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 1 mark] Name the functional unit of the kidney and the pigment that absorbs light energy in plants.
    Ans: Nephron; chlorophyll.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Why is diffusion insufficient to meet the oxygen requirements of multicellular organisms like human beings?
    Ans: Because their cells are not all in direct contact with the environment, and the volume of the body is large, so the distance oxygen would have to diffuse is too great. Diffusion is too slow to supply every cell, which is why special respiratory and transport systems are needed.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] Name the raw materials and the products of photosynthesis, and write the overall equation.
    Ans: Raw materials: carbon dioxide from the air and water absorbed from the soil, using chlorophyll and sunlight. Products: glucose (stored as starch) and oxygen. Overall: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ in the presence of sunlight and chlorophyll.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] Why is the rate of breathing in aquatic organisms much faster than in terrestrial organisms?
    Ans: Because the amount of dissolved oxygen in water is far lower than the oxygen in air. Aquatic animals must therefore pass a larger volume of water over their gills per unit time to obtain enough oxygen, which makes the breathing rate faster.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] What are the differences between aerobic and anaerobic respiration? Name two organisms that use anaerobic respiration.
    Ans:
      Aerobic occurs in the presence of oxygen, in the mitochondria, giving CO2 and H2O and a large amount of energy.
      Anaerobic occurs without oxygen in the cytoplasm, giving ethanol and CO2 (yeast) or lactic acid (muscles), and much less energy.
      Organisms: yeast, some bacteria and parasitic worms such as Taenia; human muscle cells respire anaerobically temporarily.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] Draw a labelled diagram of the human excretory system, or describe the working of a nephron.
    Ans:
      Kidneys → ureters → urinary bladder → urethra.
      Nephron: blood enters the glomerulus under pressure inside Bowman's capsule, where water, glucose, amino acids, salts and urea are filtered into the tubule (ultrafiltration).
      As the filtrate moves down the tubule, useful substances and most water are selectively reabsorbed; the remaining fluid, now urine, collects in the collecting duct and passes into the ureter.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes: “Bile juice digests fats because it contains the enzyme lipase.” What is wrong with this? State the real role of bile and name the enzyme that digests fats.
    Ans:
      Bile, made in the liver and stored in the gall bladder, contains no digestive enzyme.
      Bile salts break large fat globules into tiny droplets (emulsification), giving more surface for enzymes, and make the medium alkaline for the pancreatic enzymes.
      Fats are then digested by the enzyme lipase from the pancreatic and intestinal juices.

Q8. [Application · 3 marks] A potted plant is kept in the dark for three days. One of its leaves is then partly covered with black paper and kept in sunlight for six hours. The leaf is boiled in alcohol and tested with iodine solution. Which part turns blue-black, and what does this prove?
    Ans:
      The leaf is first destarched by keeping the plant in the dark.
      The uncovered part, which received light, turns blue-black with iodine because starch has been made there. The covered part stays brown and contains no starch.
      This shows that light is necessary for photosynthesis.

Q9. [Short · 3 marks] Describe how water and minerals are transported in plants.
    Ans:
      Root hair cells absorb water and dissolved minerals from the soil by osmosis and active transport.
      They pass into the xylem vessels, which form a continuous tube from the roots to the leaves.
      Loss of water vapour through the stomata (transpiration) creates a suction pull that lifts the water column upward; no energy is spent by the plant.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A runner feels a sharp cramp in her leg muscles at the end of a sprint, when her muscles could not get enough oxygen.
    
    (a) Name the process that supplied energy in her muscle cells and the substance that caused the cramp.
    (b) Write the pathway that begins with glucose.
    (c) How can the cramp be relieved?
    Ans:
      (a) Anaerobic respiration; the build-up of lactic acid caused the cramp.
      (b) Glucose (6-carbon) → pyruvate (3-carbon, in the cytoplasm) → lactic acid (3-carbon) + a small amount of energy.
      (c) Rest, or a hot-water massage, improves blood flow so that more oxygen reaches the muscles and the lactic acid is broken down.

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A student examines a model of the human heart and notes that the wall of one lower chamber is much thicker than the others.
    
    (a) Which chamber has the thickest wall, and why?
    (b) Name the vessel that carries oxygenated blood from the heart to the body and the vessel that brings oxygenated blood from the lungs.
    (c) What prevents blood from flowing backwards?
    Ans:
      (a) The left ventricle, because it must pump blood at high pressure to the whole body.
      (b) The aorta carries blood to the body; the pulmonary vein brings blood from the lungs to the left atrium.
      (c) Valves between the chambers and in the vessels keep the blood flowing in one direction.

Q12. [Application · 4 marks] Explain double circulation in human beings and state why it is advantageous.
    Ans:
      Blood passes through the heart twice during one complete round of the body.
      Pulmonary circuit: right side of the heart → lungs (oxygenation) → left side of the heart.
      Systemic circuit: left side → body organs → right side of the heart.
      Because oxygenated and deoxygenated blood stay separate, tissues receive blood with the maximum possible oxygen, which supports the high energy needs of a warm-blooded body.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] Explain the process of nutrition in human beings, from ingestion to egestion.
    Ans:
      Mouth: teeth chew the food; salivary amylase begins the breakdown of starch into simpler sugars.
      Oesophagus: peristaltic movements push the food to the stomach.
      Stomach: HCl makes the medium acidic for pepsin to digest proteins, kills germs, and mucus protects the stomach wall.
      Small intestine: the site of complete digestion — bile from the liver emulsifies fat, pancreatic trypsin acts on proteins, lipase on fats, and intestinal enzymes convert carbohydrates to glucose and proteins to amino acids; the finger-like villi absorb the digested food into the blood.
      Large intestine and anus: water is absorbed from the undigested food and the waste is egested.

Q14. [Long · 5 marks] Describe the three main events of photosynthesis and explain how stomata help the process.
    Ans:
      Photosynthesis takes place in the chloroplasts, which contain the green pigment chlorophyll.
      Event 1: chlorophyll absorbs light energy.
      Event 2: light energy is converted into chemical energy and water is split into hydrogen and oxygen.
      Event 3: carbon dioxide is reduced to carbohydrates: 6CO₂ + 12H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ + 6H₂O (in sunlight, with chlorophyll).
      Stomata are tiny pores on the leaf surface through which CO₂ enters and O₂ leaves. Guard cells open and close them, and water vapour is also lost through them.

M1. The kidneys in human beings are a part of the system for: (a) excretion (b) nutrition (c) respiration (d) transportation
    Ans: (a) excretion — they remove nitrogenous waste (urea and uric acid) from the blood.

M2. Which of the following is not a function of the stomach? (a) killing bacteria (b) digestion of protein (c) absorption of digested food (d) protection of the stomach wall by mucus
    Ans: (c). Absorption of digested food occurs mainly in the small intestine through the villi.

M3. The breakdown of pyruvate to give CO2, water and energy takes place in the: (a) mitochondria (b) cytoplasm (c) chloroplast (d) nucleus
    Ans: (a) mitochondria. Glycolysis (glucose → pyruvate) occurs in the cytoplasm; the further oxidation of pyruvate is mitochondrial.

M4. Which of the following gives the correct path of food in the human alimentary canal? (a) mouth → stomach → oesophagus → small intestine → large intestine (b) mouth → oesophagus → small intestine → stomach → large intestine (c) mouth → oesophagus → stomach → large intestine → small intestine (d) mouth → oesophagus → stomach → small intestine → large intestine

M6. Photosynthesis in a leaf takes place inside the: (a) mitochondria  (b) chloroplast  (c) ribosome  (d) vacuole

M8. The main nitrogenous waste filtered out by the kidneys is: (a) carbon dioxide  (b) urea  (c) glucose  (d) glycogen
    Ans: (b) urea. It is filtered out of blood in the nephrons along with water and salts to form urine.

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (14)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 1 mark] Name the largest part of the human brain and the hormone secreted by the pancreas.
    Ans: Cerebrum; insulin.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Why is the use of iodised salt advisable?
    Ans: Iodine is essential for the synthesis of thyroxin by the thyroid gland. A shortage of iodine causes the gland to swell, producing goitre, and can lead to mental and physical disability in children, so iodised salt ensures an adequate intake.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] How does phototropism occur in plants?
    Ans: Auxin is synthesised at the tip of the shoot and diffuses to the shaded side of the stem. There it causes the cells to elongate more than on the illuminated side, so the stem bends toward the light.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] Name the plant hormone responsible for cell elongation and the one that ripens fruit.
    Ans: Auxin causes cell elongation and is responsible for the bending of a shoot towards light. Ethylene is a gaseous hormone that promotes the ripening of fruits.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] Draw a labelled diagram of a neuron, or explain how a nerve impulse travels across a synapse.
    Ans:
      A neuron has a cell body with dendrites, a long axon, and branching nerve endings.
      When the impulse reaches the nerve ending, an electrical signal is converted into a chemical — a neurotransmitter is released into the synaptic gap.
      The chemical diffuses across the gap and triggers a new electrical impulse in the next neuron's dendrite, so the impulse travels in one direction only.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] What is a reflex arc? Why are reflex actions important?
    Ans:
      A reflex arc is the path followed by a nerve impulse during a reflex action: receptor → sensory neuron → spinal cord (relay neuron) → motor neuron → effector.
      They are important because they are immediate and do not require the brain to think, so they protect the body from sudden injury such as heat, sharp objects or bright light.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] A student draws a reflex action as: receptor → sensory neuron → brain → motor neuron → effector. What is wrong? Give the correct reflex arc and say why the pathway is useful.
    Ans:
      In a reflex action the impulse is processed in the spinal cord, not in the brain; the brain is informed afterwards.
      Correct reflex arc: receptor → sensory neuron → spinal cord (relay neuron) → motor neuron → effector.
      Skipping the brain makes the response very fast, which protects the body from harm, for example when a hand touches a hot object.

Q8. [Application · 3 marks] The leaves of a touch-me-not (Mimosa) plant fold when touched, and a pea tendril coils round a support it touches. Classify each movement and name the cause.
    Ans:
      Mimosa: a nastic (non-directional) movement. Touch makes cells at the base of the leaf lose water and shrink, so the leaf droops and folds.
      Pea tendril: a tropic (directional) movement, called thigmotropism. The side away from the support grows faster, so the tendril coils round it.
      Both are growth or turgor changes caused by a stimulus, but only the tendril’s response depends on the direction of the stimulus.

Q9. [Short · 3 marks] Explain how the nervous and endocrine systems work together, using adrenaline as the example.
    Ans:
      The nervous system gives a fast, point-to-point electrical message; hormones give a slower, longer-lasting chemical message.
      In a frightening situation the brain signals the adrenal medulla, which releases adrenaline into the blood.
      Adrenaline increases heart rate and breathing, and diverts blood to the skeletal muscles — the ‘fight or flight’ response — so the whole body reacts, not just one muscle.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Two children of the same age differ greatly in height: one is much taller than normal and the other is much shorter than normal. A doctor finds that this is related to one hormone.
    
    (a) Name the hormone and the gland that secretes it.
    (b) What condition arises from too little or too much of it in childhood?
    (c) Why is this gland called the “master gland”?
    Ans:
      (a) Growth hormone, secreted by the pituitary gland.
      (b) Too little causes dwarfism; too much causes gigantism.
      (c) The pituitary secretes hormones that control other endocrine glands such as the thyroid, adrenal glands and gonads.

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    During a long dry spell, the stomata of a plant close and its older leaves begin to wilt and fall.
    
    (a) Name the plant hormone mainly responsible.
    (b) State two effects of this hormone.
    (c) Name one plant hormone that promotes growth, working opposite to it.
    Ans:
      (a) Abscisic acid.
      (b) It inhibits growth, closes the stomata under water stress, and causes wilting and falling of leaves.
      (c) Auxin (or gibberellin, or cytokinin) promotes growth.

Q12. [Application · 4 marks] Describe the feedback mechanism that keeps blood sugar level steady.
    Ans:
      When the glucose level in blood rises, the pancreas releases insulin, which makes body cells take up glucose and the liver convert the excess into glycogen.
      As the level falls, the pancreas reduces insulin and secretes glucagon, which converts the stored glycogen back into glucose.
      This is a negative feedback loop. If insulin secretion fails, the blood sugar stays high — the condition called diabetes.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] Describe the parts of the human brain and their functions.
    Ans:
      Forebrain — contains the cerebrum, the main thinking part, which controls voluntary actions, memory, intelligence and the senses; it also contains centres for hunger and smell.
      Midbrain — controls involuntary actions such as the change in the size of the pupil.
      Hindbrain — pons helps regulate respiration; the medulla controls involuntary actions such as blood pressure, salivation and vomiting; the cerebellum maintains posture and balance and makes voluntary movements precise, such as walking in a straight line or picking up a pencil.
      The whole brain is protected by the cranium, meninges and the shock-absorbing cerebrospinal fluid.

Q14. [Long · 5 marks] Name five endocrine glands of the human body, the hormone each secretes and one function of each hormone.
    Ans:
      Pituitary — growth hormone — regulates growth and development.
      Thyroid — thyroxin — regulates the metabolism of carbohydrates, proteins and fats (it needs iodine).
      Pancreas — insulin — lowers the blood sugar level.
      Adrenal gland — adrenaline — prepares the body for “fight or flight” by increasing heart rate and breathing rate.
      Testes — testosterone — brings about changes at puberty in boys. (Ovaries — oestrogen — do the same in girls.)

M1. The gap between two neurons is called: (a) synapse (b) dendrite (c) axon (d) impulse

M6. The hormone that regulates the body's metabolic rate is: (a) thyroxine  (b) insulin  (c) growth hormone  (d) adrenaline

M7. The part of a neuron that receives the impulse is the: (a) axon  (b) cell body  (c) dendrite  (d) myelin sheath

    Ans: (b) spinal cord. Because the brain is not involved in the decision, the response is quick and automatic.

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (14)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 1 mark] Where does fertilisation occur in human beings, and which gland secretes testosterone?
    Ans: Fertilisation occurs in the fallopian tube (oviduct); testosterone is secreted by the testes.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Why are the testes located outside the abdominal cavity in the scrotum?
    Ans: Because sperm formation requires a temperature 2–3 °C lower than the normal body temperature. The scrotum, being outside the abdomen, maintains this lower temperature.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] List two advantages of vegetative propagation.
    Ans: It is a quick and cheap method of multiplying plants, and the new plants are genetically identical to the parent, so desirable qualities such as fruit size and taste are retained. Plants that do not produce viable seeds, like banana and rose, can also be propagated this way.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] Why is variation useful to a species, and how does sexual reproduction produce it?
    Ans: Sexual reproduction combines DNA from two different individuals, so offspring differ from both parents. Variation means that when the environment changes or a disease strikes, some individuals are likely to survive, the population is not wiped out, and natural selection has raw material to work on.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] Explain regeneration in Planaria with a diagram description.
    Ans:
      If a Planaria is cut into several pieces, each piece can grow into a complete organism.
      Specialised cells at the wound surface proliferate (multiply) to form a mass of cells.
      These cells then move to their proper positions and redifferentiate into the various tissues and organs needed, so a whole new organism develops from the fragment.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] Distinguish between pollination and fertilisation.
    Ans:
      Pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from the anther to the stigma of the same or another flower of the same species; it needs an external agent such as wind, water or insects.
      Fertilisation is the fusion of the male gamete from the pollen tube with the female gamete (egg) inside the ovule, forming a zygote. It occurs inside the ovary and needs no external agent.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes: “The egg is fertilised in the uterus, where the zygote also settles and grows.” Correct the error and state where fertilisation and implantation each occur.
    Ans:
      Fertilisation occurs in the fallopian tube (oviduct), not in the uterus.
      The zygote divides as it moves down the oviduct and forms a ball of cells.
      This embryo then implants in the thick lining of the uterus, where the placenta forms to nourish it.

Q8. [Application · 3 marks] Name the mode of reproduction in (i) Hydra, (ii) Spirogyra, (iii) Rhizopus and (iv) Bryophyllum, and explain how any one of them takes place.
    Ans:
      (i) Hydra — budding; (ii) Spirogyra — fragmentation; (iii) Rhizopus — spore formation; (iv) Bryophyllum — vegetative propagation by leaves.
      In Hydra, regenerative cells divide repeatedly to form a bud on the parent’s body.
      The bud grows into a small Hydra with its own mouth and tentacles and then detaches to live independently.

Q9. [Short · 3 marks] Describe what happens in a flower after fertilisation.
    Ans:
      The male gamete fuses with the egg to form a zygote, which develops into an embryo.
      The ovule becomes the seed and its wall becomes the seed coat.
      The ovary grows into the fruit, while the petals, sepals and stamens wither and fall off.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    At a health camp, a doctor describes three birth-control methods: a condom, oral pills and the copper-T.
    
    (a) Which method physically stops the sperm from reaching the egg?
    (b) Which method changes the hormonal balance so that eggs are not released?
    (c) Which of these also lowers the risk of sexually transmitted diseases?
    Ans:
      (a) The condom, a barrier method.
      (b) Oral contraceptive pills.
      (c) The condom. (The copper-T prevents implantation but gives no protection against infection.)

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A student studies two flowers: a hibiscus, which has both stamens and carpels, and a papaya flower, which has only one of them.
    
    (a) What are bisexual and unisexual flowers?
    (b) Can a papaya flower pollinate itself? Why?
    (c) Which kind of pollination gives more variation?
    Ans:
      (a) A bisexual flower (hibiscus) has both stamens and carpels; a unisexual flower (papaya) has only stamens or only carpels.
      (b) No. A unisexual flower has only one sex, so pollen must be carried to a flower on another plant: cross-pollination.
      (c) Cross-pollination, because it combines genes from two different plants.

Q12. [Application · 4 marks] Describe the menstrual cycle and state what happens when the egg is not fertilised.
    Ans:
      Roughly once every 28 days one egg matures in an ovary and is released around day 14 (ovulation).
      Meanwhile the lining of the uterus thickens and develops blood vessels to receive a fertilised egg.
      If the egg is not fertilised, the thickened lining is no longer needed, breaks down, and is shed along with blood through the vagina for about three to five days — menstruation.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] Describe the structure and working of the human female reproductive system.
    Ans:
      Ovaries — a pair of glands that produce the female gamete (ovum) and the hormone oestrogen.
      Fallopian tubes (oviducts) — tubes with finger-like ends near the ovary and ciliated walls; the egg is captured here and fertilisation takes place in the tube.
      Uterus — a muscular bag where the fertilised egg implants and the embryo develops.
      Cervix and vagina — the cervix is the narrow opening of the uterus; the vagina is the passage through which sperm enter and the baby is delivered.
      Every month the uterus prepares a thick, spongy lining to receive a fertilised egg. If no fertilisation occurs, the lining breaks down and is discharged as blood and mucus through the vagina — this is menstruation. After fertilisation, the embryo is nourished by the mother's blood through the placenta.

Q14. [Long · 5 marks] Describe the human male reproductive system, naming the organs and the function of each.
    Ans:
      Testes: lie in the scrotum, outside the abdomen, where the lower temperature suits sperm production; they also secrete testosterone.
      Vas deferens: carries sperms from the testes towards the urethra.
      Seminal vesicles and prostate gland: add fluids to the sperms, which nourish them and make transport easier; together with the sperms this forms semen.
      Urethra: a common passage for semen and urine, which runs through the penis.
      Penis: the organ that delivers sperms into the female body during mating.

Q1. [Short · 1 mark] What is the sex chromosome combination in a human male, and what is the functional unit of inheritance?
    Ans: XY; the gene (a segment of DNA on a chromosome).

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] A man with blood group A marries a woman with blood group O and their daughter has blood group O. Is this enough to tell which trait is dominant? Why?
    Ans: No. From one family we cannot tell. If A were dominant, the father could be heterozygous (IAi) and the daughter could inherit i from both parents; but a single observation does not establish dominance, which requires studying many such crosses.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] Distinguish between acquired and inherited traits, with one example of each.
    Ans: An acquired trait develops during an organism's lifetime due to experience or environment and is not passed on — for example, a wrestler's well-developed muscles or a pierced ear. An inherited trait is present from birth and is passed on through the DNA of germ cells — for example, the colour of eyes or free ear lobes.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] Distinguish between genotype and phenotype with an example.
    Ans: Genotype is the genetic constitution of an organism (for example Tt), while phenotype is the observable character it produces (for example a tall plant). Because the tall allele is dominant, the genotypes TT and Tt both give the tall phenotype.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] Explain Mendel's monohybrid cross with a Punnett square.
    Ans:
      Pure tall (TT) × pure dwarf (tt). Gametes: T and t. F1: all Tt — tall, because tall is dominant and dwarf is recessive.
      Selfing F1 (Tt × Tt) gives gametes T and t from each parent. Punnett square → TT, Tt, Tt, tt.
      Phenotype: 3 tall : 1 dwarf. Genotype: 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt. The dwarf character was only masked in F1, not lost.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] How is the sex of a child determined in human beings? Show diagrammatically.
    Ans:
      Mother (XX) produces only X-bearing eggs; father (XY) produces X-bearing and Y-bearing sperms in equal numbers.
      X egg + X sperm → XX = girl; X egg + Y sperm → XY = boy.
      The probability of either is 50%, so the sex of the child is decided by chance and not by either parent's wish or action.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] In a cross between two F₁ pea plants (Tt × Tt), a student writes: “The ratio of tall to dwarf plants is 1 : 2 : 1.” What is the mistake? Write the correct phenotypic and genotypic ratios.
    Ans:
      The ratio 1 : 2 : 1 is the genotypic ratio (TT : Tt : tt), not the ratio of tall to dwarf plants.
      Both TT and Tt plants look tall, so the phenotypic ratio is tall : dwarf = 3 : 1.
      Genotype is the gene combination (TT, Tt, tt); phenotype is the visible trait (tall or dwarf).

Q8. [Application · 3 marks] In humans, free earlobes (F) are dominant over attached earlobes (f). A man with free earlobes, whose mother had attached earlobes, marries a woman with attached earlobes. What kinds of earlobes can their children have, and in what proportion?
    Ans:
      The man’s mother was ff, so he received f from her; he has free earlobes, so he is Ff. The woman is ff.
      Cross Ff × ff: gametes F, f from the man and only f from the woman.
      Children: 1 Ff : 1 ff, i.e. 50% free earlobes and 50% attached earlobes.

Q9. [Short · 3 marks] A tall pea plant (Tt) is crossed with a dwarf plant (tt). What proportion of the progeny will be tall?
    Ans:
      The tall parent produces two kinds of gametes, T and t; the dwarf parent produces only t.
      Combining them gives Tt, Tt, tt and tt.
      So half the progeny are Tt (tall) and half are tt (dwarf) — a 1 : 1 ratio. This cross with a recessive parent is called a test cross.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A population of red beetles lives on green bushes, and crows easily spot and eat the red beetles. In one reproductive event, a variation appears and a green beetle is born.
    
    (a) Why is the green beetle more likely to survive?
    (b) What happens to the colour of the beetle population over several generations?
    (c) Does this variation help the individual only, or the species? Explain.
    Ans:
      (a) Its green colour camouflages it against the green leaves, so crows find it harder to see.
      (b) Green beetles survive and breed, passing the colour to their offspring, so their number grows each generation while the red beetles decline.
      (c) It helps the species: because the variation is inherited, it spreads and increases the chance that the population survives the crows.

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A student crosses pure tall pea plants with pure dwarf pea plants. All F₁ plants are tall. When the F₁ plants are self-pollinated, the F₂ generation has 600 tall and 200 dwarf plants.
    
    (a) What is the ratio of tall to dwarf plants in F₂?
    (b) Which trait is recessive, and why was it not seen in F₁?
    (c) What does the result show about how traits are inherited?
    Ans:
      (a) 600 : 200 = 3 : 1.
      (b) Dwarfness is recessive. In F₁ every plant is Tt, and the dominant allele T masks t, so all look tall.
      (c) Each parent passes one copy of the factor for a trait; the factors do not blend, and the recessive trait reappears in F₂ when two copies of it come together.

Q12. [Application · 4 marks] A family blames the mother whenever a daughter is born. Using what you know about sex determination, write the reply you would give them.
    Ans:
      Human females have two X chromosomes, so every egg carries an X chromosome.
      Human males have one X and one Y, so half their sperm carry X and half carry Y.
      An X-bearing sperm fertilising the egg gives XX, a girl; a Y-bearing sperm gives XY, a boy. The egg always contributes an X, so it is the sperm — that is, the father — that decides the sex of the child.
      Repeated daughters in a family are a matter of chance, exactly like repeated tails when a coin is tossed; there is no biological basis for blaming the mother.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] Describe Mendel's dihybrid cross and state the conclusion drawn from it.
    Ans:
      He crossed pea plants with round-yellow seeds (RRYY) with plants having wrinkled-green seeds (rryy).
      F1: all plants had round-yellow seeds (RrYy) — round and yellow are dominant.
      On self-pollinating F1, F2 gave four phenotypes: 9 round-yellow : 3 round-green : 3 wrinkled-yellow : 1 wrinkled-green (16 combinations in the 4×4 Punnett square).
      Conclusion: the two pairs of characters are inherited independently of each other, which is why new combinations (round-green and wrinkled-green) appear in F2 that were absent in the parents. This is the law of independent assortment.

Q14. [Long · 5 marks] How do variations arise in sexually reproducing organisms? Explain with examples why variation is important for the survival of a species.
    Ans:
      Copying of DNA during reproduction is never perfectly accurate, so small differences (variations) arise between parent and offspring.
      In sexual reproduction the DNA of two different parents is combined, which produces much more variation than copying one parent alone.
      Some variations help an organism in a changed environment. For example, when a heat wave kills most bacteria in a pond, the few heat-tolerant ones survive and multiply.
      Similarly, a green beetle on green bushes escapes crows and leaves more offspring than a red beetle.
      A population with no variation could be wiped out by one change in its surroundings, so variation gives a species a chance to survive.

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (14)

Q1. [Short · 1 mark] State the mirror formula and the SI unit of the power of a lens.
    Ans: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f. The SI unit of power is the dioptre (D), equal to 1 m⁻¹.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Why is a convex mirror preferred as a rear-view mirror in vehicles?
    Ans: Because it always gives an erect, virtual and diminished image, and it has a wider field of view than a plane mirror, so the driver can see a much larger area of the traffic behind.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] A lens of power −4.0 D is placed in the path of light. Find its focal length and identify it.
    Ans: P = 1/f, so f = 1/(−4.0) = −0.25 m = −25 cm. Negative focal length means it is a concave (diverging) lens.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] Define the principal focus of a concave mirror and relate it to the radius of curvature.
    Ans: The principal focus is the point on the principal axis where rays parallel to the axis meet (or appear to meet) after reflection. For a spherical mirror the focus lies midway between the pole and the centre of curvature, so f = R/2.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] An object 4 cm high is placed 25 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 15 cm. Find the position, nature and size of the image.
    Ans:
      u = −25 cm, f = −15 cm. Using 1/v + 1/u = 1/f: 1/v = 1/(−15) − 1/(−25) = (−5 + 3)/75 = −2/75.
      So v = −37.5 cm — the image forms 37.5 cm in front of the mirror, hence real and inverted.
      m = −v/u = −(−37.5)/(−25) = −1.5, so h' = −1.5 × 4 = −6 cm: the image is 6 cm tall, inverted, and magnified.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] Draw a ray diagram for an object placed between C and F of a concave mirror and state the nature of the image.
    Ans:
      Draw the principal axis with P, F and C marked. Place the object (an arrow) between C and F.
      Ray 1: parallel to the axis, reflected through F. Ray 2: through F, reflected parallel to the axis. (A third ray through C retraces its path.)
      The rays meet beyond C, so the image is real, inverted and magnified. This is the arrangement used in a shaving or makeup mirror when the object is close.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] A student finds the power of a lens of focal length 20 cm as P = 1/f = 1/20 = 0.05 D. Find the mistake and give the correct power. What type of lens is it?
    Ans:
      Mistake: the focal length must be in metres for power in dioptres. f = 20 cm = 0.2 m.
      P = 1/f = 1/0.2 = +5 D.
      The focal length is positive, so the power is positive: it is a convex (converging) lens.

Q8. [Application · 3 marks] A dentist uses a concave mirror of focal length 2 cm to see an enlarged, erect image of a tooth. At what distance from the mirror should the tooth be held for an image three times as large? Where is the image?
    Ans:
      An erect, magnified image means m = +3, so v = −3u (with the sign convention).
      Mirror formula: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f gives 1/(−3u) + 1/u = 2/(3u) = 1/(−2), so u = −4/3 cm.
      The tooth must be held about 1.33 cm in front of the mirror. The image distance is v = −3u = +4 cm: a virtual image 4 cm behind the mirror.

Q9. [Short · 3 marks] An object 5 cm tall is placed 30 cm from a convex lens of focal length 20 cm. Find the position, size and nature of the image.
    Ans:
      Using the lens formula 1/f = 1/v − 1/u with f = +20 cm and u = −30 cm:
      1/v = 1/20 − 1/30 = (3 − 2)/60 = 1/60, so v = 60 cm.
      Magnification m = v/u = 60/(−30) = −2, so h₂ = m × h₁ = −2 × 5 = −10 cm.
      The image is real, inverted and twice the size, formed 60 cm on the other side of the lens.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A student places a candle 40 cm from a convex lens of focal length 20 cm and obtains a sharp image on a screen.
    
    (a) Where is the image formed?
    (b) State its nature and its size relative to the object.
    (c) What is the power of the lens?
    Ans:
      (a) Lens formula: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f gives 1/v = 1/20 − 1/40 = 1/40, so v = +40 cm (on the other side of the lens).
      (b) m = v/u = 40/(−40) = −1, so the image is real, inverted and the same size as the object (the object is at 2F).
      (c) P = 1/f = 1/0.2 m = +5 D.

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A coin lies at the bottom of an empty opaque cup, just out of sight. When water is poured gently into the cup, the coin becomes visible.
    
    (a) Name the phenomenon.
    (b) Explain why the coin becomes visible.
    (c) How does a ray bend as it passes from water into air?
    Ans:
      (a) Refraction of light.
      (b) Rays from the coin bend as they leave the water for the air, so they now reach the eye over the rim of the cup and the coin appears raised.
      (c) Going from the denser water into the rarer air, the ray bends away from the normal.

Q12. [Long · 4 marks] State the sign convention used for spherical mirrors and lenses, and apply it to a concave mirror.
    Ans:
      All distances are measured from the pole (mirror) or the optical centre (lens).
      Distances measured in the direction of the incident light are positive and those against it are negative; heights above the principal axis are positive and those below are negative.
      In practice the object distance u is always negative, the focal length of a concave mirror and of a concave lens is negative, and that of a convex mirror and a convex lens is positive.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] State the laws of refraction and solve: light enters from air into glass (n = 1.5) with an angle of incidence of 30°. Find the angle of refraction and the speed of light in glass.
    Ans:
      Laws: (i) the incident ray, the refracted ray and the normal at the point of incidence lie in the same plane; (ii) for a given pair of media and a given colour, sin i / sin r is constant (Snell's law).
      Angle of refraction: sin r = sin i / n = 0.5/1.5 = 0.3333, so r ≈ 19.5° — the ray bends toward the normal.
      Speed in glass: v = c/n = (3 × 10⁸)/1.5 = 2 × 10⁸ m/s.

Q14. [Long · 5 marks] An object 5 cm high is placed 20 cm in front of a convex mirror of focal length 10 cm. Find the position, nature and size of the image.
    Ans:
      Sign convention: u = −20 cm and f = +10 cm for a convex mirror.
      Mirror formula: 1/v = 1/f − 1/u = 1/10 + 1/20 = 3/20, so v = +20/3 ≈ +6.7 cm (behind the mirror).
      Magnification m = −v/u = −(20/3)/(−20) = +1/3.
      Image height h′ = m × h = (1/3) × 5 ≈ +1.7 cm.
      The image is virtual, erect and diminished, formed behind the mirror between the pole and the focus.

    Ans: (d) convex. Its image is always virtual, erect and smaller, which is why it is used as a rear-view mirror.

M8. When a ray of light goes from glass into air, it: (a) bends towards the normal  (b) bends away from the normal  (c) does not bend  (d) is totally absorbed
    Ans: (b) bends away from the normal. Moving from a denser to a rarer medium, the ray speeds up and bends away from the normal.

M9. An object is placed 20 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 10 cm. The image is formed: (a) 10 cm in front of the mirror  (b) 20 cm behind the mirror  (c) 20 cm in front of the mirror  (d) at infinity
    Ans: (c) 20 cm in front of the mirror. The object is at C (2f = 20 cm), so the image is also at C, real, inverted and the same size. Mirror formula: 1/v = 1/f − 1/u = −1/10 + 1/20 = −1/20, v = −20 cm.

M10. A ray of light enters a denser medium along the normal (angle of incidence 0°). It: (a) passes undeviated  (b) bends towards the normal  (c) bends away from the normal  (d) is totally reflected
    Ans: (a) passes undeviated. Refraction changes the speed but there is no bending when the ray is along the normal, because sin 0° = 0 on both sides of Snell's law.

M11. The image formed by a concave lens of a real object is always: (a) real and inverted  (b) virtual, erect and magnified  (c) virtual, erect and diminished  (d) real and magnified
    Ans: (c) virtual, erect and diminished. A diverging lens forms the image between F₁ and the optical centre on the same side as the object, whatever the object distance.

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (14)

Q1. [Short · 1 mark] Name the part of the eye that controls the amount of light entering it, and the least distance of distinct vision for a normal eye.
    Ans: The iris (which adjusts the size of the pupil); 25 cm.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Why do stars twinkle but planets do not?
    Ans: Starlight passes through layers of air of changing refractive index, so its path keeps changing slightly and the apparent brightness and position fluctuate — the star twinkles. Planets are much nearer and behave as a collection of many point sources, so the fluctuations from different parts cancel out and the total light remains steady.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] Why does the sun appear reddish early in the morning?
    Ans: Because near the horizon sunlight travels through a much thicker layer of air. The shorter blue wavelengths are scattered away, and mainly the longer red wavelengths reach our eyes, so the sun looks red. At noon the path is short, little blue light is scattered, and the sun looks white.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] What is the power of accommodation of the eye?
    Ans: It is the ability of the eye lens to change its focal length — by the action of the ciliary muscles — so that both near and distant objects are focused sharply on the retina. In a normal eye the near point is about 25 cm and the far point is infinity.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] What is myopia? State its two causes and its correction.
    Ans:
      Myopia (short-sightedness) is the defect in which a person can see nearby objects clearly but cannot see distant objects distinctly; the image of a distant object forms in front of the retina.
      Causes: (i) excessive curvature of the eye lens, (ii) elongation of the eyeball.
      Correction: a concave lens of suitable power, which diverges the light so that the image is formed exactly on the retina.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] Explain the formation of a rainbow in the sky.
    Ans:
      After rain, tiny water droplets remain suspended in the air and act as small glass prisms.
      Sunlight entering a droplet is first refracted and dispersed into its constituent colours, then undergoes total internal reflection at the inner surface of the droplet, and is refracted again as it leaves.
      Because different colours emerge at slightly different angles, the observer sees a coloured arc — a rainbow — with the sun always behind them.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] A student explains the twinkling of stars by saying, “Stars burn unevenly, so their brightness keeps changing.” Correct the explanation and say why planets do not twinkle.
    Ans:
      Twinkling is caused by atmospheric refraction, not by the star burning unevenly.
      Starlight passes through layers of air whose density and refractive index keep changing, so the star’s apparent position wobbles slightly and the amount of light entering the eye fluctuates.
      A planet is much nearer and acts like a collection of many point sources; the fluctuations average out, so a planet does not twinkle.

Q8. [Application · 3 marks] A student cannot see objects clearly beyond 80 cm. Name the defect, the type of corrective lens required, and calculate its power.
    Ans:
      The far point is only 80 cm, so the student is short-sighted (myopic).
      A concave lens is needed; it must form a virtual image of a distant object at 80 cm, so f = −80 cm = −0.8 m.
      P = 1/f = 1/(−0.8) = −1.25 D.

Q9. [Short · 3 marks] What is presbyopia? State its cause and how it is corrected.
    Ans:
      Presbyopia is the loss of the eye's power of accommodation with age: the ciliary muscles weaken and the lens becomes less flexible.
      As a result the near point recedes and reading or threading a needle becomes difficult.
      It is corrected with converging (convex) spectacles; when a person has both myopia and presbyopia, bifocal lenses are used.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Rahul sees distant objects clearly but cannot read a book held at the normal distance of 25 cm.
    
    (a) Name the defect.
    (b) Give two possible causes.
    (c) Name the corrective lens and explain how it helps.
    Ans:
      (a) Hypermetropia (far-sightedness).
      (b) The focal length of the eye lens is too long, or the eyeball is too short, so the image of a near object forms behind the retina.
      (c) A convex lens: it converges the rays a little before they enter the eye, so the image forms on the retina.

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A narrow beam of white light passes through a glass prism and forms a band of seven colours on a screen. When the same beam passes through a rectangular glass slab, no band of colours appears.
    
    (a) Name the phenomenon seen with the prism.
    (b) Which colour bends the most and which the least?
    (c) Why does the rectangular slab not show it?
    Ans:
      (a) Dispersion of white light.
      (b) Violet bends the most and red the least.
      (c) The opposite faces of a slab are parallel, so the colours spread on entering are brought back together on leaving, and emerge as white light again.

Q12. [Application · 4 marks] Explain why the sky looks blue and the sun looks white at noon but red at sunrise.
    Ans:
      Air molecules and fine particles scatter light of short wavelength much more strongly than long wavelength light (scattering ∝ 1/λ⁴).
      Blue light is scattered the most, so light reaching the eye from all parts of the sky is rich in blue.
      At noon the sun is overhead and its light crosses the shortest possible path, so very little blue is removed and the sun looks white.
      Near sunrise and sunset the path through the atmosphere is much longer, most of the blue is scattered away, and the remaining direct light looks red.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] Explain atmospheric refraction and describe any three phenomena based on it.
    Ans:
      Atmospheric refraction is the bending of light as it passes through layers of air of different densities; the refractive index of air decreases as we go up, so light from a celestial body bends gradually toward the normal.
      (1) Twinkling of stars: the continuously changing refractive index makes the apparent position and brightness of a star fluctuate.
      (2) Advance of sunrise and delay of sunset: light from the sun below the horizon is refracted toward the earth, so the sun becomes visible about two minutes before actual sunrise and remains visible about two minutes after sunset — the day is lengthened by about 4 minutes.
      (3) Apparent flattening of the sun's disc at sunrise and sunset, and stars appearing slightly higher than their true position, are also caused by the same refraction.

Q14. [Long · 5 marks] Describe how the human eye forms an image of an object and how it adjusts to see objects at different distances.
    Ans:
      Light enters through the transparent cornea, which does most of the bending of light; the iris controls the size of the pupil and so the amount of light entering.
      The eye lens focuses the light and forms a real, inverted image on the retina.
      The retina contains light-sensitive cells that produce electrical signals, which the optic nerve carries to the brain; the brain interprets the image as erect.
      For different distances, the ciliary muscles change the curvature of the lens, and so its focal length. This is called accommodation.
      For nearby objects the lens becomes thicker (shorter focal length); for distant objects it becomes thinner.

M1. The change in focal length of the eye lens is caused by the action of the: (a) pupil (b) retina (c) iris (d) ciliary muscles
    Ans: (d) ciliary muscles — they change the curvature of the crystalline lens, giving the eye its power of accommodation.

M2. The danger signals installed at the top of tall buildings are red because red light: (a) is scattered the most by smoke or fog (b) is scattered the least by smoke or fog (c) is absorbed the most (d) moves fastest in air
    Ans: (b) is scattered the least — being of the longest wavelength, so it can be seen from a greater distance through fog or smoke.

M3. A person cannot read a newspaper without glasses. He is probably suffering from: (a) myopia (b) astigmatism (c) hypermetropia (d) night blindness
    Ans: (c) hypermetropia — difficulty in seeing near objects clearly, corrected with a convex lens.

M4. The eye defect hypermetropia is corrected by using a: (a) convex lens  (b) concave lens  (c) cylindrical lens  (d) bifocal lens with a concave upper part
    Ans: (a) convex lens. In hypermetropia the image forms behind the retina, so a converging (convex) lens is used.

M5. The part of the eye that contains the light-sensitive cells is the: (a) retina  (b) cornea  (c) iris  (d) pupil

M6. The eye defect in which the far point is nearer than infinity is: (a) hypermetropia  (b) myopia  (c) presbyopia  (d) cataract

M7. The splitting of white light into its component colours is called: (a) dispersion  (b) scattering  (c) total internal reflection  (d) polarisation

M8. Stars twinkle mainly because of: (a) dispersion  (b) reflection from clouds  (c) their own flickering  (d) atmospheric refraction

M9. The least distance of distinct vision for a young adult with normal vision is about: (a) 2.5 cm  (b) 25 m  (c) 25 cm  (d) infinity

M10. The clear sky appears blue because: (a) blue light is absorbed the most  (b) shorter wavelengths are scattered more by air molecules  (c) the sea reflects blue light upwards  (d) the atmosphere refracts blue light towards the eye
    Ans: (b) shorter wavelengths are scattered more by air molecules. Fine particles scatter blue light far more than red, so scattered blue reaches the eye from every direction.

M11. A student with myopia is prescribed a lens of power −2.0 D. The lens is: (a) convex, focal length 50 cm  (b) concave, focal length 2 m  (c) concave, focal length 50 cm  (d) convex, focal length 2 m
    Ans: (c) concave, focal length 50 cm. f = 1/P = 1/(−2) m = −0.5 m; the negative sign means a concave (diverging) lens, which corrects myopia.

Q1. [Short · 1 mark] Define one ohm and name the device used to measure potential difference.
    Ans: One ohm is the resistance of a conductor when a potential difference of 1 volt across its ends produces a current of 1 ampere through it. Potential difference is measured with a voltmeter, connected in parallel.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Why are the coils of electric toasters and electric irons made of an alloy rather than a pure metal?
    Ans: Because alloys have higher resistivity than the pure metals, so they produce more heat for the same current, and they do not oxidise (burn) readily at high temperatures.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] An electric iron of rating 750 W, 220 V is used for 2 hours daily. Calculate the cost of using it for 30 days at ₹5 per unit.
    Ans:
      Energy per day = P × t = 750 W × 2 h = 1500 Wh = 1.5 kWh.
      Energy for 30 days = 1.5 × 30 = 45 kWh = 45 units.
      Cost = 45 × ₹5 = ₹225.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] Why are household appliances connected in parallel and not in series?
    Ans: In a parallel circuit every appliance gets the full supply voltage and works independently, so switching one off does not affect the others and a fault in one branch does not stop the rest — none of which is possible in a series circuit.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] State Ohm's law and draw the V–I graph for an ohmic conductor.
    Ans:
      At constant temperature, the current flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across its ends: V = IR, where R is the resistance.
      The graph of V against I is a straight line passing through the origin; its slope (V/I) gives the resistance of the conductor.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] Three resistors of 2 Ω, 3 Ω and 6 Ω are connected in parallel. Find the equivalent resistance.
    Ans:
      1/Rp = 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/6 = (3 + 2 + 1)/6 = 6/6 = 1.
      So Rp = 1 Ω — less than the smallest resistor (2 Ω), as expected for a parallel combination.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] A student finds the equivalent resistance of 6 Ω and 3 Ω connected in parallel as 6 + 3 = 9 Ω. Identify the mistake and find the correct value.
    Ans:
      Mistake: resistances are added directly only in series. In parallel the reciprocals are added.
      1/R = 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/6 + 2/6 = 3/6 = 1/2.
      R = 2 Ω, which is smaller than the smallest resistance (3 Ω), as it must be in a parallel combination.

Q8. [Application · 3 marks] A copper wire has a resistance of 10 Ω. What will be the resistance of a wire of the same material that is twice as long and has half the area of cross-section?
    Ans:
      Resistance R = ρl/A, so for a given material R is proportional to l/A.
      New length = 2l and new area = A/2, so the new resistance = ρ(2l)/(A/2) = 4 × ρl/A.
      New resistance = 4 × 10 = 40 Ω.

Q9. [Short · 3 marks] Resistors of 4 Ω and 12 Ω are connected in parallel and the combination is joined to a 6 V battery. Find the equivalent resistance and the current drawn from the battery.
    Ans:
      1/Rₚ = 1/4 + 1/12 = (3 + 1)/12 = 4/12, so Rₚ = 3 Ω.
      Current I = V/R = 6/3 = 2 A.
      Check: the branch currents are 6/4 = 1.5 A and 6/12 = 0.5 A, which add to 2 A. Note the equivalent resistance is less than the smaller resistor.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A 12 V battery is connected in series to resistors of 4 Ω and 2 Ω, an ammeter, and a voltmeter across the 4 Ω resistor.
    
    (a) Find the current in the circuit.
    (b) Find the voltmeter reading.
    (c) Find the heat produced in the 2 Ω resistor in 10 s.
    Ans:
      (a) Total resistance = 4 + 2 = 6 Ω, so I = V/R = 12/6 = 2 A.
      (b) V across 4 Ω = IR = 2 × 4 = 8 V.
      (c) H = I²Rt = 2² × 2 × 10 = 80 J.

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A household uses three 40 W bulbs for 5 hours daily, a 1 kW heater for 2 hours daily and a 100 W fan for 10 hours daily.
    
    (a) Find the electrical energy used per day in kWh.
    (b) How many units are used in 30 days?
    (c) What is the monthly cost at Rs 7 per unit?
    Ans:
      (a) Bulbs: 0.12 kW × 5 h = 0.6 kWh; heater: 1 kW × 2 h = 2 kWh; fan: 0.1 kW × 10 h = 1 kWh. Total = 3.6 kWh per day.
      (b) 3.6 × 30 = 108 units (1 unit = 1 kWh).
      (c) 108 × Rs 7 = Rs 756.

Q12. [Application · 4 marks] A 100 W bulb and a 60 W bulb are both rated at 220 V. Which has the higher resistance, and what current does each draw?
    Ans:
      Using R = V²/P: R₁₀₀ = 220²/100 = 484 Ω and R₆₀ = 220²/60 ≈ 806.7 Ω.
      So the 60 W bulb has the higher resistance — the lower-power bulb resists the current more.
      Currents: I = P/V, so the 100 W bulb draws 100/220 ≈ 0.45 A and the 60 W bulb draws 60/220 ≈ 0.27 A.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] A 4 Ω resistor and a 6 Ω resistor are connected in series with a 20 V battery. Find the total resistance, the current, the potential difference across each resistor, and the heat produced in the 4 Ω resistor in 1 minute.
    Ans:
      Total resistance Rs = 4 + 6 = 10 Ω.
      Current I = V/R = 20/10 = 2 A — the same through both resistors.
      Potential differences: across 4 Ω, V = 2 × 4 = 8 V; across 6 Ω, V = 2 × 6 = 12 V. (8 + 12 = 20 V ✓)
      Heat in the 4 Ω resistor H = I²Rt = (2)² × 4 × 60 = 960 J.

Q14. [Long · 5 marks] State Joule’s law of heating. Derive the expression H = I²Rt and give three applications of the heating effect of current.
    Ans:
      Joule’s law: the heat produced in a resistor is directly proportional to the square of the current, to its resistance and to the time for which the current flows.
      Derivation: charge Q = It flows through potential difference V, so work done (energy converted) W = VQ = VIt.
      By Ohm’s law V = IR, so H = (IR) × I × t = I²Rt.
      Applications: electric heater, iron and toaster (coils of high-resistance alloy); the fuse (it melts when the current is too high); the filament of an electric bulb (tungsten glows white-hot).

M10. Three 6 Ω resistors are connected in parallel. Their equivalent resistance is: (a) 18 Ω  (b) 6 Ω  (c) 2 Ω  (d) 3 Ω

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (14)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 1 mark] State Fleming's right-hand rule.
    Ans: Stretch the thumb, forefinger and middle finger of the right hand so that they are mutually perpendicular. If the forefinger points in the direction of the magnetic field and the thumb in the direction of motion of the conductor, the middle finger gives the direction of the induced current.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Why do two magnetic field lines never intersect each other?
    Ans: Because at the point of intersection there would be two directions of the magnetic field for a single point, which is impossible — a compass needle placed there could not point in two directions at once.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] Differentiate between an AC generator and a DC generator.
    Ans: An AC generator uses two complete slip rings, and the current it produces reverses direction periodically (50 Hz in India). A DC generator uses a split-ring commutator, so although the current in the coil reverses, the current in the external circuit always flows in one direction.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] State the rule used to find the direction of the magnetic field around a straight current-carrying conductor.
    Ans: Right-hand thumb rule: imagine holding the conductor in your right hand with the thumb pointing along the current; the direction in which your fingers curl around the conductor is the direction of the magnetic field lines, which are concentric circles around the wire.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] Explain the principle and working of an electric motor.
    Ans:
      Principle: a current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field experiences a force, given by Fleming's left-hand rule.
      Working: a rectangular coil ABCD is placed between the poles of a magnet. When current flows, one arm is pushed up and the other down, so the coil rotates.
      The split-ring commutator reverses the direction of current in the coil after every half rotation, so the coil keeps rotating in the same direction.
      Uses: electric fans, mixers, washing machines and pumps.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] What is electromagnetic induction? Describe an activity to demonstrate it.
    Ans:
      When the magnetic field linked with a coil changes, a current is induced in the coil — this is electromagnetic induction.
      Activity: connect a galvanometer to a coil. Push a bar magnet's north pole into the coil and the galvanometer needle deflects momentarily in one direction; pull it out and it deflects in the opposite direction; hold it still inside and there is no deflection.
      The deflection shows that a current is induced only when the magnetic flux through the coil is changing, i.e. only while the magnet is in relative motion.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] A student draws the field lines of a bar magnet as coming out of the south pole and entering the north pole outside the magnet. Correct the statement and state two properties of magnetic field lines.
    Ans:
      Outside the magnet, field lines run from the north pole to the south pole; inside the magnet they run from south to north, so each line is a closed loop.
      Property 1: the lines are closed curves that never cross one another.
      Property 2: the field is strongest where the lines are closest together, i.e. near the poles.

Q8. [Application · 3 marks] A coil of wire is connected to a galvanometer. When a bar magnet is pushed into the coil, the needle deflects. What happens (i) when the magnet is held still inside the coil, (ii) when it is pulled out, and (iii) how can the deflection be increased?
    Ans:
      (i) No deflection: a current is induced only while the magnetic field linked with the coil is changing.
      (ii) The needle deflects in the opposite direction, because the change in the field is reversed.
      (iii) Move the magnet faster, use a stronger magnet or use a coil with more turns. This is electromagnetic induction.

Q9. [Short · 3 marks] How does the strength of the magnetic field at the centre of a current-carrying circular coil depend on the current, the number of turns and the radius of the coil?
    Ans:
      It is directly proportional to the current flowing through the coil.
      It is directly proportional to the number of turns, because the field of each turn adds up.
      It is inversely proportional to the radius of the coil, so a smaller coil gives a stronger field. This is the principle behind electromagnets and solenoids.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A student winds insulated copper wire round an iron nail and connects the ends to a battery. The nail picks up small iron pins, but drops them when the battery is disconnected.
    
    (a) Name this device.
    (b) Why does it hold the pins only while the current flows?
    (c) State two ways to make it stronger.
    Ans:
      (a) An electromagnet (a solenoid with a soft iron core).
      (b) The current-carrying coil magnetises the iron core; soft iron loses its magnetism as soon as the current stops, so the pins fall.
      (c) Increase the number of turns of the coil, or increase the current (a soft iron core also strengthens it).

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A socket in a house is protected by a 5 A fuse, and the supply is 220 V.
    
    (a) Find the current drawn by a 1 kW heater.
    (b) Will the fuse blow if a 2 kW geyser is plugged in instead? Justify.
    (c) State the purpose of the fuse and of the earth wire.
    Ans:
      (a) I = P/V = 1000/220 ≈ 4.5 A, which is below 5 A.
      (b) For the geyser, I = 2000/220 ≈ 9.1 A, which is above 5 A, so the fuse wire heats up, melts and breaks the circuit.
      (c) The fuse protects appliances and wiring from overloading and short circuits. The earth wire joins the metal body of an appliance to the ground, so any leakage current flows to earth and does not give a shock.

Q12. [Application · 4 marks] Explain the working of an electric generator, including the role of slip rings and the commutator.
    Ans:
      A coil is rotated mechanically in a magnetic field, so the magnetic flux linked with it keeps changing and an induced current is produced (electromagnetic induction).
      The direction of the induced current reverses every half rotation, which is found by Fleming's right-hand rule.
      In an AC generator two slip rings keep the coil connected to the external circuit without reversing the connections, so the output is alternating current.
      A DC generator replaces them with a split-ring commutator, which reverses the connection to the external circuit every half turn so the output flows in one direction.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] Describe a domestic electric circuit and explain the function of the earth wire and the fuse.
    Ans:
      The supply has three wires: live (red, at about 220 V), neutral (black, at zero potential) and earth (green).
      Appliances are connected in parallel so that each receives the full supply voltage, each has its own switch, and the failure of one does not affect the others. Separate 15 A and 5 A circuits are provided for heavy and light appliances.
      Earth wire: the metallic body of an appliance is connected to a metal plate buried in the earth, so if the live wire touches the body the leakage current flows safely into the ground instead of through a person — preventing a fatal shock.
      Fuse: a short piece of wire of low melting point connected in series with the appliance. If the current exceeds the safe value, the fuse wire melts and breaks the circuit, protecting the appliance from damage due to overloading or short circuiting.

Q14. [Long · 5 marks] Describe the magnetic field produced by a current-carrying solenoid and explain how an electromagnet is made from it.
    Ans:
      A solenoid is a coil of many closely wound circular turns of insulated copper wire.
      The field lines inside the solenoid are parallel straight lines, so the field there is uniform; outside, the pattern is like that of a bar magnet.
      One end of the solenoid behaves as a north pole and the other as a south pole.
      If a piece of soft iron is placed inside the coil, the strong field magnetises it, and the combination is an electromagnet.
      It is a temporary magnet: it is magnetised only while the current flows. It is used in cranes for lifting iron, in electric bells and in MRI machines.

M1. The direction of the magnetic field produced by a straight current-carrying wire is given by: (a) Fleming's left-hand rule (b) Fleming's right-hand rule (c) Lenz's law (d) the right-hand thumb rule
    Ans: (d) the right-hand thumb rule — hold the wire in the right hand with the thumb along the current; the curled fingers give the direction of the field lines.

M3. The frequency of AC supply in India is 50 Hz. The direction of the current changes: (a) 50 times per second (b) 25 times per second (c) 200 times per second (d) 100 times per second
    Ans: (d) 100 times per second — the direction reverses twice in each cycle.

M4. Outside a bar magnet, the magnetic field lines run: (a) from the south pole to the north pole  (b) in straight lines parallel to the axis only  (c) from the centre to both poles  (d) from the north pole to the south pole
    Ans: (d) from the north pole to the south pole. By convention the field lines emerge from the north pole and enter the south pole outside the magnet, and continue from south to north inside it.

M5. A straight conductor carrying current is placed in a magnetic field. The force on it is greatest when the current is: (a) parallel to the field  (b) at 45° to the field  (c) perpendicular to the field  (d) zero
    Ans: (c) perpendicular to the field. The force F = BIl sin θ is maximum at θ = 90°, and zero when the conductor is parallel to the field.

M6. The magnetic field lines inside a long current-carrying solenoid are: (a) concentric circles  (b) parallel straight lines  (c) diverging curves  (d) absent
    Ans: (b) parallel straight lines. The field inside a solenoid is nearly uniform, like the field inside a bar magnet.

M7. An electric motor converts: (a) electrical energy into mechanical energy  (b) mechanical energy into electrical energy  (c) heat into light  (d) chemical energy into sound
    Ans: (a) electrical energy into mechanical energy. A generator does the reverse conversion.

M8. The insulation on the earth wire in a domestic circuit is: (a) green  (b) red  (c) black  (d) blue
    Ans: (a) green. Live is red or brown, neutral is black or blue, and earth is green or green-yellow.

M9. The magnetic field at a point near a long straight current-carrying wire: (a) increases as you move away from the wire  (b) does not depend on the current  (c) decreases as you move away from the wire  (d) is zero everywhere around the wire
    Ans: (c) decreases as you move away from the wire. The concentric field lines get farther apart with distance; the field is stronger for a larger current and closer to the wire.

M10. Fleming's left-hand rule gives the direction of the: (a) induced current in a coil  (b) force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field  (c) magnetic field due to a current  (d) current produced by a generator
    Ans: (b) force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field. Forefinger = field, middle finger = current, thumb = force (motor). The right-hand rule is for generators.

M11. The production of a current in a closed coil by a changing magnetic field is called: (a) electromagnetic induction  (b) magnetic effect of current  (c) electrolysis  (d) electrostatic induction
    Ans: (a) electromagnetic induction. Faraday's discovery — the basis of the electric generator; the induced current direction is given by Fleming's right-hand rule.

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (13)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 1 mark] Name the organisms that always occupy the first trophic level of a food chain, and define an ecosystem.
    Ans: Green plants and blue-green algae (producers). An ecosystem is a self-sustaining system in which all the living organisms of an area interact with each other and with the non-living (abiotic) components such as soil, water and air.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Why are some substances biodegradable and some non-biodegradable?
    Ans: Because micro-organisms such as bacteria and fungi produce enzymes that can break down only certain substances like paper, wood and vegetable waste. Substances such as plastic, glass and DDT cannot be acted upon by these enzymes, so they remain in the environment for a very long time and are called non-biodegradable.

Q3. [Application · 2 marks] Suggest two ways to reduce the problem of waste disposal.
    Ans: Segregate waste at the source and send biodegradable waste for composting or vermicomposting; and practise the 5 R's — refuse what is unnecessary, reduce consumption, reuse containers, repurpose items, and recycle paper, plastic, glass and metal.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] Distinguish between a food chain and a food web.
    Ans: A food chain is a single linear sequence showing who eats whom, for example grass → grasshopper → frog → snake. A food web is a network of many interconnected food chains in an ecosystem; because each organism can feed at more than one level, the web is far more stable than a single chain.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] In a food chain, if 10,000 joules of solar energy is available to the producers, how much energy will be available to the secondary consumer?
    Ans:
      Producers convert about 1% of the solar energy into food energy, but taking 10,000 J as the energy in the producers: primary consumer receives 10% = 1,000 J.
      Secondary consumer receives 10% of 1,000 J = 100 J.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] What is ozone? How is it formed, and why is damage to the ozone layer a cause for concern?
    Ans:
      Ozone is a molecule of oxygen with three atoms, O3, found in the upper atmosphere.
      Formation: ultraviolet radiation splits an oxygen molecule into free oxygen atoms, O2 →(UV) 2O; each free atom combines with an oxygen molecule to form ozone, O + O2 → O3.
      Concern: the ozone layer absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation from the sun. Its depletion, caused mainly by CFCs from refrigerators, air conditioners and aerosols, allows more UV to reach the earth, causing skin cancer, damage to the eyes, reduced crop yields and harm to marine organisms. The Montreal Protocol of 1987 froze CFC production to protect it.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] A student draws the food chain as Hawk → Snake → Frog → Grasshopper → Grass. What is wrong? Redraw it correctly and name the trophic levels.
    Ans:
      An arrow shows the direction of energy flow, from the food to the organism that eats it. The student’s arrows point from predator to prey.
      Correct chain: Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Hawk.
      Trophic levels: grass is the producer (1st); grasshopper the primary consumer (2nd); frog the secondary consumer (3rd); snake the tertiary consumer (4th); hawk the top consumer (5th).

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] Explain the role of decomposers in an ecosystem.
    Ans:
      Decomposers such as bacteria and fungi break down the dead bodies of plants and animals and their wastes into simple inorganic substances.
      These substances — mineral salts, carbon dioxide and water — go back into the soil and air and are reused by the producers.
      They also clean up the environment. Without decomposers nutrients would stay locked in dead matter and the cycling of matter would stop.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    In a grassland food chain, the producers capture 20,000 J of energy.
    
    (a) How much energy reaches the herbivores?
    (b) How much energy reaches the carnivores that eat the herbivores?
    (c) Why do food chains rarely have more than four or five trophic levels?
    Ans:
      (a) By the 10% law, herbivores get 10% of 20,000 J = 2,000 J.
      (b) The carnivores get 10% of 2,000 J = 200 J.
      (c) About 90% of the energy at each level is lost as heat in life processes, so very little is left to support more levels.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A sealed glass jar contains water, a few water plants, small fish and snails, and some soil at the bottom. It is kept in sunlight.
    
    (a) Name the producers, consumers and decomposers in the jar.
    (b) What is the role of sunlight?
    (c) What would happen if the jar were kept in the dark for a long time?
    Ans:
      (a) Producers: the water plants (and algae); consumers: the fish and snails; decomposers: the bacteria and fungi in the water and soil.
      (b) Sunlight is the source of energy: the plants use it for photosynthesis, which provides food and oxygen for the other organisms.
      (c) Without light, the plants could not photosynthesise, so food and oxygen would run out and the organisms would die.

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] A village panchayat plans to burn its collected plastic waste in the open. Advise them, giving reasons from what you have studied about ecosystems.
    Ans:
      Open burning of plastic releases poisonous gases such as hydrogen chloride and dioxins into the air, harming people, animals and crops.
      Plastics are non-biodegradable, so if they are buried or dumped they persist in the soil for decades and choke drains and the guts of animals.
      Better: segregate waste at source, send recyclable plastic for recycling, compost the biodegradable fraction, and replace single-use plastic with cloth or paper bags.
      This keeps harmful chemicals out of the soil and water, so they cannot enter food chains and build up by biological magnification.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] What is biological magnification? Will the level of magnification be different at different levels of the ecosystem?
    Ans:
      Biological magnification is the progressive increase in the concentration of non-biodegradable harmful chemicals at successive trophic levels of a food chain.
      Yes, the level differs. Because these chemicals are not metabolised or excreted, they pass on to the next level, and the amount of food consumed at each level is large, so the concentration keeps increasing.
      Example: DDT sprayed on crops is absorbed by plants; grasshoppers eating many plants accumulate more; frogs eating many grasshoppers more still; and hawks at the top accumulate the highest concentration. Human beings, being at the top of many food chains, are among the most affected.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] What is a trophic level? Explain the flow of energy in an ecosystem and why it is unidirectional.
    Ans:
      A trophic level is a step in a food chain, for example producers, herbivores and carnivores.
      The ultimate source of energy is the Sun; producers capture it through photosynthesis and pass it on when eaten.
      At each step only about 10% of the energy is passed on to the next level (the 10% law); the rest is lost as heat in respiration and other life processes.
      The energy lost as heat cannot be used again by the producers, so energy flows in one direction only, from the Sun to producers to consumers.
      Because so little energy remains, food chains are short, and plants always occupy the first level.

B. MCQs (8)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. In the food chain Grass → Deer → Lion, if 10,000 J of energy is available to the grass, the energy available to the lion is: (a) 1000 J (b) 100 J (c) 10 J (d) 1 J
    Ans: (b) 100 J. Grass 10,000 J → deer 1,000 J → lion 100 J (10% at each transfer).

M2. The depletion of the ozone layer is mainly caused by: (a) CFCs (b) CO2 (c) methane (d) SO2
    Ans: (a) CFCs — chlorofluorocarbons from refrigerators, air conditioners and fire extinguishers destroy ozone molecules.

M3. Which of the following groups contains only biodegradable substances? (a) paper, plastic, wood (b) glass, wood, plastic (c) lime juice, grass, aluminium foil (d) vegetable peels, paper, lime juice
    Ans: (d) — all three are broken down by the action of micro-organisms.

M4. Which of the following is a non-biodegradable waste? (a) vegetable peelings (b) a plastic bag (c) a paper bag (d) a cotton cloth
    Ans: (b) a plastic bag. Microorganisms cannot break down plastics, so they persist in the environment for a very long time.

M5. In the food chain Plant → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake, the secondary consumer is the: (a) grasshopper (b) snake (c) frog (d) plant
    Ans: (c) frog. The grasshopper is the primary consumer and the frog, which eats it, is the secondary consumer.

M6. The ‘10% law’ in an ecosystem describes the transfer of: (a) energy  (b) water  (c) minerals  (d) oxygen
    Ans: (a) energy. Only about 10% of the energy at one trophic level is stored in the next; the rest is lost as heat and in life processes.

M7. Which of these is a renewable resource? (a) coal  (b) petroleum  (c) sunlight  (d) natural gas
    Ans: (c) sunlight. Coal, petroleum and natural gas are fossil fuels that take millions of years to form.

M8. The ozone layer is being depleted mainly by: (a) methane  (b) nitrogen fertilisers  (c) carbon monoxide  (d) CFCs
    Ans: (d) CFCs. Chlorofluorocarbons from refrigerants and aerosol sprays release chlorine atoms that destroy ozone molecules.
