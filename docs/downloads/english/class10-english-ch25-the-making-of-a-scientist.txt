A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] How did Richard Ebright's mother help him in his work?
    Ans: She was his only companion after his father's death, and she encouraged his learning in every way. She took him on trips, bought him telescopes, microscopes, cameras and other scientific equipment, and always found work for him to do. Most importantly she gave him the children's book The Travels of Monarch X, which opened the world of science to him and started him on his work with butterflies.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What did Ebright discover about the twelve gold spots on a monarch pupa?
    Ans: He found that the twelve tiny gold spots on a monarch pupa were not merely ornamental, as was thought, but produced a hormone necessary for the full development of the butterfly. He demonstrated this by removing the spots and showing the effect, and the discovery won him the county science fair and a place at the National Insect Collection.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] What qualities go into the making of a scientist?
    Ans: According to the chapter, three things are needed: a first-rate mind, curiosity, and the will to win for the right reasons — not merely to win, but to do the best job one can. Richard Ebright had all three, and he also had the advantage of a mother who encouraged him and a teacher who opened his mind to ideas and taught him to compete in a healthy way.

Q4. [Application · 3 marks] Richard Ebright's early science-fair entry did not win. Explain why that setback helped him become a better investigator.
    Ans:
      At first he displayed collected butterfly specimens instead of asking a scientific question or testing an idea.
      The result showed him that a science fair rewarded investigation, not simply a large collection.
      He began designing experiments, learning from each project and asking questions that could be answered with evidence.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A young student believes that winning is the only purpose of a science project. What lesson from Ebright's teacher and later work would you give the student?
    Ans:
      Richard A. Weiherer taught Ebright to compete by doing the best job he could, not by winning at any cost.
      Ebright's progress came from curiosity, careful experiments and learning from setbacks; the work mattered more than a prize.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] What did Richard Ebright learn from his monarch butterfly research?
    Ans:
      He began by tagging monarch butterflies to track their migration to Mexico and beyond.
      He discovered what caused a viral disease that kills nearly all monarchs, and later, with a friend, he found that the gold spots on the pupa are not decorative.
      The spots produce a hormone needed for the full development of the butterfly.
      This work won him the Searle scholarship, the Schering Plough award and a place in the Intel Science Talent Search.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] How did Ebright's failure at the county science fair in seventh grade help him later?
    Ans: Young Ebright exhibited slides of frog tissues — neat work, but only display, not experiment — and won nothing. The failure taught him what real science needs: a question, an experiment and an answer. Thereafter he did genuine investigations, starting with the viral disease of monarch caterpillars, and began winning every fair he entered.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:

    As a child, Ebright collected butterflies and entered a science fair with a display of specimens. He did not win. Later, he designed experiments and investigated questions about monarch butterflies, including the function of the gold spots on a pupa.

    (a) Why was the first entry not a strong scientific investigation?
    (b) What change did Ebright make after the setback?
    (c) What qualities does this development show?
    Ans:
      A display of specimens showed his collection but did not test a question or present an experiment.
      He began doing investigations, using experiments to explore questions about monarchs and their development.
      He showed curiosity, persistence and willingness to learn from failure, then improved his work through evidence and experimentation.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:

    Ebright's mother encouraged his interests and supplied books and materials. His teacher urged him to compete in order to do his best work. Ebright later continued his research beyond the science fair.

    (a) How did his mother support his learning?
    (b) What did his teacher mean by competing to do the best job?
    (c) Why was support not enough on its own?
    Ans:
      His mother encouraged his curiosity, helped him find resources and supported projects that kept him learning.
      The aim was to do careful, high-quality work, not to defeat others by any means.
      Ebright still had to supply his own curiosity, effort and experiments; guidance created opportunities but did not do the scientific work for him.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] What qualities made Richard Ebright a scientist?
    Ans:
      He had first-rate curiosity and a mind that never stopped: he collected butterflies, rocks, fossils and coins from childhood.
      He was competitive in the best sense — when he lost a county science fair, he resolved to do a real experiment the next year.
      He was a hard worker and a careful observer, and he had the drive to write to a top scientist, which led to work at a laboratory.
      Most of all, he had a mother who encouraged him, teachers and mentors who took him seriously, and the imagination to ask why.

Q11. [Application · 4 marks] ‘A scientist needs a first-rate mind, curiosity and the will to win for the right reasons.’ Show how Ebright's life illustrates each of these three.
    Ans:
      A first-rate mind — he designed real experiments, like proving the gold spots produce a hormone, and published in a scientific journal while still at school.
      Curiosity — from collecting butterflies and rocks as a child to chasing the question of cell life, he never stopped asking why.
      The will to win for the right reasons — his teacher Weiherer taught him to compete to do the best job he could, not merely to beat others.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] How do curiosity, persistence and guidance combine to make Richard Ebright a scientist?
    Ans:
      Ebright's curiosity begins with butterflies and grows into questions about how monarchs develop; he does not stop at collecting specimens.
      His first science-fair setback teaches him that a display alone is not an investigation, so he begins testing ideas and learning from results.
      His mother supplies encouragement and resources, while teacher Richard A. Weiherer shapes his attitude to competition and good work.
      Ebright's later success depends on his own persistence and first-rate research as well as this support. The story presents science as a process of questioning, testing and improving.

B. MCQs (8)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. The book that opened the world of science to Richard Ebright was: (a) The Travels of Monarch X (b) The Making of a Scientist (c) The Diary of a Young Girl (d) The Butterfly Effect
    Ans: (a) The Travels of Monarch X, a children's book about the migration of monarch butterflies.

M2. Ebright's teacher Richard A. Weiherer taught him to: (a) compete in order to do the best job he could (b) win at any cost (c) avoid competition (d) study only butterflies
    Ans: (a) compete in order to do the best job he could, not for the sake of winning.

M3. The twelve tiny gold spots on a monarch pupa: (a) are ornamental (b) attract mates (c) protect the pupa from heat (d) produce a hormone needed for the butterfly's development
    Ans: (d) they produce a hormone necessary for the full development of the butterfly.

M4. What did Ebright's first science-fair project mainly contain? (a) A working model of a satellite (b) A study of frog bones (c) A collection of rocks (d) A display of butterfly specimens
    Ans: (d) A display of butterfly specimens. The entry lacked the experimental question that he pursued in later work.

M5. What did Ebright learn from his early specimen display at the science fair? (a) Scientists should avoid butterflies (b) Prizes matter more than evidence (c) A project needs an investigation, not only a collection (d) Experiments are not useful
    Ans: (c) A project needs an investigation, not only a collection. His first entry did not ask or test a scientific question.

M6. Ebright's first serious scientific work was on: (a) ants and their nests  (b) frog anatomy  (c) monarch butterflies and a viral disease  (d) the stars
    Ans: (c) monarch butterflies and a viral disease. He had collected butterflies since he was in the second grade.

M7. Ebright and his friend discovered that the gold spots on a monarch pupa: (a) are only decoration  (b) produce a hormone needed for the butterfly's development  (c) warn away birds  (d) store food
    Ans: (b) produce a hormone needed for the butterfly's development. This was the finding that made his name.

M8. Richard Ebright grew up in: (a) New York City (b) Reading, Pennsylvania (c) London (d) Chicago
    Ans: (b) Reading, Pennsylvania. There his mother encouraged his collecting and gave him The Travels of Monarch X.
