A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] Is Amanda an orphan? Why does she say she is one?
    Ans: No, she is not an orphan. She imagines herself to be one because she is tired of the constant instructions and scolding. As an orphan roaming the street, patterning the dust with her bare feet, she would be free, silent and alone — free from anyone telling her what to do. The fantasy is an escape, not a statement of fact.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] Why does Amanda want to be Rapunzel?
    Ans: Because Rapunzel lives in a tranquil tower, far away from the world, and her life there is rare and peaceful. Amanda longs for that peace and solitude, away from the nagging. But she adds that she would never let down her bright hair, because she does not want anyone to find her — she wants the tower for its silence, not for rescue.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] What does the poem tell us about the relationship between Amanda and the adult?
    Ans: It shows a relationship of constant control and silent resistance. The adult gives instruction after instruction — about nails, posture, homework, shoes, chocolate and mood — and Amanda withdraws into her imagination each time. The adult interprets this silence as sulking and asks if she is moody, which shows that the adult has not understood that the child needs space to dream. The poem criticises a way of bringing up children that leaves no room for them.

Q4. [Application · 3 marks] A reader concludes that Amanda is literally an orphan because she imagines being one. Correct this conclusion and explain what the fantasy reveals.
    Ans:
      Amanda is not stated to be an orphan; this is one of her imagined escapes from repeated instructions.
      The fantasy of being alone lets her picture silence and freedom from constant correction.
      It shows her frustration, but the contrast also suggests that the adult's commands leave little room for her own thoughts.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A student notices that the adult gives instructions while Amanda imagines another world. Explain how this repeated contrast shapes the poem's view of their relationship.
    Ans:
      The adult's practical commands form one voice; Amanda's fantasies form a contrasting inner voice.
      Each fantasy offers an escape — for example, being a mermaid moving freely in the sea — and reveals how tired Amanda is of being directed.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] What does Amanda imagine herself to be, and why?
    Ans:
      She imagines herself as a mermaid drifting blissfully on the lapping waves of a languid emerald sea.
      She imagines herself as an orphan, roaming barefoot and free in the dust and gold of the street, with no one to scold her.
      She also dreams of being Rapunzel, living in a tower where no one can find her and life is ‘tranquil and rare, unmolested'.
      Each daydream is an escape from the constant instruction and nagging she hears at home, so the poem is really about a child's need for freedom.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Why does Amanda imagine being a mermaid ‘in the languid, emerald sea’? What is she escaping from?
    Ans: The mermaid drifts alone in a green, slow sea where ‘the only sound is silence’ — the opposite of her life of constant instructions: do not bite your nails, sit straight, stop sulking. She is escaping from adult control into a world where nobody corrects her and she can simply drift free. Each fantasy is a wish for silence and freedom.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    An adult tells Amanda to sit straight and stop a small habit. In the same poem, Amanda imagines moving freely as a mermaid in a quiet sea. The commands and the fantasy alternate.
    
    (a) What does the mermaid image offer Amanda?
    (b) Why does the poem place the fantasy beside the adult's instructions?
    (c) Does the fantasy prove that Amanda is an orphan?
    Ans:
      It offers a world of freedom, solitude and movement without someone constantly correcting her.
      The contrast lets readers hear Amanda's unspoken response and see the emotional effect of the repeated instructions.
      No. Being an orphan is part of her imagined escape, not a fact established about her family.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Amanda's mother warns that people may think she is sulking. Amanda's imagination turns toward a fairy-tale world where a girl can be alone and undisturbed. The reader hears both the adult's concern and Amanda's wish for space.
    
    (a) What does the mother's warning show?
    (b) What does Amanda's fantasy suggest she wants?
    (c) Why should a reader consider both voices?
    Ans:
      The mother is concerned about Amanda's behaviour and how it may be judged by others.
      Amanda wants quiet and independence, and she feels constrained by the stream of instructions.
      Hearing both voices makes the poem more balanced: the adult may intend guidance, but Amanda experiences the repeated corrections as pressure.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] How does the poem show the conflict between a child's freedom and adult instruction?
    Ans:
      Each stanza opens with a command — don't bite your nails, don't hunch your shoulders, don't eat chocolate, finish your homework, tidy your room.
      Between the commands Amanda drifts into silence and dream, which is her only defence.
      The adults believe they are shaping a good child; the child experiences only constant correction and the loss of her own thoughts.
      The poem suggests that endless instruction produces withdrawal rather than goodness, and ends with the warning that if she is sulked at all day she will stop loving herself.

Q11. [Long · 4 marks] The poem ends with Amanda being accused of sulking. Why does this ending prove that the adult has understood nothing?
    Ans:
      Amanda is silent not because she sulks but because she has withdrawn into daydreams — her only private space.
      The adult reads this silence as disobedience (‘you are sulking’) and adds one more instruction to the list.
      The ending thus completes the circle: nagging produces withdrawal, withdrawal is punished with more nagging. The adult hears the silence but never asks what it means.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] How does Robin Klein use the alternating voice of instruction and fantasy to show the distance between what Amanda hears and what she wants?
    Ans:
      The adult voice gives a series of everyday corrections, from posture to habits and behaviour. They may be meant as guidance, but their accumulation sounds like nagging.
      Amanda's inner voice answers not with an argument but with imaginative scenes: she is a mermaid, an orphan, or Rapunzel, each free from immediate demands.
      These fantasies reveal her desire for space and control over her own time; they do not establish that she is actually alone or unloved.
      By setting the two voices beside each other, the poem invites sympathy for Amanda while leaving room to recognise the adult's concern.

B. MCQs (8)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. 'Amanda!' is written by: (a) Robin Klein (b) Robert Frost (c) Leslie Norris (d) John Berryman
    Ans: (a) Robin Klein.

M2. Amanda imagines herself as all of the following except: (a) an orphan (b) a queen (c) Rapunzel (d) a mermaid
    Ans: (b) a queen — her three fantasies are the orphan, Rapunzel in her tower, and a mermaid in the languid emerald sea.

M3. As Rapunzel, Amanda would: (a) let down her bright hair (b) climb down the tower (c) call for help (d) never let down her bright hair
    Ans: (d) never let down her bright hair — she wants the tower for its silence, not for rescue.

M4. Which behaviour is Amanda specifically told to stop? (a) Reading a book (b) Biting her nails (c) Walking to school (d) Speaking to a friend
    Ans: (b) Biting her nails. The adult's instructions include correcting Amanda's habits and posture.

M5. What does the adult fear people will think Amanda is doing? (a) Sulking (b) Singing (c) Sleeping (d) Studying
    Ans: (a) Sulking. The warning reveals the adult's concern about how Amanda's behaviour will appear to others.

M6. Amanda is told not to: (a) read books  (b) go to school  (c) bite her nails and hunch her shoulders  (d) play outside
    Ans: (c) bite her nails and hunch her shoulders. The nagging runs through the whole poem.

M7. Amanda's daydreams are about being: (a) a queen and a teacher  (b) a bird  (c) a mermaid, Rapunzel and an orphan  (d) a soldier
    Ans: (c) a mermaid, Rapunzel and an orphan. Each dream is a form of escape from constant correction.

M8. In the poem, Amanda's own thoughts and replies are shown: (a) in bold letters (b) in capital letters (c) in footnotes (d) in brackets
    Ans: (d) in brackets. The brackets separate her inner daydreams from the adult's outer instructions.
