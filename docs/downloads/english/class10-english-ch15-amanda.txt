A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] Is Amanda an orphan? Why does she say she is one?
    Ans: No, she is not an orphan. She imagines herself to be one because she is tired of the constant instructions and scolding. As an orphan roaming the street, patterning the dust with her bare feet, she would be free, silent and alone — free from anyone telling her what to do. The fantasy is an escape, not a statement of fact.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] Why does Amanda want to be Rapunzel?
    Ans: Because Rapunzel lives in a tranquil tower, far away from the world, and her life there is rare and peaceful. Amanda longs for that peace and solitude, away from the nagging. But she adds that she would never let down her bright hair, because she does not want anyone to find her — she wants the tower for its silence, not for rescue.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] What does the poem tell us about the relationship between Amanda and the adult?
    Ans: It shows a relationship of constant control and silent resistance. The adult gives instruction after instruction — about nails, posture, homework, shoes, chocolate and mood — and Amanda withdraws into her imagination each time. The adult interprets this silence as sulking and asks if she is moody, which shows that the adult has not understood that the child needs space to dream. The poem criticises a way of bringing up children that leaves no room for them.

    Ans:

      Why the mistake costs marks: Answering that Amanda is an orphan. She only imagines herself to be one.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] What does Amanda imagine herself to be, and why?
    Ans:
      She imagines herself as a mermaid drifting blissfully on the lapping waves of a languid emerald sea.
      She imagines herself as an orphan, roaming barefoot and free in the dust and gold of the street, with no one to scold her.
      She also dreams of being Rapunzel, living in a tower where no one can find her and life is ‘tranquil and rare, unmolested'.
      Each daydream is an escape from the constant instruction and nagging she hears at home, so the poem is really about a child's need for freedom.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Why does Amanda imagine being a mermaid ‘in the languid, emerald sea’? What is she escaping from?
    Ans: The mermaid drifts alone in a green, slow sea where ‘the only sound is silence’ — the opposite of her life of constant instructions: do not bite your nails, sit straight, stop sulking. She is escaping from adult control into a world where nobody corrects her and she can simply drift free. Each fantasy is a wish for silence and freedom.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] How does the poem show the conflict between a child's freedom and adult instruction?
    Ans:
      Each stanza opens with a command — don't bite your nails, don't hunch your shoulders, don't eat chocolate, finish your homework, tidy your room.
      Between the commands Amanda drifts into silence and dream, which is her only defence.
      The adults believe they are shaping a good child; the child experiences only constant correction and the loss of her own thoughts.
      The poem suggests that endless instruction produces withdrawal rather than goodness, and ends with the warning that if she is sulked at all day she will stop loving herself.

Q11. [Long · 4 marks] The poem ends with Amanda being accused of sulking. Why does this ending prove that the adult has understood nothing?
    Ans:
      Amanda is silent not because she sulks but because she has withdrawn into daydreams — her only private space.
      The adult reads this silence as disobedience (‘you are sulking’) and adds one more instruction to the list.
      The ending thus completes the circle: nagging produces withdrawal, withdrawal is punished with more nagging. The adult hears the silence but never asks what it means.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Discuss “Amanda!” in detail. Include the idea that carries marks, one worked example or answer frame, and why students lose marks.
    Ans:
      The poem. The poem alternates between the instructions of an adult — presumably Amanda's mother — and Amanda's daydreams. The adult tells her not to bite her nails, not to hunch her shoulders, not to slouch, to sit up straight, to do her homework, to clean her room, to shine her shoes, not to eat chocolate because of her acne, and not to look so moody. Amanda answers each instruction with a fantasy: she is an orphan.
      Marks lens: Reference-to-context extracts; a 3-mark answer on whether Amanda is an orphan or a mermaid, and on the theme of control.
      Method / memory trick: Three fantasies in order: orphan, Rapunzel, mermaid. Each is her escape from one kind of instruction.
      Do not: Answering that Amanda is an orphan. She only imagines herself to be one.

B. MCQs (8)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. 'Amanda!' is written by: (a) Robin Klein (b) Robert Frost (c) Leslie Norris (d) John Berryman
    Ans: (a) Robin Klein.

M2. Amanda imagines herself as all of the following except: (a) an orphan (b) Rapunzel (c) a mermaid (d) a queen
    Ans: (d) a queen — her three fantasies are the orphan, Rapunzel in her tower, and a mermaid in the languid emerald sea.

M3. As Rapunzel, Amanda would: (a) let down her bright hair (b) never let down her bright hair (c) climb down the tower (d) call for help
    Ans: (b) never let down her bright hair — she wants the tower for its silence, not for rescue.

M4. The most exam-ready way to revise “Amanda!” is: (a) read the heading only  (b) learn the key idea plus one example  (c) skip written practice  (d) memorise a random extra topic
    Ans: (b) learn the key idea plus one example. The chapter is Robin Klein: a girl's daydreams against her mother's nagging.

M5. A 3-mark answer on “Amanda!” should: (a) be one sentence  (b) start with the idea, then a reason or method  (c) copy the whole chapter  (d) leave the example out
    Ans: (b) start with the idea, then a reason or method. Step marks follow that frame.

M6. Amanda is told not to: (a) bite her nails and hunch her shoulders  (b) read books  (c) go to school  (d) play outside
    Ans: (a) bite her nails and hunch her shoulders. The nagging runs through the whole poem.

M7. Amanda's daydreams are about being: (a) a mermaid, Rapunzel and an orphan  (b) a queen and a teacher  (c) a bird  (d) a soldier
    Ans: (a) a mermaid, Rapunzel and an orphan. Each dream is a form of escape from constant correction.

M8. In the poem, Amanda's own thoughts and replies are shown: (a) in brackets (b) in bold letters (c) in capital letters (d) in footnotes
    Ans: (a) in brackets. The brackets separate her inner daydreams from the adult's outer instructions.
