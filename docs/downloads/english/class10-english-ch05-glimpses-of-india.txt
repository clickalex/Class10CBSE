Q1. [Short · 3 marks] Why is the baker called a friend, companion and guide in Goa?
    Ans: Because he was woven into the daily life of the village. His arrival, announced by the jangling of his bamboo staff, was a fixed event twice a day, and children ran to meet him to choose their bread bangles. He was present at every celebration — bol for marriages, bolinhas and coconut cookies for Christmas, sandwiches for engagements — so his presence marked the family's important occasions.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What do you know about the people of Coorg?
    Ans: They are a proud race of martial men, beautiful women and wild creatures. They are perhaps the most hospitable people in India and are the only people in India permitted to carry firearms without a licence. One theory says they are descended from a part of Alexander's army that settled there and married among the locals; another says they are of Arab origin, because the Kuppia, the long black coat with an embroidered waist-belt worn by the Kodavus, resembles the kuffia worn by Arabs and Kurds.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] How does the legend of tea connect China and India?
    Ans: According to a Chinese legend, tea was first drunk in China around 2700 BC, when a few leaves from a twig burning under a vessel of boiling water fell into it and gave a delicious drink. An Indian legend says that Bodhidharma, an ancient Buddhist ascetic, cut off his eyelids because he felt sleepy during meditation, and ten tea plants grew out of the eyelids; the leaves of these plants, when put in hot water and drunk, banished sleep. Tea came to Europe only in the sixteenth century, and was drunk more as medicine than as a beverage.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] What is a ‘pader' and how does Goa's Portuguese inheritance survive in its bread?
    Ans:
      A pader is the traditional Goan baker; the descendants of these bakers still run the bread business in Goa.
      The Portuguese introduced the leavened bread called pao, which is still the staple of every Goan meal alongside rice, fish, curry and vegetables.
      The bread is baked in the old-fashioned ‘pod' or mud ovens, and the baker's bamboo staff thumping on the floor is still heard at dawn.
      Marriage gifts called ‘bol' and the sweet bread of special occasions keep the tradition alive.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] The chapter covers Goa, Coorg and Assam. What does each place contribute to the ‘glimpse of India’?
    Ans:
      Goa contributes living history — the Portuguese pader, the kabai and the bol show how a colonial past survives in daily bread.
      Coorg contributes landscape and valour — coffee-scented hills, the martial Kodavus and the Kaveri's origin.
      Assam contributes a global story — tea drunk eighty crore cups a day, linking Chinese legend and Indian gardens. Together they show India's variety.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] Describe Rajvir's journey to Assam and what he saw in the tea gardens.
    Ans:
      Rajvir travelled by train to Dhekiabari with his friend Pranjol, whose father managed a tea estate.
      As the train neared the tea country, Rajvir looked out at endless stretches of orderly tea bushes and doll-like women plucking leaves.
      Pranjol, who had grown up on a plantation, slept through it, but Rajvir could not sleep with excitement.
      He impressed Pranjol with his knowledge: the second flush or sprouting period gives the best tea, and he told the legend of the Chinese emperor who discovered tea when leaves fell into his boiling water.

Q11. [Long · 5 marks] Describe the Coorg landscape and its attractions.
    Ans:
      Coorg, or Kodagu, is the smallest district of Karnataka, lying midway between Mysore and the coastal town of Mangalore. It is a rolling hillside with a permanent fragrance of fresh coffee, coffee estates and spice plantations, colonial bungalows tucked under treetops, and dense forests.
      The best season is from September to March, when the weather is perfect, the air breathes of invigorating coffee, and the rain showers keep the hills fresh.
      Wildlife: the Kaveri river, which takes its birth from the hills and forests of Coorg, is home to Mahaseer, a large freshwater fish; kingfishers, squirrels and langurs can be seen, and wild elephants bathe in the river.
      Attractions: the climb to the Brahmagiri hills gives a panoramic view of the entire misty landscape of Coorg; a walk into the sixty-acre Buddhist monastery at Bylakutte, hidden among the coffee plantations, is a surprise; and there are rappelling, river rafting, canoeing and rock climbing for the adventurous.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Discuss “Glimpses of India” in detail. Include the idea that carries marks, one worked example or answer frame, and why students lose marks.
    Ans:

B. MCQs (8)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. The people of Coorg wear a long black coat with an embroidered waist-belt called: (a) kabai (b) Kuppia (c) kuffia (d) bol
    Ans: (b) Kuppia — its resemblance to the kuffia of the Arabs and Kurds supports the Arab-origin theory.

M2. According to the chapter, how many cups of tea are drunk every day throughout the world? (a) eight crore (b) eighty crore (c) eight hundred crore (d) eight lakh
    Ans: (b) eighty crore cups a day.

M3. In Goa, the sweet bread given as a marriage gift is called: (a) pao (b) bol (c) bolinhas (d) kabai
    Ans: (b) bol — bolinhas are the coconut cookies served at Christmas.

M4. The most exam-ready way to revise “Glimpses of India” is: (a) read the heading only  (b) learn the key idea plus one example  (c) skip written practice  (d) memorise a random extra topic
    Ans: (b) learn the key idea plus one example. The chapter is Goa's bakers, Assam's tea country and Coorg's people.

M5. A 3-mark answer on “Glimpses of India” should: (a) be one sentence  (b) start with the idea, then a reason or method  (c) copy the whole chapter  (d) leave the example out
    Ans: (b) start with the idea, then a reason or method. Step marks follow that frame.

M6. The traditional bakers of Goa are called: (a) paders  (b) pao wallahs  (c) bakers  (d) lohars
    Ans: (a) paders. Their ovens are called ‘pods' and the marriage gift bread is called ‘bol'.

M7. Coorg is famous for: (a) coffee plantations and oranges  (b) tea only  (c) spices only  (d) wheat
    Ans: (a) coffee plantations and oranges. The Coorgis are also known for their hospitality and their Kodava martial traditions.

M8. The finest tea is picked during: (a) the first flush (b) the second flush, from May to July (c) winter (d) the monsoon
    Ans: (b) the second flush, from May to July. This is when the best Assam tea comes to the market.
