A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] What does the poet mean by 'honey-coloured ramparts at your ear'?
    Ans: He is comparing Anne's yellow hair to the ramparts, or high walls, of a fort. The hair hangs around her ears like a wall that young men cannot cross, and it throws them into despair because they cannot help being attracted to her beauty. It is a metaphor, since no 'like' or 'as' is used.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What is Anne Gregory's reply, and does it answer the poet's point?
    Ans: Anne replies that she can dye her hair another colour — brown, or black, or carrot — so that young men could love her for herself alone and not for her yellow hair. She believes that by removing the attraction of her hair she would prove the poet wrong. But the poet answers that according to a religious text only God can love a person for themselves alone, so her plan would not change the fact that human love is always mixed with physical attraction.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] What is the central idea of the poem?
    Ans: The central idea is that physical beauty draws people, and that it is almost impossible for a human being to love another for their inner self alone. Anne thinks that by dyeing her hair she could prove otherwise, but the poet quotes a religious text to show that only God is capable of such selfless love. The poem sets outer beauty against inner worth and suggests that true, disinterested love is divine rather than human.

Q4. [Application · 3 marks] A reader says the “honey-coloured ramparts” are actual walls around Anne's home. Correct the reading and explain what the image says about the young men's attention.
    Ans:
      The ramparts are a metaphor for Anne's yellow hair, which frames or surrounds her face.
      The image shows that the young men are strongly drawn to her visible beauty.
      The poem questions whether such attraction lets them see Anne herself, apart from her appearance.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] Anne offers to dye her hair different colours. What is she trying to prove, and why does the speaker's final response leave the question unsettled for human relationships?
    Ans:
      Anne believes that if her hair colour changes, a young man may love her for herself rather than for her appearance.
      The speaker cites an old religious saying that only God can love a person entirely for themselves.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] What does the poet say about young men's love? Give the reason he gives.
    Ans:
      The poet tells Anne Gregory that young men who are in love with her will never love her for herself alone, but only for her yellow hair.
      He says ‘never shall a young man, Thrown into despair By those great honey-coloured Ramparts at your ear, Love you for yourself alone And not your yellow hair.'
      This is because youth is taken by physical beauty and cannot see the person behind it.
      The poem therefore makes a distinction between being loved for one's looks and being loved for oneself.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Why does Anne Gregory offer to dye her hair ‘brown, or black, or carrot’? What is she trying to prove?
    Ans: Anne offers to change her honey-coloured hair to prove that young men could love her for herself alone, not for her yellow hair. By removing the beauty the poet blames, she hopes to remove his argument. The poet's reply is that even this would not settle it — only God, as the old religious text says, can love a person for himself alone.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:

    The speaker addresses Anne Gregory's yellow hair as “honey-coloured ramparts”. Anne replies that she could dye it brown, black or carrot-coloured. The speaker then quotes an old religious man.

    (a) What does the rampart image describe?
    (b) What does Anne hope a change of hair colour will show?
    (c) What claim does the religious quotation add?
    Ans:
      The image describes Anne's yellow hair, using a metaphor of protective or enclosing walls.
      She hopes that a young man could then love her for her own self rather than being attracted only by her hair.
      The quotation says that loving someone entirely for themselves is beyond ordinary human love; the old religious man says only God can do it.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:

    Anne thinks she can alter her appearance by changing her hair colour. The speaker responds by recalling a religious saying about love for the self alone. The exchange moves from a visible feature to a question about inner worth.

    (a) What is Anne's practical proposal?
    (b) Why does the speaker bring in the old saying?
    (c) How does the exchange develop the theme of outer and inner beauty?
    Ans:
      Anne proposes dyeing her hair a different colour so that a young man might not be attracted by its yellow colour.
      He uses the saying to challenge her confidence that changing one feature can prove the nature of another person's love.
      The poem distinguishes visible beauty from a person's inner self, while leaving open whether human love can ever ignore outward appearance completely.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] How does the poem contrast outer beauty with inner worth?
    Ans:
      Anne Gregory protests that she can dye her hair — brown, black or carrot — so that young men may love her for herself alone.
      The poet replies that a religious man who has spent his nights praying has found ‘texts' proving that only God can love her for herself alone and not for her yellow hair.
      The contrast is between human love, which depends on appearance and is therefore changeable, and divine love, which sees the person.
      The poem is not cynical so much as realistic: youth values the face and hair; the worth of a person is known only to love that does not depend on appearance.

Q11. [Application · 4 marks] The poet quotes an old religious text instead of answering Anne himself. Why does he bring in this authority?
    Ans:
      Anne's offer — dyeing her hair — is practical and testable, and the poet cannot disprove it from experience.
      So he shifts the ground from experience to faith: ‘he found a text to prove that only God, my dear, could love you for yourself alone’.
      The authority lets him keep his point without sounding cruel — it is not his opinion but heaven's. At the same time it admits his claim cannot be proved, only believed.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] How does Yeats use dialogue and the hair image to explore whether a person can be loved for inner qualities alone?
    Ans:
      The speaker begins by suggesting that a young man may be captivated by Anne's yellow hair and may not love her “for yourself alone”.
      Anne answers confidently that she could dye her hair brown, black or carrot-coloured, and hopes this would make her inner self the object of love.
      The speaker then brings in an old religious saying that only God can love someone entirely for themselves, complicating Anne's proposed test.
      The dialogue turns a striking image of outward beauty into a question about how far human love can see beyond appearance; the poem offers a tension, not a simple experiment with a guaranteed result.

B. MCQs (8)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. 'For Anne Gregory' is written by: (a) Ogden Nash (b) Carl Sandburg (c) W. B. Yeats (d) Robert Frost
    Ans: (c) W. B. Yeats.

M2. 'Honey-coloured ramparts' refers to: (a) the walls of a castle (b) the setting sun (c) Anne's yellow hair (d) a religious text
    Ans: (c) Anne's yellow hair, compared by metaphor to the ramparts of a fort.

M3. According to the old religious man, who can love a person for themselves alone? (a) a true friend (b) a mother (c) a husband (d) only God
    Ans: (d) only God — the poet quotes a text to prove it.

M4. What change does Anne suggest making to her appearance? (a) Cutting it very short (b) Dyeing her hair (c) Covering it with a hat (d) Wearing a crown
    Ans: (b) Dyeing her hair. She imagines trying other colours to test what young men love.

M5. Who is quoted in the poem's final response about loving a person for themselves alone? (a) An old religious man (b) Anne's father (c) A young painter (d) A school teacher
    Ans: (a) An old religious man. The speaker cites his saying that only God can love someone wholly for themselves.

M6. According to the poet, young men are thrown into despair by: (a) Anne's honey-coloured hair  (b) her wealth  (c) her silence  (d) her voice
    Ans: (a) Anne's honey-coloured hair. The ‘great honey-coloured ramparts' at her ear are her hair.

M7. The poet says that only God can love a person: (a) for his wealth  (b) for himself alone and not for his yellow hair  (c) for his beauty  (d) for his fame
    Ans: (b) for himself alone and not for his yellow hair. Human love is entangled with physical appearance.

M8. The poem is written in the form of: (a) a letter (b) a diary entry (c) a ballad (d) a dialogue
    Ans: (d) a dialogue. The poet speaks, Anne Gregory answers, and the poet replies again.
