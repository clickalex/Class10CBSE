A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] Why was Matilda unhappy with her life?
    Ans: Because she believed she was born for all the delicacies and luxuries of life, and her actual life was poor and plain. She suffered from the poverty of her home, from the shabby walls, the worn chairs and the ugly curtains, and she dreamed of silent antechambers, tall bronze candelabra and expensive dinners. Her dissatisfaction came from comparing herself with the rich, so she could not enjoy what she had.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What was the effect of losing the necklace on the Loisels' lives?
    Ans: They had to replace it, and a similar necklace cost forty thousand francs. Her husband used the eighteen thousand francs his father had left him and borrowed the rest at ruinous interest. For ten years they lived in poverty: they dismissed their maid, moved to a garret, and Matilda did all the heavy housework while her husband worked evenings and did copying at twopence-halfpenny a page. Matilda grew old, rough and hard-looking, and they lost their youth and their comfort to pay a debt they need never have had.

Q3. [Application · 3 marks] Matilda borrows jewellery to appear wealthy for one evening. Explain how her desire to impress others helps bring about the long hardship that follows.
    Ans:
      Matilda feels dissatisfied with her ordinary life and believes that luxury and admiration are what she deserves.
      She borrows Madame Forestier's necklace to complete the appearance she wants at the reception, then loses it.
      The replacement costs far more than she can afford, and the Loisels spend ten years repaying the debt; the wish to seem rich transforms their lives.

Q4. [Application · 3 marks] The Loisels work for years to replace the necklace without telling Madame Forestier the truth. What more honest choice could they have made, and what might it have changed?
    Ans:
      They could have told Madame Forestier promptly that the necklace was lost and asked her what it was worth or how to replace it.
      That conversation might have revealed that the original was imitation jewellery, avoiding the enormous loan and years of hardship.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] What was the price of the lost necklace and how did the Loisels pay for it?
    Ans:
      They borrowed 18,000 francs from usurers to buy a replacement necklace that cost 36,000 francs.
      For ten years they dismissed their maid, moved to a garret, and Matilda did all the housework while her husband worked evenings and night.
      He copied manuscripts at five sous a page and often worked late into the night; at the end of ten years they had paid back everything.
      The price was not only money: Matilda lost her youth, her beauty and her comfortable life, and became a hard, coarse woman.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] Why did Matilda borrow a necklace instead of wearing flowers, as her husband suggested? What does this choice reveal?
    Ans: Her husband suggested natural flowers, which would have been pretty and honest — but Matilda wanted to look rich, not pretty. She borrowed the diamond necklace from Mme Forestier because her dream was to be envied by the other women at the ball. The choice reveals her pride: she would rather borrow glamour than admit her real position.

Q7. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:

    Matilda attends the reception wearing a borrowed necklace and enjoys being admired. On returning home, she discovers that it is missing. The Loisels buy a replacement and spend ten years paying off the debt, only to learn that the original necklace was paste.

    (a) Why did Matilda borrow the necklace?
    (b) What decision creates the years of hardship?
    (c) What makes the ending ironic?
    Ans:
      She wants to look prosperous and elegant at the reception and believes the jewels will complete her appearance.
      The Loisels replace the necklace without first checking its value with Madame Forestier, and the replacement costs 40,000 francs.
      They sacrifice ten years to repay the price of a real-looking replacement, then learn that the original was imitation jewellery worth very little.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:

    After losing the borrowed necklace, the Loisels choose not to explain what happened to Madame Forestier. They buy a costly replacement and accept years of work and debt. When Matilda later meets her friend, the truth changes how she understands the sacrifice.

    (a) What does the Loisels' silence cause?
    (b) What does Matilda learn from Madame Forestier?
    (c) What is the story's warning about appearances?
    Ans:
      Their silence prevents them from discovering the original necklace's true value and leads them to take on an unnecessary debt.
      Madame Forestier reveals that the original necklace was paste, not genuine diamonds.
      A valuable appearance may be deceptive; the Loisels' belief about the jewels changes their lives more than the actual object did.

Q9. [Long · 4 marks] Explain the twist at the end of ‘The Necklace' and what it says about pride.
    Ans:
      When they meet again, Mme Forestier does not at first recognise Matilda, who looks old and worn.
      Matilda tells her the truth, and Mme Forestier reveals that the original necklace was paste, worth only 500 francs.
      So ten years of sacrifice were spent replacing an imitation, while the borrowed glitter of one evening cost them everything.
      Maupassant's point is that vanity and the desire to appear rich punished them far more than poverty itself did.

Q10. [Application · 4 marks] Matilda's husband works evenings, borrows at ruinous rates and never complains. What does the story gain by making him, not Matilda, the silent sufferer?
    Ans:
      His silent suffering measures the true cost of Matilda's vanity — the debt falls on the one person who never wanted luxury.
      It also keeps the reader's sympathy balanced: Matilda causes the disaster, but her husband's loyalty makes the ten years of poverty moving rather than merely deserved.
      Without him the story would be a fable about pride; with him it becomes a tragedy about love paying for pride.

Q11. [Long · 5 marks] Was Matilda's suffering deserved? Give reasons for your answer.
    Ans:
      The case that it was deserved: her suffering came from her own vanity and discontent. She had a kind husband who loved her and a comfortable enough life, but she could not enjoy either because she compared herself with the rich. She was embarrassed to borrow a dress, insisted on jewels for the ball, and was so anxious to escape quickly that she did not check the necklace. Her vanity caused the loss.
      The case that it was not deserved: she never stole anything, and she behaved honourably afterwards. Rather than admit the loss to her friend, she and her husband replaced the necklace honestly and spent ten years repaying a ruinous debt. She did the heavy housework and gave up her looks and her youth. A mistake of vanity, however foolish, does not deserve ten years of punishment.
      Conclusion: the punishment was far greater than the fault, and that disproportion is what makes the ending so bitter. The real tragedy is that the necklace was only paste, so the ten years of suffering were wasted entirely.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] How does Maupassant use Matilda's desire for status and the final revelation to make “The Necklace” a story about the cost of appearances?
    Ans:
      Matilda is unhappy because she imagines herself suited to luxury and feels ashamed of her modest home and clothes.
      At the reception, the borrowed necklace helps her appear wealthy, but after it goes missing the Loisels hide the truth and buy an expensive replacement.
      They spend ten years working and sacrificing to repay 40,000 francs, believing they are replacing valuable diamonds.
      Madame Forestier's final revelation that the original was paste makes the sacrifice tragic and ironic: appearances deceive Matilda, but also the couple's fear and silence deepen the cost.

B. MCQs (8)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. 'The Necklace' is written by: (a) Anton Chekhov (b) Ruskin Bond (c) H. G. Wells (d) Guy de Maupassant
    Ans: (d) Guy de Maupassant, the French short-story writer.

M2. The necklace the Loisels bought to replace the lost one cost: (a) 18,000 francs (b) 36,000 francs (c) 40,000 francs (d) 500 francs
    Ans: (c) forty thousand francs — eighteen thousand inherited and the rest borrowed.

M3. The original necklace belonging to Madame Forestier was: (a) a real diamond necklace worth 36,000 francs (b) stolen (c) never found (d) paste, worth at most 500 francs
    Ans: (d) paste, worth at most five hundred francs — the irony revealed in the last line.

M4. Why does Matilda borrow jewellery from Madame Forestier? (a) She wants to appear elegant at the reception (b) She plans to sell it at a market (c) Her husband asks her to repair it (d) She wants to give it to her sister
    Ans: (a) She wants to appear elegant at the reception. Matilda believes the jewellery will complete the prosperous appearance she wants.

M5. Which ministry sends the invitation to the reception? (a) The Ministry of War (b) The Ministry of Public Instruction (c) The Ministry of Trade (d) The Ministry of Agriculture
    Ans: (b) The Ministry of Public Instruction. M. Loisel brings home an invitation from the ministry where he works.

M6. Matilda borrowed the necklace from: (a) Mme Forestier  (b) her sister  (c) her mother  (d) a shop
    Ans: (a) Mme Forestier. Her rich friend lent her what Matilda believed were real diamonds.

M7. The Loisels spent how many years repaying the debt? (a) five  (b) twenty  (c) ten  (d) two
    Ans: (c) ten. At the end of ten years Mme Loisel looked old and worn out.

M8. The dress Matilda's husband gave her money for cost: (a) 4,000 francs (b) 400 francs (c) 40 francs (d) 40,000 francs
    Ans: (b) 400 francs. He had been saving it for a gun — and gave it up for her ball dress.
