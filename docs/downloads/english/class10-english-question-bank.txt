CHAPTER 1 · A Letter to God
Unit: First Flight · Prose
========================================================================

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] Why did Lencho say the post office employees were a bunch of crooks?
    Ans: Because he had asked God for one hundred pesos and received only seventy. His faith was so absolute that he could not believe God had made a mistake or had sent less, so he concluded that the post office employees must have taken the remaining thirty pesos. The irony is that these were the very men who had collected the money for him out of charity.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What made the postmaster decide to help Lencho?
    Ans: He was moved by the farmer's unshakable faith in God. He did not want to shake that faith, so he collected money from his employees and friends, gave part of his own salary, and sent it to Lencho in a letter signed 'God'.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] Was Lencho surprised to find a letter with money in it? What made him angry?
    Ans: No, he was not surprised at all — he had expected an answer from God, and his confidence in God was so complete that receiving a reply seemed natural. What made him angry was that the envelope contained only seventy pesos instead of the hundred he had asked for. He was certain that God could not have made a mistake, so he concluded that the post office employees had taken the rest.

Q4. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes: “Lencho received one hundred pesos from God and thanked Him.” Correct this using the text.
    Ans:
      Lencho asked for a hundred pesos but received only seventy; the money had been collected by the postmaster from his employees and friends, plus part of his own salary.
      He was not surprised to get a reply, since he was sure God would answer, but he became angry when he counted the money.
      He then wrote a second letter asking God to send the rest, and not by post, because the post-office employees were “a bunch of crooks”.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] Lencho never thanks the postmaster or his helpers. Is he being fair to them? Give two reasons.
    Ans:
      No. The postmaster and his employees gave their own money to help a stranger, and Lencho accuses them of stealing the missing thirty pesos.
      His faith in God is so complete that he cannot imagine ordinary people being generous, so he credits God alone and suspects men.
      His mistake comes from innocence, not cruelty; this is what makes the ending ironic.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] Bring out the irony in the ending of ‘A Letter to God'.
    Ans: Lencho is certain that God sent him the money and cannot imagine that human beings could be so generous. He writes a second letter to complain and calls the post office employees ‘a bunch of crooks' because thirty pesos were missing. The irony is that the very people who helped him are the ones he distrusts: his faith in God survives, while his faith in human beings is destroyed.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] “The postmaster was a fat, jolly fellow.” How does his character contrast with Lencho's, and why does the contrast matter to the story?
    Ans:
      Lencho is intense, single-minded and absolute in his faith; the postmaster is warm, practical and humane — he helps without needing to be believed in.
      The contrast creates the story's deepest irony: the believer cannot recognise human kindness, while the kind man asks for no recognition at all.
      Without this contrast the ending would be merely sad; with it, it becomes a comment on faith that blinds as well as sustains.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    After a gentle rain, large hailstones began to fall on Lencho’s valley. He said they looked like new coins. Soon the field was white, as if covered with salt, and he said a plague of locusts would have left more than the hail had.
    
    (a) What did the hailstones first look like to Lencho?
    (b) What do the white field and the locusts tell us about the damage?
    (c) Where did Lencho turn for hope, and why?
    Ans:
      (a) They looked like new silver coins, which at first seemed like a blessing.
      (b) The crop was completely destroyed, even worse than a plague of locusts would have left it, so the family faced hunger for the year.
      (c) To God, because his faith was unshakable and he believed that no one else could help.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    The postmaster read Lencho’s letter to God.
    
    (a) How did he react at first?
    (b) What changed his attitude?
    (c) What did he do to help, and how did he hide it?
    Ans:
      (a) He laughed.
      (b) He became serious when he realised how deep the writer’s faith was; he did not want to shake it.
      (c) He collected money from his employees and friends and gave part of his own salary, and sent it in an envelope with a letter signed “God”.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] Describe Lencho's character and say what his faith tells us about him.
    Ans:
      Lencho is a poor but hard-working farmer who knows his field like a child and never stops hoping for a good crop.
      His faith is complete and childlike: after the hailstorm destroys everything, he writes to God for a hundred pesos with the same confidence with which he sows his field.
      The same faith makes him naive — he cannot imagine that the postmaster and his staff collected the money themselves.
      The story uses this innocence to show both the strength of faith and the loneliness of the poor, who trust God more than the people around them.

Q11. [Long · 5 marks] 'The story is built around two ironies.' Explain with reference to the text.
    Ans:
      The first irony is in the weather: Lencho's field needed rain, and rain did come — but it turned into a hailstorm that destroyed the entire crop. The very thing that promised a good harvest took it away.
      The second irony is in the ending: the post office employees, moved by Lencho's faith, collected seventy pesos and sent them to him. Lencho, finding the amount short, wrote back accusing those same men of stealing the money and calling them 'a bunch of crooks'. The helpers become the accused thieves, and the man who never doubted God doubts the honesty of human beings.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] What does “A Letter to God” suggest about faith and human kindness? Discuss with reference to Lencho and the postmaster.
    Ans:
      Lencho’s faith is complete: after the hail he writes to God as confidently as he sowed his field.
      The postmaster represents human goodness: he acts out of kindness and asks for no thanks.
      The irony is that Lencho keeps his faith in God but loses his faith in people, accusing the helpers of cheating him.
      The story shows that faith is admirable, yet it can blind a person to the real kindness around him.
      It also suggests that God’s help can reach us through human hands.

B. MCQs (8)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. Lencho asked God for: (a) fifty pesos (b) seventy pesos (c) one hundred pesos (d) one hundred and fifty pesos
    Ans: (c) one hundred pesos, to sow his field again and to live until the coming harvest.

M2. The letter Lencho received was signed: (a) the postmaster (b) God (c) the employees of the post office (d) the President
    Ans: (b) God — the postmaster signed it that way so that Lencho's faith would not be shaken.

M3. The writer of the story 'A Letter to God' is: (a) G. L. Fuentes (b) Nelson Mandela (c) Liam O'Flaherty (d) Betty Renshaw
    Ans: (a) G. L. Fuentes.

M4. When the postmaster first read Lencho’s letter, he: (a) tore it up (b) gave it to the police (c) ignored it (d) laughed, and then became serious
    Ans: (d) laughed, and then became serious. He laughed at the idea of a letter to God, but Lencho’s faith moved him and he decided to answer it.

M5. In his second letter, Lencho described the post-office employees as: (a) kind-hearted men (b) a bunch of crooks (c) God’s messengers (d) his relatives
    Ans: (b) a bunch of crooks. He believed they had kept the missing thirty pesos, though they had actually collected the seventy.

M6. Lencho asked God for: (a) 30 pesos  (b) 70 pesos  (c) 100 pesos  (d) 1000 pesos
    Ans: (c) 100 pesos. He asked for a hundred pesos to sow his field again and live until the harvest.

M7. The postmaster and his staff managed to collect: (a) 100 pesos  (b) 70 pesos  (c) 30 pesos  (d) 700 pesos
    Ans: (b) 70 pesos. They contributed from their own salaries, and the postmaster put in the rest of his own money.

M8. The postmaster collected the seventy pesos by: (a) asking the government (b) giving part of his salary and asking employees and friends (c) borrowing from a bank (d) selling his watch
    Ans: (b) giving part of his salary and asking employees and friends. The money was charity collected peso by peso.

CHAPTER 2 · Nelson Mandela: Long Walk to Freedom
Unit: First Flight · Prose
========================================================================

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] What does courage mean to Mandela?
    Ans: For Mandela, courage is not the absence of fear but the triumph over it. The brave man is not the one who does not feel afraid, but the one who conquers that fear. He says he felt fear more times than he can remember, and it was this repeated conquest of fear that made him bold.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What 'twin obligations' does Mandela mention?
    Ans: The first obligation is to his family — his parents, his wife and children. The second is to his people, his community and his country. In a civil and humane society each person can fulfil both, but in South Africa under apartheid, a man like Mandela who tried to live as a human being was punished and isolated, and could not discharge either obligation.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] Why did such a large number of international leaders attend the inauguration? What did it signify?
    Ans: It signified the recognition by the international community of the birth of a free, democratic, non-racial South Africa, and a common victory for justice, for peace and for human dignity. The presence of leaders of many nations showed that the end of apartheid was not only a South African event but a triumph of values shared by the whole world.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] Why does Mandela say that the oppressor is also not free?
    Ans: Because the oppressor is a prisoner of hatred, locked behind the bars of prejudice and narrow-mindedness. A man who takes away another man's freedom is himself deprived of his own humanity, so the oppressor and the oppressed are both robbed of what makes them human.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes that Mandela’s inauguration took place on 10 May 1995 and that apartheid simply meant keeping races apart. Correct both statements.
    Ans:
      The inauguration took place on 10 May 1994, in the amphitheatre formed by the Union Buildings in Pretoria.
      Apartheid was a system of racial discrimination: laws separated people by race and denied the black majority their freedom and rights.
      Under it, a man who tried to live as a free human being could be punished and isolated.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] Mandela says, “Courage was not the absence of fear, but the triumph over it.” Give an example from school life that fits this idea and explain why.
    Ans:
      Courage means acting rightly in spite of feeling afraid.
      Example: a student who is terrified of public speaking, yet takes part in a debate. She is brave because she feels the fear and still goes ahead.
      In the same way, Mandela felt fear many times but conquered it, and that is what made him brave.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] What does Mandela mean when he says that freedom is indivisible?
    Ans: He means that no one is truly free while others are unfree: the oppressor is as much a prisoner of hatred as the oppressed is of chains. His own hunger for freedom for his people was what made him lose the freedom to live with his family, and this cannot be separated from the freedom of every South African, white or black.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    In his speech Mandela said, “Never, never and never again shall it be that this beautiful land will again experience the oppression of one by another.”
    
    (a) On what occasion did he say this?
    (b) What is the effect of repeating “never”?
    (c) What does “the oppression of one by another” refer to?
    Ans:
      (a) At his inauguration as the first black President of South Africa, on 10 May 1994.
      (b) The repetition adds force and shows a firm, solemn promise that the past will not return.
      (c) The system of apartheid, in which one racial group dominated and oppressed the others.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Mandela remembers his boyhood as a time when he was “born free”: running in the fields near his mother’s hut, swimming in a clear stream and roasting mealies under the stars. Later he calls this freedom an illusion.
    
    (a) Name two of the freedoms of his boyhood.
    (b) Why does he call them an illusion?
    (c) What did he begin to hunger for as a young man?
    Ans:
      (a) Any two: running in the fields, swimming in the stream, roasting mealies, riding the backs of bulls.
      (b) He found that, as a black South African, his basic freedom had already been taken away by the laws of apartheid.
      (c) The freedom of his people to live with dignity and to achieve their potential.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] Describe the inauguration of 10 May 1994 and the obligations Mandela spoke of.
    Ans:
      The ceremony took place in the amphitheatre of the Union Buildings in Pretoria, watched by leaders and citizens of many countries and by a crowd of South Africans.
      Mandela was sworn in as the first black President of a democratic South Africa, with Thabo Mbeki and de Klerk as his deputies, and the old and new national anthems were sung.
      He spoke of the courage of the people, of the end of white rule and of the birth of a society in which all would be equal.
      He said the obligation of the new government was to free the people from poverty, deprivation, suffering, gender and other discrimination, so that the dignity of every person is respected.

Q11. [Long · 5 marks] How did Mandela's understanding of freedom change with age and experience?
    Ans:
      As a boy in the villages of Umtata, freedom meant being able to run in the fields near his mother's hut, to swim in the stream, to roast mealies under the stars and to ride the broad backs of slow-moving bulls. It was the freedom of a child, and it seemed boundless.
      As a student he wanted the transitory freedoms of staying out at night, reading what he pleased and going where he liked.
      As a young man he yearned for the honourable freedoms — of achieving his potential, of earning his keep, of marrying and having a family, of living lawfully in a law-abiding society.
      Later he realised that these were only illusory, because he was not free and neither were his people. He came to understand that freedom is indivisible: the chains on any one of his people were chains on all of them. He saw that the oppressed and the oppressor alike are robbed of their humanity, and that freedom is not merely to throw off one's chains but to live in a way that respects and enhances the freedom of others.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] What kind of South Africa did Mandela promise at his inauguration, and what values did he stress?
    Ans:
      He promised a society in which all South Africans, black and white, could walk tall without fear, assured of their human dignity: a “rainbow nation”.
      He said that the new government would never allow one group to oppress another again.
      He paid tribute to the courage of ordinary people who had resisted apartheid.
      He spoke of the twin obligations to family and to one’s people, and said freedom is indivisible: the oppressor and the oppressed must both be freed.
      His speech joined the personal and the national, showing that freedom, to be real, must be shared by everyone.

CHAPTER 3 · Two Stories about Flying
Unit: First Flight · Prose
========================================================================

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] Why was the young seagull afraid to fly? What compelled him to finally make the flight?
    Ans: He was afraid because he felt certain that his wings would never support him, and because his siblings had already flown while he was left behind on the ledge. What compelled him to fly was hunger: when his mother tore a piece of fish and stood on the ledge, the sight of the food maddened him, and he dived at it. In that dive he fell outward into space and, flapping his wings, found that he could fly.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] Describe the help the pilot received during the storm in 'The Black Aeroplane'.
    Ans: When his instruments failed and he ran low on fuel, he saw a black aeroplane with no lights on its wings. Its pilot waved to him and led him through the storm until the narrator came out of the clouds and could see the runway. When he landed and asked the woman in the control centre about the other pilot, she told him there had been no other aeroplane in the sky that night, and no other plane on her radar.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] How did the seagull's family react when he flew for the first time?
    Ans: They screamed around him, praising him and offering him scraps of fish. They landed ahead of him on the water, beckoning him to join them, and when he finally sank tired onto the sea and floated, they praised him again — his first flight was complete.

Q4. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes that the young seagull flew because his father threatened him. Correct this with evidence from the story.
    Ans:
      It was hunger, not a threat, that made him fly.
      His parents and brothers had coaxed and taunted him, but he stayed frozen on the ledge.
      When his mother flew by with a piece of fish and stopped just out of reach, he was so hungry that he dived at it, and in the dive his wings opened and he found himself flying.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A friend refuses to try swimming after one bad experience. Using the young seagull’s experience, give her three pieces of advice.
    Ans:
      Fear often comes from imagining that we cannot do something; the seagull was sure his wings would not hold him, yet they did.
      A small push or a good reason can help us take the first step; in the story it was hunger.
      Once we begin, the fear passes quickly, as it did for the seagull, so she should try again with someone supportive.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] How did the young seagull finally overcome his fear of flying?
    Ans:
      His family taunted and cajoled him, but he was too afraid to leave the ledge.
      When his mother flew past him with a piece of fish in her beak and stopped just out of reach, the pang of hunger was stronger than his fear.
      He dived at the fish, lost his balance, and his wings spread and lifted him automatically.
      His fear lasted only a moment; once in the air he was flying, and his parents and brothers praised him.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] Compare the mother's role in ‘His First Flight’ with the black aeroplane pilot's role. How is each a guide who appears at the right moment?
    Ans:
      The mother tempts the seagull with fish and then stops short, forcing him to cross the gap between fear and flight — she guides by testing.
      The unknown pilot appears when the narrator's instruments fail and his fuel runs low, leading him through the storm to safety — he guides by rescuing.
      Both appear at the exact moment when the frightened one must move forward, and both vanish once courage takes over.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    The seagull’s parents and brothers coaxed and scolded him, and his mother finally held a piece of fish out of his reach.
    
    (a) Why did the mother hold the food out of reach?
    (b) What effect did hunger have on the seagull?
    (c) Do you approve of the family’s method? Why?
    Ans:
      (a) She wanted to tempt him to leave the ledge; he could get the fish only by flying.
      (b) It was stronger than his fear, so he dived at the fish and began to fly.
      (c) An answer with a reason: for example, it worked because gentle persuasion had failed, though it was a harsh push for a frightened young bird.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Over the storm, the pilot’s instruments failed and his fuel ran low. Then a black aeroplane appeared in front of him.
    
    (a) How would you describe the pilot’s state of mind?
    (b) Which two details increase the tension?
    (c) What finally brought him to safety?
    Ans:
      (a) Anxious and frightened, but hopeful once he saw the other plane.
      (b) The failure of his instruments in the storm and the shortage of fuel.
      (c) He followed the black aeroplane, which led him through the clouds until he saw the runway.

Q10. [Application · 4 marks] Who do you think was in the black aeroplane? Give reasons for your answer.
    Ans:
      The pilot saw no lights, no other plane and no runway below, and he had lost his radio, compass and fuel gauge in the storm.
      The black aeroplane appeared beside him, guided him through the clouds and then vanished before he landed.
      The Paris control room reported that the radar showed an unidentified plane flying beside him the whole time.
      These details suggest the helper was not an ordinary pilot — it reads like the pilot's own courage, or a guardian spirit, and the story leaves the question deliberately open.

Q11. [Long · 5 marks] 'Both the stories are about overcoming fear.' Justify with reference to the two stories.
    Ans:
      In His First Flight, the young seagull is paralysed by fear while his brothers and sister fly. His parents scold him and his siblings taunt him, but fear keeps him on the ledge for twenty-four hours. Only extreme hunger, and the sight of his mother with a piece of fish, drive him to dive — and in that desperate moment he discovers that his wings do support him. His fear is overcome by necessity and by the example of his family.
      In The Black Aeroplane, the narrator is an experienced pilot, but the storm terrifies him: the compass and radio fail, he cannot see anything, and he has only a few minutes of fuel left. His fear is overcome by following the mysterious black aeroplane, whose pilot leads him out of the storm and vanishes. He never finds out who helped him, which suggests that the courage came from within.
      In both stories, fear is conquered only by taking the leap — the seagull by diving, the pilot by following into the dark — and in both the reward is a safe arrival.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Draw a character sketch of the young seagull and show how he changes from the beginning of the story to the end.
    Ans:
      At first he is timid and afraid: he stays on the ledge while his brothers and sisters fly.
      He is stubborn in his fear: he ignores the coaxing and scolding of his family.
      He is also very hungry, and this makes him forget his fear for a moment and dive for the fish.
      Once in the air he finds that his wings carry him; he becomes joyful and confident.
      The change shows that fear is often only in the mind, and that an experience can end it.

CHAPTER 4 · From the Diary of Anne Frank
Unit: First Flight · Prose
========================================================================

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] Why did Anne Frank feel the need for a diary?
    Ans: Because although she had a loving family, friends and admirers, she had no true friend — nobody to whom she could confide her innermost thoughts. She wrote that paper has more patience than people, so she began the diary as a substitute for a real friend, and named it Kitty.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What argument did Anne make in her first essay, 'A Chatterbox'?
    Ans: She argued that talking is a student's trait and that she would try to keep it under control, but that she would never be able to cure herself completely of the habit, because her mother talks as much as she does. She added that there is little one can do about inherited traits — an argument which Mr Keesing took in the right spirit.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] What does Anne write about her grandmother?
    Ans: Anne writes that nobody knows how often she thinks of her grandmother and still loves her. Her grandmother was born in 1888 and died in January 1942, and Anne says that she wrote the diary partly so that this love would not be lost. She adds that this whole diary was written in the thought of her grandmother.

Q4. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes that Anne had no friends and named her diary “Anne”. Correct both statements.
    Ans:
      Anne had many friends and admirers, about thirty of them, but no true friend with whom she could share her deepest thoughts.
      She named her diary Kitty and wrote to it as to a friend.
      She felt that “paper has more patience than people”, so she could pour out her feelings in it.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] Many teenagers today keep a diary or a blog. Compare their reasons with Anne’s and give two similarities.
    Ans:
      Like Anne, they want a private place to express feelings they cannot share easily with others.
      They also use writing to understand themselves and to record events and memories.
      A difference is that Anne wrote in hiding and for herself, while a blog today is often written for an audience.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] Why did Anne Frank decide to keep a diary?
    Ans:
      She had no real friend in whom she could confide; although she had many acquaintances, she could not discuss her innermost thoughts with them.
      A diary cannot talk back, and she felt that paper has more patience than people.
      She wanted to write out her deepest thoughts and feelings, and the diary became the friend she called Kitty.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] What was Anne's argument in her third essay, and why did it win over Mr Keesing?
    Ans:
      The third essay was a poem — ‘Quack, Quack, Quack, Said Mistress Chatterbox’ — about a mother duck and a father swan whose three ducklings are bitten to death by the father for quacking too much.

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] Why is the baker called a friend, companion and guide in Goa?
    Ans: Because he was woven into the daily life of the village. Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What do you know about the people of Coorg?
    Ans: They are a proud race of martial men, beautiful women and wild creatures. They are perhaps the most hospitable people in India and are the only people in India permitted to carry firearms without a licence. One theory says they are descended from a part of Alexander's army that settled there and married among the locals; another says they are of Arab origin, because the Kuppia, the long black coat with an embroidered waist-belt worn by the Kodavus, resembles the kuffia worn by Arabs and Kurds.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] How does the legend of tea connect China and India?
    Ans: According to a Chinese legend, tea was first drunk in China around 2700 BC, when a few leaves from a twig burning under a vessel of boiling water fell into it and gave a delicious drink. An Indian legend says that Bodhidharma, an ancient Buddhist ascetic, cut off his eyelids because he felt sleepy during meditation, and ten tea plants grew out of the eyelids; the leaves of these plants, when put in hot water and drunk, banished sleep. Tea came to Europe only in the sixteenth century, and was drunk more as medicine than as a beverage.

      According to the Chinese legend, tea was first drunk around 2700 BC, when leaves from a burning twig fell into a vessel of boiling water.
      (An Indian legend adds that tea plants grew from the eyelids of the monk Bodhidharma.)

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] The Goan baker is part of village life. Name one person from your own locality, such as a milkman or a postman, who is similarly part of community life, and explain how in about three lines.

      Like the baker, he is present in families’ regular life and sometimes in special occasions, so people think of him as a friend and not only a seller.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] What is a ‘pader' and how does Goa's Portuguese inheritance survive in its bread?
    Ans:

      The bread is baked in the old-fashioned ‘pod' or mud ovens, and the baker's bamboo staff thumping on the floor is still heard at dawn.
      Marriage gifts called ‘bol' and the sweet bread of special occasions keep the tradition alive.

      Assam contributes a global story — tea drunk eighty crore cups a day, linking Chinese legend and Indian gardens. Together they show India's variety.

From this she learnt that the town was six miles from her village, that the fare was thirty paise one way, and that the trip took forty-five minutes.
      Saving: she resisted every temptation to buy peppermints, toys and balloons, and finally she even overcame her urge to ride the merry-go-round at the village annual fair, so that she could save sixty paise in all.

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] Why did Kisa Gotami go from house to house, and what did she discover?
    Ans: Her only son had died, and she went from house to house asking for medicine to cure him. The Buddha told her to bring a handful of mustard seed, but on the condition that it be taken from a house where no one had died. In every house she entered, someone had died — a father, a daughter, a servant — so she could not find a single such house. She discovered that death is common to all, and that her grief had made her blind to the suffering of others.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What did the Buddha say about grieving?
    Ans: He said that no one obtains peace of mind from weeping or from grief; on the contrary, the pain will be the greater and the body will suffer, and the person will make himself sick and pale, yet the dead are not saved by his lamentation. Those who seek peace of mind should draw out the arrow of lamentation, complaint and grief, and having drawn it out, they will obtain peace.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] Explain the meaning of 'the world is afflicted with death and decay, therefore the wise do not grieve'.
    Ans: It means that death and change are built into the world, so no amount of sorrow can prevent them. The wise understand this truth and accept it, so they do not grieve; they realise that lamentation cannot bring back the dead and only harms the living. Accepting the nature of the world is what brings peace of mind.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] What were the four sights that led the Buddha to leave the palace?
    Ans: While out hunting at the age of about twenty-five, the prince came upon a sick man, then an aged man, then a funeral procession, and finally a monk begging for alms. These four sights showed him sickness, old age, death and renunciation, and moved him so deeply that he left the palace at once in search of enlightenment.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes that the Buddha refused to help Kisa Gotami and that the sermon was given at Bodh Gaya. Correct both statements.
    Ans:
      The Buddha did not refuse; he asked her to bring a handful of mustard seed, with one condition: it must come from a house where no one had died.
      The sermon is called the Sermon at Benares because it was delivered at Sarnath, near Benares.
      The condition was the whole lesson: it led Kisa Gotami to see for herself that death comes to every house.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A friend has lost her grandfather and is deeply upset. Using the Buddha’s approach, say how you would console her in three points.
    Ans:
      Listen to her first and let her speak of her loss, as the Buddha listened to Kisa Gotami.
      Gently help her see that death comes to every family, so she is not alone in her grief.
      Remind her that grieving cannot bring the person back, and that remembering him with love will bring peace.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] What did Kisa Gotami learn from the handful of mustard seed?
    Ans:
      Carrying her dead child from neighbour to neighbour, Kisa Gotami was told to bring mustard seed from a house where no one had ever died.
      She went from house to house and everyone was willing to give her seed, but no household was free of death.
      She saw that there was not a single house where some beloved one had not died, and understood that death is common to all.
      She accepted the truth, stopped grieving, and took her child's body to the cremation ground before returning to the Buddha to become his follower.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    The Buddha said, “Those who seek peace of mind should pull out the arrow of lamentation, and complaint, and grief.”
    
    (a) What is the “arrow”?
    (b) What does “pulling it out” mean?
    (c) Why does the Buddha use this image?
    Ans:
      (a) The pain of lamentation, complaint and grief.
      (b) Letting go of grief and accepting what has happened.
      (c) An arrow that stays in the body goes on hurting; in the same way, grief that is held on to goes on wounding the mind, so removing it brings peace.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Kisa Gotami carried her dead son from house to house asking for medicine. At last she went to the Buddha.
    
    (a) Why did she carry the dead child with her?
    (b) How did the people react to her request for mustard seed?
    (c) Why did the Buddha let her find the answer herself?
    Ans:
      (a) She could not accept that her son was dead and hoped someone could cure him.
      (b) Everyone was willing to give the seed, but no house could say that no one had died there.
      (c) A truth that one discovers by experience is understood and accepted more deeply than one that is simply told.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] Describe the Buddha's sermon on death and its message.
    Ans:
      The Buddha told Kisa Gotami that the life of mortals in this world is troubled and brief and combined with pain; there is no means by which those who are born can avoid dying.
      He said that a man is like a potter's earthen vessel: the vessels end in being broken, and so does mortal life.
      Both young and adult, both those who are fools and those who are wise, all fall into the power of death; the only escape is to accept it and to live wisely.
      He asked her not to grieve, because weeping and complainers gain nothing; only a man who does not tremble at death and does not grieve finds peace.

Q11. [Long · 5 marks] How did the Buddha make Kisa Gotami understand the nature of death without preaching to her?
    Ans:
      He did not tell her the truth directly. He asked her to bring him a handful of mustard seed — a simple request that gave her hope.
      He then added one condition: the mustard seed must be taken from a house where no one had died, where no son, husband, parent or servant had been lost.
      Kisa Gotami went from door to door with confidence, but in the first house she was told the father had died; in the next, a daughter; then a servant, then a slave. There was no house untouched by death.
      By evening she was tired and hopeless. She sat and watched the lights of the city being lit and then going out until darkness covered everything, and she realised the selfishness of her grief — she had been absorbed in her own loss while death is common to all.
      So the Buddha's method was to let her experience the truth rather than hear it, which is why it changed her completely. She took her child's body to the forest and returned to him ready to listen.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] What lessons does the Buddha’s sermon offer for dealing with loss today? Give five points.
    Ans:
      Death is common to all; no person or family is spared.
      Grief, however deep, cannot bring the dead back and only harms the living.
      A person is helped more by understanding than by advice, as Kisa Gotami learned by her own search.
      Seeing other people’s sorrow makes us more compassionate and less alone.
      Acceptance of change brings peace of mind, and that is the wisdom to which the wise hold.

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] Why does Chubukov think Lomov has come to him?
    Ans: Because Lomov had come to him the previous year to borrow money, and Chubukov expects the same again. He even complains that Lomov is always coming to him for a loan, and when he hears that Lomov has a request, he assumes it is for money. Only when Lomov asks for Natalya's hand does Chubukov understand.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What is the Oxen Meadows quarrel about?
    Ans: It is about who owns the Oxen Meadows, which lie between Lomov's Burnt Marsh and Chubukov's birchwood. Natalya insists the meadows are hers; Lomov says they are his, arguing that his aunt's grandmother gave the peasants of Chubukov's grandfather the free use of the meadows in perpetuity in return for their making bricks for her. The peasants had used them for forty years and regarded them as their own, but neither side will give way, and the argument turns into an exchange of insults.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] Describe Lomov's character.
    Ans: Lomov is a thirty-five-year-old landowner who is timid, nervous and easily flustered. He is a hypochondriac: he complains of palpitations, a twitching right eyebrow, sleeplessness and numbness in his leg. He is also stubborn and quick to quarrel, which is why a marriage proposal turns into two arguments. Yet he is sincere in his wish to settle down, and he finally gets what he came for.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] How does the play end? Comment on the ending.
    Ans: Lomov collapses after the argument about the dogs, and Chubukov, without waiting for anything to be settled, blesses the couple and cries that Natalya is willing, calling for champagne. Natalya does not object, and the proposal is agreed. The ending is comic because the marriage is decided in the middle of a quarrel that neither side has stopped fighting — nobody has spoken a word about love, and the audience is left to see how absurd the whole arrangement is.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes: “The Proposal is a short story by G. L. Fuentes in which Chubukov refuses to let Natalya marry Lomov.” Correct both errors.
    Ans:
      The Proposal is a one-act play (a farce) by Anton Chekhov; G. L. Fuentes wrote “A Letter to God”.
      Chubukov does not refuse: he is delighted at the proposal and, at the end, blesses the couple and calls for champagne.
      The quarrels over Oxen Meadows and the dogs nearly spoil the match, but they end in agreement.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] Lomov complains of palpitations, a twitching eyebrow and a numb leg whenever he is excited. What does this show about him and how does it add to the humour?
    Ans:
      It shows that he is nervous, timid and a hypochondriac who worries too much about his health.
      His complaints come at the most tense moments, so his body seems to react whenever he is upset.
      This exaggeration makes him comic and adds to the farce.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] How does ‘The Proposal' satirise the landed gentry?
    Ans:
      Instead of a romantic proposal, the play shows two neighbours quarrelling over a strip of land and two dogs.
      Lomov, a wealthy landowner, comes to ask for Natalya's hand but cannot keep from arguing about Oxen Meadows and about Squeezer and Guess.
      Both Chubukov and his daughter lose their temper, shout, and even insult each other's families, while Lomov's real business is forgotten.
      Chekhov mocks the vanity, greed and ill-temper of the gentry, for whom property and dogs matter more than affection, and who are ready to marry for convenience.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:

    (a) Whose dog is each?
    (b) What are the two sides arguing?
    (c) What does the quarrel show about the characters?
    Ans:
      (a) Squeezer belongs to Lomov; Guess belongs to Chubukov.
      (b) Each insists that his own dog is better and more valuable and can hunt better.
      (c) That the landowners are proud and petty: they fight over trivial things even when a marriage is at stake.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A farce is a comedy with exaggerated characters, absurd situations and quick action.
    
    (a) Give two features of farce in The Proposal.
    (b) Which character’s weakness is most exaggerated?
    (c) Why might Chekhov have used farce to criticise people?
    Ans:
      (a) A marriage proposal becomes two quarrels; and the characters shout, faint and make up in a hurry.
      (b) Lomov’s hypochondria: the palpitations and numb leg.
      (c) Laughter exposes their faults gently but clearly; the audience sees how silly the landed gentry’s pride and greed are.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] Describe the three quarrels in the play and how the proposal is finally made.
    Ans:
      First quarrel: over Oxen Meadows, which Lomov says belonged to his aunt's grandmother and Natalya claims for her family.
      Second quarrel: over the dogs — Lomov's Squeezer and Chubukov's Guess, and whose dog is worth more and can hunt better.
      Third quarrel: after Lomov faints and recovers, Chubukov simply tells Natalya to agree, and Lomov's hypochondria and palpitations are brought up.
      Chubukov finally forces the proposal through, blesses the couple and hurries them to be married before they quarrel again; the ending is deliberately comic, not romantic.

Q11. [Long · 5 marks] How does Chekhov use the two quarrels to create comedy and to criticise the characters?
    Ans:
      The first quarrel, about the Oxen Meadows, begins before Lomov has even made his proposal. Natalya claims the meadows are hers, Lomov insists they are his, and both become abusive, calling each other a land grabber and an intriguess. Chubukov joins in, calls Lomov an old rat and a Jesuit, and orders him out of the house. The absurdity is that a marriage proposal has become a land dispute, and that Chubukov does not know what the proposal was until after the quarrel.
      The second quarrel, about the dogs, follows immediately. Lomov praises his dog Squeezer; Chubukov insists his own Guess is better, and they argue over whether Squeezer's muzzle is short, whether Guess is purebred, and whether Squeezer's legs are crooked. The two men become hysterical and Lomov collapses.
      The criticism: Chekhov shows that for these landowners, property and possessions matter far more than human relationships. Neither Lomov nor Natalya says a word about love or about each other's feelings, and the marriage is arranged in the middle of an argument. The comedy exposes a class that treats marriage as a transaction.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Compare the characters of Natalya and Chubukov, showing how each behaves during the play.
    Ans:
      Natalya is proud and quick-tempered: she insists that Oxen Meadows are hers and argues about the dogs.
      She is also eager to be married: when she learns that Lomov came to propose, she is upset that he left and calls him back.
      Chubukov is welcoming when he thinks Lomov has come to propose, becomes abusive in the quarrel, and again is eager when the match seems possible.
      Both are stubborn about property and pride, and both quickly change their mood.
      This makes them comic: their feelings follow their interests, not reason.

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] What change of mood does the poet describe and what caused it?
    Ans: The poet was in a depressed or sorrowful mood and had regretted the day. The change came when a crow shook down a dust of snow on him from a hemlock tree. This small, unexpected event lifted his spirits and saved some part of the day that he had rued.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] Why is it significant that the bird is a crow and the tree a hemlock?
    Ans: Because both are traditionally associated with sorrow and death — the crow is considered an ill omen, and the hemlock is a poisonous plant. The poet deliberately chooses them to show that joy can come from the most unlikely and unfavourable sources, and that our associations should not blind us to the good in things.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] Explain the central idea of the poem.
    Ans: The central idea is that a small, natural event can transform a person's outlook. The poet is gloomy and regretting his day, but the dust of snow shaken down by a crow gives him a sudden change of mood and saves part of the day. Frost suggests that we should stay open to such small joys, because they have the power to alter our whole perspective.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What is the humour in the poem?
    Ans: The humour comes from the poet's pretended practical advice: the only reliable way to identify a wild animal is to be attacked by it, and by the time you are certain, you are being eaten. The poet also plays with the animals' characteristics — the bear's hug, the crocodile's tears, the hyena's smile — turning well-known sayings into a comic method of identification.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] Explain the lines about the crocodile and the hyena.

A hyena is supposed to laugh, so the hyena 'comes with merry smiles'. By contrasting weeping with smiling, the poet gives the reader an amusing rule for telling the two apart — a rule that would only be tested at the cost of one's life.

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] What does the poet compare the fog to? Does he actually say that the fog is a cat?
    Ans: The poet compares the fog to a cat. He says it comes on little cat feet, sits on silent haunches looking over the harbour and city, and then moves on — all the quiet, soft, unhurried movements of a cat. He never says directly that the fog is a cat, and he never uses the word 'like' or 'as', so the comparison is a metaphor rather than a simile.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What is the central idea of the poem?
    Ans: The central idea is that fog, like a cat, arrives silently and without warning, covers the city and the harbour, looks down on them, and then moves away. The comparison brings out the mysterious, gentle and unobtrusive nature of fog, and shows how a simple natural event can be described vividly in a few lines.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] Identify the figure of speech used in 'The fog comes on little cat feet'.
    Ans: It is a metaphor, since the fog is directly compared to a cat without the use of 'like' or 'as'. There is also personification, because the fog is given the cat's action of walking on little feet and sitting on silent haunches.

    Ans:
      Correct method: It is a metaphor, not a simile. The poet never uses 'like' or 'as' — that distinction is a marked point.
      Why the mistake costs marks: Saying the poet says the fog is a cat. He compares it to a cat; the fog is not literally a cat.
      Write the definition or formula first, then the working or example.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] How does Carl Sandburg describe the fog?
    Ans:
      The fog comes ‘on little cat feet': silently, slowly and almost without anyone noticing.
      It sits looking over harbour and city on silent haunches, like a cat perched and watching.
      Then it moves on; it does not announce itself, and it does not stay.
      The six-line poem turns an ordinary weather event into a living creature.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] The fog ‘sits looking over harbour and city’ and then ‘moves on’. What does this arrival and departure suggest about fog?
    Ans: It suggests that fog comes quietly, stays a while like a watchful visitor, and leaves on its own — no one can summon or dismiss it. The sitting shows its stillness and patience; the moving on shows its mystery and freedom. Fog, like a cat, arrives and departs exactly as it pleases.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] What does the comparison with a cat achieve in the poem?
    Ans:
      A cat is quiet, self-possessed and indifferent to human plans, which is exactly how fog behaves in a city.
      The comparison explains why fog is noticed only when it has already arrived, and why it leaves without ceremony.
      The image also softens what is usually an inconvenience: the fog becomes playful rather than threatening.
      By using a domestic animal, Sandburg makes the natural phenomenon familiar, which is typical of his plain, imagist style.

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] Why did Custard cry for a nice safe cage?
    Ans: Because he was a coward, or at least thought of himself as one. While Belinda and the other pets boasted of their bravery, Custard admitted his fear and asked for a nice safe cage. His cry is honest, and it is what makes his later action so surprising — he is the only one who did not pretend to be brave.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] Describe the pirate and how Custard dealt with him.
    Ans: The pirate climbed in through the window with a pistol in his hand and two cutlasses shining, a black beard and one wooden leg, and he was clearly bad. Mustard fled with a terrified cry, Ink trickled down to the bottom of the house, and Blink disappeared into his mouse hole. Custard jumped up, snorting like an engine, clashing his tail like iron in a dungeon, and went at the pirate like a robin at a worm — and he ate him, every bit.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] How does Custard prove himself when the pirate appears?
    Ans:
      While Belinda, Ink, Blink and Mustard all run away in terror, Custard alone stands his ground.
      He snorts, claps his sharp teeth, gulps some mustard at the pirate and charges at him like a robin at a worm.
      The pirate fires two bullets that miss, and Custard swallows him in one bite, clashing his tail like irons in a dungeon.
      The ‘cowardly' dragon turns out to be the only one with real courage, which is the joke of the poem.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] How do Belinda and the other pets behave after Custard kills the pirate? What does their behaviour show?
    Ans:
      Belinda embraces Custard and Mustard licks him — but within a page they go back to boasting: Mustard says he would have been twice as brave, Ink and Blink say they would have been three times as brave, and Custard agrees with all of them.
      Their behaviour shows that cowards mock true courage and then claim the credit afterwards — while the truly brave Custard keeps quiet and asks again for his nice safe cage.

Q9. [Long · 4 marks] What makes ‘The Tale of Custard the Dragon' a ballad of humour?
    Ans:
      It is written in the form of a ballad, with regular quatrains, a strong rhythm and a repeating refrain about the little pet dragon.
      The names are comic — Belinda, Ink, Blink, Mustard and Custard — and the rhyme is deliberately playful, as in ‘realio, trulio, little pet dragon'.
      The situation is upside down: the fierce-looking pets flee while the teased dragon fights, and the pirate is described as a dastardly coward.
      The ending is comic too: once the danger is over, everyone goes back to boasting, and Custard meekly wishes for his nice safe cage again.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] ‘The Tale of Custard the Dragon’ is called a ballad. Which features of a ballad do you find in it?
    Ans:
      A story in verse — the ballad narrates a complete tale with a beginning (the household), a crisis (the pirate) and an ending (the kill).
      Music and repetition — the aabb rhyme, the rolling rhythm and repeated refrains like ‘coward, and she called him Custard’ make it singable.
      A dramatic climax — the pirate's attack and Custard's sudden bravery give it the ballad's single moment of high action.

Q11. [Long · 5 marks] What is the central idea of the poem? Is Custard a coward?
    Ans:
      The poem is a humorous story about bravery and boasting. Belinda, Ink, Blink and Mustard all boast about how brave they are, while Custard openly admits his fear and asks for a nice safe cage.
      When the pirate actually appears, the boasters run away: Mustard flees with a terrified cry, Ink trickles to the bottom of the house, and Blink disappears into his mouse hole. Only Custard faces the pirate and eats him up, every bit.
      The moral is that those who boast are not necessarily brave, and the one who admits his fear may be the bravest of all when action is needed. Bravery is shown in deeds, not in words.
      Is Custard a coward? The poet leaves this open. Custard himself says he is a coward and would still prefer a nice safe cage, and the others all agree they are braver than he is. The humour lies in the fact that nobody changes their opinion, even after Custard has saved them. So the poem suggests that a person can be genuinely brave in action and still be timid by nature — the two are not contradictory.

CHAPTER 19 · For Anne Gregory
Unit: First Flight · Poems
========================================================================

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] What does the poet mean by 'honey-coloured ramparts at your ear'?
    Ans: He is comparing Anne's yellow hair to the ramparts, or high walls, of a fort. The hair hangs around her ears like a wall that young men cannot cross, and it throws them into despair because they cannot help being attracted to her beauty. It is a metaphor, since no 'like' or 'as' is used.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What is Anne Gregory's reply, and does it answer the poet's point?
    Ans: Anne replies that she can dye her hair another colour — brown, or black, or carrot — so that young men could love her for herself alone and not for her yellow hair. She believes that by removing the attraction of her hair she would prove the poet wrong. But the poet answers that according to a religious text only God can love a person for themselves alone, so her plan would not change the fact that human love is always mixed with physical attraction.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] What is the central idea of the poem?
    Ans: The central idea is that physical beauty draws people, and that it is almost impossible for a human being to love another for their inner self alone. Anne thinks that by dyeing her hair she could prove otherwise, but the poet quotes a religious text to show that only God is capable of such selfless love. The poem sets outer beauty against inner worth and suggests that true, disinterested love is divine rather than human.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] What does the poet say about young men's love? Give the reason he gives.

Unit: Footprints Without Feet

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] Why did Tricki become so ill?

Unit: Reading, Grammar & Writing
========================================================================

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] What is the difference between a discursive and a case-based passage?
    Ans: A discursive passage is 400–450 words and argues or discusses an idea, so the questions test inference, interpretation and evaluation. A case-based factual passage is 200–250 words and is supported by visual input such as statistical data or a chart, so the questions test your ability to read the data and connect it to the text.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] Describe a method for attempting the reading section in the time available.
    Ans: Read the questions first, so that you know what to look for. Then read the passage once quickly for the main idea. Go question by question, locate the relevant line and underline it. Eliminate the two options that are clearly wrong, then choose between the remaining two by returning to the text. Keep to about ninety seconds per question and mark any you cannot decide, returning to them at the end.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] How should you answer a vocabulary-in-context question?
    Ans: Find the sentence containing the word, cover the word, and decide for yourself what word would fit in its place. Then look at the options and choose the one closest to your own word. Do not choose a meaning you have met elsewhere, because the meaning must fit the sentence in front of you.

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] Describe the structure of an analytical paragraph.
    Ans: The paragraph opens with a sentence introducing the data — what it shows, the period it covers and the units used.       ‘Fell sharply’ — for a sudden drop, as in accidents after speed cameras were installed.
      ‘Peaked in’ — for the highest point, as in sales peaking in December.
      ‘Twice as much as’ — for direct comparison, as in bus users being twice as many as car users.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:

Q9. [Application · 4 marks] A survey of 200 students of a school showed these preferences for co-curricular activities: Sports 80, Music 50, Art 40, Debate 30. Write an analytical paragraph.
    Ans:
      A survey of 200 students shows clear preferences among the four co-curricular activities offered by the school.
      Sports is the most popular choice, preferred by 80 students, or 40 per cent of those surveyed, while music is second with 50 students, or 25 per cent.
      Art is chosen by 40 students (20 per cent) and debate by only 30 (15 per cent), which together account for just over a third of the students.
      The pattern suggests that physical activity attracts the largest number of students, while activities that require performance in front of an audience, such as debate, attract the fewest; the school could encourage the less popular activities by giving them more time, trained coaches and recognition.

Q10. [Application · 4 marks] A student writes: ‘I think the chart is wrong and the school should buy more buses.’ Identify what is wrong with this sentence for an analytical paragraph.
    Ans:
      First, ‘I think the chart is wrong’ disputes the given data — the data must be accepted and analysed, never challenged.
      Second, ‘the school should buy more buses’ is a personal opinion and a suggestion — analytical paragraphs report trends and inferences, not opinions.
      The correct close would state what the data shows, for example that bus use dominates and is still rising.

Q11. [Long · 5 marks] Write an analytical paragraph on a bar chart showing the mode of transport used by students: bus 45%, cycle 25%, walk 20%, private car 10%.
    Ans:
      The bar chart shows how students travel to school, expressed as a percentage of the total number of students.
      The most common mode of transport is the bus, used by 45 per cent of students — almost half. Cycling is the second most popular, at 25 per cent, while 20 per cent of students walk to school. Only 10 per cent travel by private car, the least common mode.
      Taken together, the figures show that the great majority of students — 90 per cent — use public or non-motorised transport, and that reliance on private cars is minimal. This suggests that improving bus services and cycle lanes would benefit far more students than any measure aimed at car users.

B. MCQs (8)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. An analytical paragraph is based on: (a) a story (b) a chart, graph, table or set of clues (c) a poem (d) a personal experience
    Ans: (b) a chart, graph, table, data set, outline or set of clues provided in the question.

M2. The word limit for an analytical paragraph is: (a) 50–60 words (b) 100–120 words (c) 150–200 words (d) no limit
    Ans: (b) 100–120 words.

M3. The concluding sentence of an analytical paragraph should: (a) give a personal opinion (b) state a trend or inference from the data (c) list more figures (d) repeat the introduction
    Ans: (b) state a trend or inference from the data — not a personal opinion, unless the question asks for one.

M4. The most exam-ready way to revise “Analytical Paragraph” is: (a) read the heading only  (b) learn the key idea plus one example  (c) skip written practice  (d) memorise a random extra topic
    Ans: (b) learn the key idea plus one example. The chapter is A data-based paragraph from a chart, graph, table or set of clues.

M5. A 3-mark answer on “Analytical Paragraph” should: (a) be one sentence  (b) start with the idea, then a reason or method  (c) copy the whole chapter  (d) leave the example out
    Ans: (b) start with the idea, then a reason or method. Step marks follow that frame.

M6. An analytical paragraph must be based on: (a) the data given in the chart or table  (b) personal opinion only  (c) imaginary facts  (d) a news report
    Ans: (a) the data given in the chart or table. Your own views are added only as a conclusion.

M7. The usual length of an analytical paragraph in the board paper is: (a) 100 to 120 words  (b) 300 words  (c) 20 words  (d) 500 words
    Ans: (a) 100 to 120 words. It should cover the trend, the data and one conclusion.

M8. Figures in an analytical paragraph should be: (a) compared with each other to show a trend (b) listed one by one without comment (c) invented to fill space (d) rounded off to zero
    Ans: (a) compared with each other to show a trend. Data without comparison is a list, not an analysis.
