A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] How does the poet suggest you can tell a leopard from a tiger?
    Ans: The Bengal tiger has a hide with black stripes on a yellow ground and announces itself with a roar. The leopard has a hide with spots that pepper it, and it does not roar — it simply leaps at you, and it will do no good to roar with pain, because it will only leap again.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What is the humour in the poem?
    Ans: The humour comes from the poet's pretended practical advice: the only reliable way to identify a wild animal is to be attacked by it, and by the time you are certain, you are being eaten. The advice is useless to the reader, who is meant to be the bait. The poet also plays with the animals' characteristics — the bear's hug, the crocodile's tears, the hyena's smile — turning well-known sayings into a comic method of identification.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] Explain the lines about the crocodile and the hyena.
    Ans: The poet plays on two popular beliefs. A crocodile is said to weep before it eats its prey, hence 'crocodile tears'; so if a creature weeps, it is a crocodile. A hyena is supposed to laugh, so the hyena 'comes with merry smiles'. By contrasting weeping with smiling, the poet gives the reader an amusing rule for telling the two apart — a rule that would only be tested at the cost of one's life.

Q4. [Application · 3 marks] A visitor uses the poem as a real field guide and plans to approach a striped animal to identify it. Explain why this misunderstands Carolyn Wells's method and contrast the tiger with the leopard.
    Ans:
      The poem's “identification” advice is a joke: its tests involve getting dangerously close to an animal, often while it attacks.
      The Bengal tiger is described by its black stripes on a yellow ground; the leopard is recognised by its spots and its leap.
      The speaker's exaggerated method creates humour and should not be followed as actual wildlife guidance.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] You are making a safety poster about the poem. Choose two of its animal clues and explain how the poet turns each into a comic, but dangerous, test.
    Ans:
      The bear is identified by a very tight hug, while the hyena is recognised by its merry smile.
      The joke is that these are not safe tests: the reader would have to be close to wild animals, and the “answer” could come at a fatal cost.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] What is the poet's humorous advice for identifying a Bengal tiger?
    Ans:
      She tells the reader to roar at a tiger, and then count — one, two, three.
      If he roars again, you will know you ‘might have been' a Bengal tiger.
      The joke is that you will not live to count: the tiger will eat you.
      The humour comes from giving practical-sounding advice that is completely useless, which is the method of the whole poem.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] How would you recognise a bear, a hyena and a chameleon according to the poem?
    Ans:
      A bear — if it hugs you very, very hard when you meet it in the yard, you may be sure it is a bear.
      A hyena — it smiles merrily while it eats you; the smiling eater is the hyena, unlike the weeping crocodile.
      A chameleon — a small lizard-like thing with no ears and no wings, seen sitting on a tree.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    The poem offers clues to several animals: a tiger has black stripes on yellow, a leopard has spots, the bear gives a powerful hug, and the hyena appears to smile. The advice is delivered as if the reader can test each clue at close range.
    
    (a) Which two animals are distinguished by their markings?
    (b) Which clue is linked with the bear?
    (c) Why is the speaker's “guide” humorous rather than practical?
    Ans:
      The Bengal tiger has black stripes on a yellow ground; the leopard has spots.
      The bear's tight hug is the clue associated with it.
      The tests require meeting dangerous animals at close range, so the confident instructions become absurd and ironic.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A student groups the poem's clues like this: the hyena smiles, the crocodile sheds tears, and the chameleon is hard to notice on a tree. Each stanza sounds like advice from a guide, but the outcome of following the advice could be dangerous.
    
    (a) Which two animals are contrasted through smiling and tears?
    (b) What makes the chameleon clue different?
    (c) How does the repeated guide-like tone create humour?
    Ans:
      The hyena is associated with a merry smile, while the crocodile is associated with tears.
      The chameleon is identified by blending into its surroundings; it is not presented through an attack like the other clues.
      The calm, confident tone treats risky encounters as simple tests, while readers recognise how dangerous they would be in reality.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] How does Carolyn Wells create humour in the poem?
    Ans:
      She writes in the tone of a serious field guide, with instructions like ‘if you should go to the jungle, you must beware of the Bengal tiger'.
      She gives absurd tests: roar at the tiger, hug a bear, and if a creature roars at you while you are dying you are in the east.
      Her descriptions are accurate but the situations are fatal, and she describes death as lightly as a game — ‘a novice might nonplus', ‘you'll find the Chameleon, I think' .
      The effect is a comic, mock-serious poem that teaches the reader a little natural history while making fun of the very idea of a handbook on wild animals.

Q11. [Application · 4 marks] The poem's humour depends on danger. Explain with two examples how the poet makes deadly animals funny.
    Ans:
      First, the Asian lion — the poet says you will know it when it roars while you are dying beside it; the deadpan tone treats death as a mere identification mark.
      Second, the Bengal tiger — he nobly calls it ‘a noble wild beast’ and says you will know it when it eats you; politeness applied to being eaten is absurd.
      The humour lies in this mismatch: a calm, guidebook manner describing fatal attacks.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] How does Carolyn Wells combine a guidebook structure with exaggeration to make “How to Tell Wild Animals” comic?
    Ans:
      The speaker arranges the poem as a sequence of clues, offering a different way to recognise each animal as if giving a useful lesson.
      The clues are memorable: the tiger's stripes, the leopard's spots, the bear's hug, the hyena's smile and the crocodile's tears.
      The joke is that many identifications happen only when the reader is in peril, or has already been attacked. The advice is confident but absurd.
      That mismatch between a tidy guide and a dangerous encounter creates irony; the poem entertains without being a sensible field guide.

B. MCQs (8)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. 'How to Tell Wild Animals' is written by: (a) Leslie Norris (b) Vicky Tree (c) Walt Whitman (d) Carolyn Wells
    Ans: (d) Carolyn Wells.

M2. The animal that 'hugs you very, very hard' is the: (a) hyena (b) bear (c) leopard (d) chameleon
    Ans: (b) the bear — a bear hug is the popular image the poet plays on.

M3. The leopard's hide has: (a) spots that pepper it (b) black stripes on a yellow ground (c) no markings (d) brown patches
    Ans: (a) spots that pepper it; black stripes belong to the Bengal tiger.

M4. Which animal is associated with a merry smile in the poem? (a) The bear (b) The chameleon (c) The Bengal tiger (d) The hyena
    Ans: (d) The hyena. The poem contrasts its smile with the crocodile's tears.

M5. Which animal's clue depends on being difficult to see among the tree's surroundings? (a) The leopard (b) The bear (c) The chameleon (d) The Asian lion
    Ans: (c) The chameleon. Its camouflage makes it hard to notice when it is on a tree.

M6. According to the poem, the Asian lion is best known because: (a) it is striped  (b) it roars while killing its victim  (c) it is spotted  (d) it lives in the hills
    Ans: (b) it roars while killing its victim. The poet says the roar tells you it is the Asian lion, and that this is difficult to do.

M7. The poet of ‘How to Tell Wild Animals' is: (a) Ogden Nash  (b) W. B. Yeats  (c) Carolyn Wells  (d) Robin Klein
    Ans: (c) Carolyn Wells. She wrote light verse and also compiled anthologies of humorous poetry.

M8. The rhyme scheme of ‘How to Tell Wild Animals’ is: (a) ababcc (b) abab cdcd (c) aabbcc (d) abcb
    Ans: (a) ababcc. Each six-line stanza of the light verse follows this pattern.
