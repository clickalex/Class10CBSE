A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] How does the poet suggest you can tell a leopard from a tiger?
    Ans: The Bengal tiger has a hide with black stripes on a yellow ground and announces itself with a roar. The leopard has a hide with spots that pepper it, and it does not roar — it simply leaps at you, and it will do no good to roar with pain, because it will only leap again.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What is the humour in the poem?
    Ans: The humour comes from the poet's pretended practical advice: the only reliable way to identify a wild animal is to be attacked by it, and by the time you are certain, you are being eaten. The advice is useless to the reader, who is meant to be the bait. The poet also plays with the animals' characteristics — the bear's hug, the crocodile's tears, the hyena's smile — turning well-known sayings into a comic method of identification.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] Explain the lines about the crocodile and the hyena.
    Ans: The poet plays on two popular beliefs. A crocodile is said to weep before it eats its prey, hence 'crocodile tears'; so if a creature weeps, it is a crocodile. A hyena is supposed to laugh, so the hyena 'comes with merry smiles'. By contrasting weeping with smiling, the poet gives the reader an amusing rule for telling the two apart — a rule that would only be tested at the cost of one's life.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] What is the poet's humorous advice for identifying a Bengal tiger?
    Ans:
      She tells the reader to roar at a tiger, and then count — one, two, three.
      If he roars again, you will know you ‘might have been' a Bengal tiger.
      The joke is that you will not live to count: the tiger will eat you.
      The humour comes from giving practical-sounding advice that is completely useless, which is the method of the whole poem.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] How would you recognise a bear, a hyena and a chameleon according to the poem?
    Ans:
      A bear — if it hugs you very, very hard when you meet it in the yard, you may be sure it is a bear.
      A hyena — it smiles merrily while it eats you; the smiling eater is the hyena, unlike the weeping crocodile.
      A chameleon — a small lizard-like thing with no ears and no wings, seen sitting on a tree.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] How does Carolyn Wells create humour in the poem?
    Ans:
      She writes in the tone of a serious field guide, with instructions like ‘if you should go to the jungle, you must beware of the Bengal tiger'.
      She gives absurd tests: roar at the tiger, hug a bear, and if a creature roars at you while you are dying you are in the east.
      Her descriptions are accurate but the situations are fatal, and she describes death as lightly as a game — ‘a novice might nonplus', ‘you'll find the Chameleon, I think' .
      The effect is a comic, mock-serious poem that teaches the reader a little natural history while making fun of the very idea of a handbook on wild animals.

Q11. [Application · 4 marks] The poem's humour depends on danger. Explain with two examples how the poet makes deadly animals funny.
    Ans:
      First, the Asian lion — the poet says you will know it when it roars while you are dying beside it; the deadpan tone treats death as a mere identification mark.
      Second, the Bengal tiger — he nobly calls it ‘a noble wild beast’ and says you will know it when it eats you; politeness applied to being eaten is absurd.
      The humour lies in this mismatch: a calm, guidebook manner describing fatal attacks.

B. MCQs (8)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. 'How to Tell Wild Animals' is written by: (a) Carolyn Wells (b) Leslie Norris (c) Vicky Tree (d) Walt Whitman
    Ans: (a) Carolyn Wells.

M2. The animal that 'hugs you very, very hard' is the: (a) bear (b) hyena (c) leopard (d) chameleon
    Ans: (a) the bear — a bear hug is the popular image the poet plays on.

M3. The leopard's hide has: (a) black stripes on a yellow ground (b) spots that pepper it (c) no markings (d) brown patches
    Ans: (b) spots that pepper it; black stripes belong to the Bengal tiger.

M4. The most exam-ready way to revise “How to Tell Wild Animals” is: (a) read the heading only  (b) learn the key idea plus one example  (c) skip written practice  (d) memorise a random extra topic
    Ans: (b) learn the key idea plus one example. The chapter is Carolyn Wells: a humorous way to identify six animals.

M5. A 3-mark answer on “How to Tell Wild Animals” should: (a) be one sentence  (b) start with the idea, then a reason or method  (c) copy the whole chapter  (d) leave the example out
    Ans: (b) start with the idea, then a reason or method. Step marks follow that frame.

M6. According to the poem, the Asian lion is best known because: (a) it roars while killing its victim  (b) it is striped  (c) it is spotted  (d) it lives in the hills
    Ans: (a) it roars while killing its victim. The poet says the roar tells you it is the Asian lion, and that this is difficult to do.

M7. The poet of ‘How to Tell Wild Animals' is: (a) Carolyn Wells  (b) Ogden Nash  (c) W. B. Yeats  (d) Robin Klein
    Ans: (a) Carolyn Wells. She wrote light verse and also compiled anthologies of humorous poetry.

M8. The rhyme scheme of ‘How to Tell Wild Animals’ is: (a) ababcc (b) abab cdcd (c) aabbcc (d) abcb
    Ans: (a) ababcc. Each six-line stanza of the light verse follows this pattern.
