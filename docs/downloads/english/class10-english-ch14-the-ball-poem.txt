A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] Why does the poet say he would not intrude on the boy?
    Ans: Because the boy is learning something important for the first time — the epistemology of loss, or the knowledge of what it is to lose something. Intruding on him, or offering a new ball, would spoil the lesson. The poet wants the boy to feel the loss fully and to learn to stand up on his own.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What does the poet mean by 'money is external'?
    Ans: He means that money can buy another ball, but it cannot buy back what has been lost or take away the feeling of loss. The material value of the ball is small — a dime — so what matters is not the object but the experience of losing it, which is something outside the reach of money.

Q3. [Application · 3 marks] A classmate says the boy in “The Ball Poem” can be consoled simply by buying another ball. Explain why the poet treats the loss as more than the price of an object.
    Ans:
      The boy has lost a particular ball connected with his own play and memories; a replacement cannot restore that experience.
      Watching him face the loss is part of learning that possessions can disappear and that grief cannot always be removed by money.
      The ball's material cost is small compared with the boy's first encounter with responsibility and loss.

Q4. [Application · 3 marks] A younger student loses a treasured object and is told to “stop worrying; we can replace it”. What lesson from the boy's experience could help the student understand the feeling?
    Ans:
      A substitute may look similar, but it cannot bring back the memories attached to the lost object.
      The boy's grief is real because he must accept that some losses cannot be undone.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] What does the boy lose in the poem, and what does the loss mean to him?
    Ans:
      He loses a ball that bounces merrily down the street and into the water.
      To an adult it is a small, cheap thing; to the boy it is the first thing he has owned and the first thing he has lost.
      He stands rigid, trembling, staring at the water where the ball went, with a helplessness the poet understands.
      The loss stands for the end of the innocent world of childhood, in which nothing is owned and nothing is lost.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] Why does the boy stand ‘rigid, trembling, staring down’? What has he understood for the first time?
    Ans: The boy stands rigid and trembling because his ball has bounced into the harbour and is gone beyond recovery. For the first time he understands loss — that what is possessed can vanish, that ‘balls will be lost always’, and that no money (‘a dime’) can buy back this particular ball with its memories. It is his first lesson in growing up.

Q7. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A boy stands still, watching the place where his ball has disappeared into the water. The speaker notices his reaction but does not rush to offer money for another one. The ball matters because of the boy's own history with it.
    
    (a) What does the boy's stillness suggest?
    (b) Why would money for a new ball not solve the whole problem?
    (c) What is the boy beginning to learn?
    Ans:
      His stillness suggests shock and grief as he watches a familiar possession disappear.
      Money could buy another ball, but not restore this ball or the memories attached to it.
      He is beginning to learn that loss is part of life and that he must face it rather than expect every loss to be reversed.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    The speaker watches the boy meet a first serious loss. The boy must understand what it means to lose something that belongs to him; a lecture or quick replacement cannot do that learning for him.
    
    (a) Why does the speaker avoid taking over the moment?
    (b) What is the boy's experience teaching him?
    (c) How does the episode connect a small object with growing up?
    Ans:
      The speaker recognises that the boy needs to experience and process the loss himself rather than be distracted by an adult's solution.
      He is learning that possessions can be lost and that grief and acceptance are part of life.
      Because the ball is tied to his own memories, losing it becomes an early lesson in responsibility, attachment and coping with what cannot be recovered.

Q9. [Long · 4 marks] Explain the ending: ‘he is learning … to stand up in the world of possessions'.
    Ans:
      The boy cannot be consoled with another ball, because the particular ball he lost cannot be replaced.
      The poet says he is learning, well behind his desperate eyes, the epistemology of loss: how to know and bear loss.
      Money is called ‘external' — it cannot buy back what is gone or undo the experience.
      So the boy is learning, through grief, to stand up among people who own and lose things: this is the painful education that makes an adult.

Q10. [Application · 4 marks] ‘Balls will be lost always.’ How does this line connect a lost ball to the larger losses of adult life?
    Ans:
      The line turns one small loss into a universal law: losing things — toys, chances, people, youth — is part of life.
      The boy's grief over a ball rehearses the grief adults feel over larger losses; the poem says we must each learn to ‘stand up’ and bear them.
      That is why the poet does not offer a new ball — sympathy cannot teach what only loss itself can teach.

Q11. [Long · 5 marks] What is the boy learning, according to the poet? Explain the significance of the first loss.
    Ans:
      The boy is learning the epistemology of loss — the knowledge of what loss means and how to live with it. He stands rigid and trembling, staring down at the harbour where his ball went, and in that moment he understands that some things, once gone, cannot be brought back.
      The poet says a dime, another ball, is worthless, because a new ball would not restore the one that is lost. Money is external, so it cannot help him here.
      The boy learns that balls will be lost always — loss is a permanent feature of life, and everyone must learn to accept it. He learns to stand up, which means to accept the loss and go on.
      The significance of the first loss is that it is the beginning of maturity. Until this moment the boy lived in a world where everything could be replaced; now he is part of a world of possessions in which things can be taken away. That realisation is painful, but it is also the first step towards becoming an adult who can bear responsibility and disappointment.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] How does John Berryman turn a lost ball into a lesson about growing up without dismissing the boy's grief?
    Ans:
      The poem focuses on the boy's fixed attention on the place where the ball has vanished; his grief is not treated as childish or silly.
      A new ball might replace the object but not the boy's memories of this one. The loss therefore cannot be measured only in money.
      The speaker does not interfere with an easy purchase or consolation. He allows the boy to meet the reality of loss and learn from it.
      The ball becomes a first lesson in responsibility and acceptance: growing up includes recognising that some things cannot be recovered.

B. MCQs (8)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. 'The Ball Poem' is written by: (a) Robert Frost (b) John Berryman (c) Leslie Norris (d) Carolyn Wells
    Ans: (b) John Berryman.

M2. The ball cost: (a) a penny (b) a dime (c) a dollar (d) five cents
    Ans: (b) a dime — a small sum, which is why a replacement is not the point.

M3. 'The epistemology of loss' means: (a) the fear of loss (b) the science of possession (c) the knowledge of what it is to lose something (d) the memory of childhood
    Ans: (c) the knowledge of what it is to lose something — the boy's first lesson in loss.

M4. Where does the boy's ball disappear? (a) Behind a classroom door (b) Into a tree (c) Under a bed (d) Into the water
    Ans: (d) Into the water. The boy watches the place where the ball is lost.

M5. What can a new ball not restore? (a) The memories linked to the lost ball (b) The shop's ability to sell toys (c) The boy's ability to play any game (d) The speaker's childhood
    Ans: (a) The memories linked to the lost ball. A replacement cannot return the particular experiences associated with the original.

M6. The boy's ball finally goes: (a) into a shop  (b) to another boy  (c) into a drain  (d) into the water
    Ans: (d) into the water. It bounces down the street and over into the harbour.

M7. According to the poet, money: (a) is external and cannot replace what is lost  (b) can buy anything  (c) should be saved  (d) is the cause of grief
    Ans: (a) is external and cannot replace what is lost. No one buys a ball back.

M8. ‘The Ball Poem’ is written in: (a) rhymed couplets (b) sonnet form (c) free verse (d) ballad form
    Ans: (c) free verse. Berryman uses no fixed rhyme or metre, letting the thought set the rhythm.
