A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] Why does the poet say he would not intrude on the boy?
    Ans: Because the boy is learning something important for the first time — the epistemology of loss, or the knowledge of what it is to lose something. Intruding on him, or offering a new ball, would spoil the lesson. The poet wants the boy to feel the loss fully and to learn to stand up on his own.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What does the poet mean by 'money is external'?
    Ans: He means that money can buy another ball, but it cannot buy back what has been lost or take away the feeling of loss. The material value of the ball is small — a dime — so what matters is not the object but the experience of losing it, which is something outside the reach of money.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] What does the boy lose in the poem, and what does the loss mean to him?
    Ans:
      He loses a ball that bounces merrily down the street and into the water.
      To an adult it is a small, cheap thing; to the boy it is the first thing he has owned and the first thing he has lost.
      He stands rigid, trembling, staring at the water where the ball went, with a helplessness the poet understands.
      The loss stands for the end of the innocent world of childhood, in which nothing is owned and nothing is lost.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] Why does the boy stand ‘rigid, trembling, staring down’? What has he understood for the first time?
    Ans: The boy stands rigid and trembling because his ball has bounced into the harbour and is gone beyond recovery. For the first time he understands loss — that what is possessed can vanish, that ‘balls will be lost always’, and that no money (‘a dime’) can buy back this particular ball with its memories. It is his first lesson in growing up.

Q9. [Long · 4 marks] Explain the ending: ‘he is learning … to stand up in the world of possessions'.
    Ans:
      The boy cannot be consoled with another ball, because the particular ball he lost cannot be replaced.
      The poet says he is learning, well behind his desperate eyes, the epistemology of loss: how to know and bear loss.
      Money is called ‘external' — it cannot buy back what is gone or undo the experience.
      So the boy is learning, through grief, to stand up among people who own and lose things: this is the painful education that makes an adult.

Q10. [Application · 4 marks] ‘Balls will be lost always.’ How does this line connect a lost ball to the larger losses of adult life?
    Ans:
      The line turns one small loss into a universal law: losing things — toys, chances, people, youth — is part of life.
      The boy's grief over a ball rehearses the grief adults feel over larger losses; the poem says we must each learn to ‘stand up’ and bear them.
      That is why the poet does not offer a new ball — sympathy cannot teach what only loss itself can teach.

Q11. [Long · 5 marks] What is the boy learning, according to the poet? Explain the significance of the first loss.
    Ans:
      The boy is learning the epistemology of loss — the knowledge of what loss means and how to live with it. He stands rigid and trembling, staring down at the harbour where his ball went, and in that moment he understands that some things, once gone, cannot be brought back.
      The poet says a dime, another ball, is worthless, because a new ball would not restore the one that is lost. Money is external, so it cannot help him here.
      The boy learns that balls will be lost always — loss is a permanent feature of life, and everyone must learn to accept it. He learns to stand up, which means to accept the loss and go on.
      The significance of the first loss is that it is the beginning of maturity. Until this moment the boy lived in a world where everything could be replaced; now he is part of a world of possessions in which things can be taken away. That realisation is painful, but it is also the first step towards becoming an adult who can bear responsibility and disappointment.

B. MCQs (8)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. 'The Ball Poem' is written by: (a) John Berryman (b) Robert Frost (c) Leslie Norris (d) Carolyn Wells
    Ans: (a) John Berryman.

M2. The ball cost: (a) a penny (b) a dime (c) a dollar (d) five cents
    Ans: (b) a dime — a small sum, which is why a replacement is not the point.

M3. 'The epistemology of loss' means: (a) the fear of loss (b) the knowledge of what it is to lose something (c) the science of possession (d) the memory of childhood
    Ans: (b) the knowledge of what it is to lose something — the boy's first lesson in loss.

M4. The most exam-ready way to revise “The Ball Poem” is: (a) read the heading only  (b) learn the key idea plus one example  (c) skip written practice  (d) memorise a random extra topic
    Ans: (b) learn the key idea plus one example. The chapter is John Berryman: a lost ball and the first lesson in loss.

M5. A 3-mark answer on “The Ball Poem” should: (a) be one sentence  (b) start with the idea, then a reason or method  (c) copy the whole chapter  (d) leave the example out
    Ans: (b) start with the idea, then a reason or method. Step marks follow that frame.

M6. The boy's ball finally goes: (a) into the water  (b) into a shop  (c) to another boy  (d) into a drain
    Ans: (a) into the water. It bounces down the street and over into the harbour.

M7. According to the poet, money: (a) is external and cannot replace what is lost  (b) can buy anything  (c) should be saved  (d) is the cause of grief
    Ans: (a) is external and cannot replace what is lost. No one buys a ball back.

M8. ‘The Ball Poem’ is written in: (a) free verse (b) rhymed couplets (c) sonnet form (d) ballad form
    Ans: (a) free verse. Berryman uses no fixed rhyme or metre, letting the thought set the rhythm.
