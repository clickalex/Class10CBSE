A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 3 marks] How does the poet contrast the tiger in the cage with the tiger in the wild?
    Ans: In the cage the tiger stalks only the few steps of his enclosure, in quiet rage, with his strength locked behind bars and his attention turned away from the visitors. In the wild he would lurk in shadow, slide through the long grass near the watering hole where plump deer pass, and snarl around houses at the jungle's edge, baring his white fangs and claws and terrorising the village. The contrast is between useless confinement and natural power.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] Explain the meaning of 'quiet rage'.
    Ans: It is an oxymoron. The tiger's rage is intense but silent, because in the cage there is nothing he can do about it. He cannot hunt, cannot roam and cannot express his anger, so the rage stays inside him and becomes quiet. The phrase captures the whole tragedy of captivity in two words.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] What is the significance of the last stanza?
    Ans: The tiger is locked in a concrete cell, his strength behind bars, and he stalks the length of his cage ignoring visitors. At night he stares with his brilliant eyes at the brilliant stars. The repetition of 'brilliant' links the tiger to the vast, free sky that he can only look at. The stanza shows that captivity has taken away everything except the longing for freedom.

Q4. [Application · 3 marks] A visitor says the caged tiger must be content because it does not attack the people watching it. Correct this reading with details from the poem.
    Ans:
      The tiger's lack of attack is not evidence of contentment; the poem shows it pacing the narrow space of the cage in “quiet rage”.
      In the forest it would stalk through long grass near the water hole and hunt, but in the zoo its strength is confined.
      The contrast makes the animal's restraint look like the result of captivity, not a free choice or a happy life.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] Imagine a zoo sign describes a tiger as “safe because it is behind bars”. Use the poem's contrast between cage and forest to explain what this sign leaves out.
    Ans:
      The bars protect visitors, but they also restrict the tiger to a few steps and keep its strength from being used naturally.
      The imagined forest gives the tiger space to stalk, hunt near the water hole and move around the jungle's edge.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] Contrast the tiger in the cage with the tiger in its natural habitat.
    Ans:
      In the cage he paces a few steps along the length of his cage, ignores visitors, looks at the brilliant stars and is described as being ‘locked in concrete cell'.
      His strength is behind bars; his voice is ‘the few steps of his cage' — reduced and silent.
      In the forest he would be lurking in shadow, sliding through long grass near the water hole, snarling around houses at the jungle's edge.
      There he would terrorise villages, show his white fangs and claws, and be free; the contrast is between helplessness and wild power.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Why does the caged tiger ‘stare with his brilliant eyes at the brilliant stars’? What do the stars mean to him?
    Ans: The stars are the only piece of the open sky left to the caged tiger — everything else is concrete and bars. He stares at them because they stand for the freedom he has lost: the night forest, the hunt, the vastness. The staring is not hope but ‘quiet rage’ — a proud animal reduced to looking at what he can never reach.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:

    A tiger paces the short length of a cage without reacting to the visitors. The poem then imagines him in long grass near a water hole, where deer pass, and later behind bars staring at the stars.

    (a) What does the tiger's pacing show about his life in the cage?
    (b) How is the imagined forest different?
    (c) What does the final image add to the contrast?
    Ans:
      His movement is restricted to a few steps; the “quiet rage” suggests frustrated strength rather than comfort.
      In the forest he could stalk and hunt in a natural habitat, with space and prey around him.
      The stars suggest the world beyond his concrete cell and underline the gap between the tiger's natural freedom and his confinement.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:

    In the poem, the tiger's strength is behind bars. Outside, the forest is imagined as a place of shadows, long grass and deer. At night the tiger looks beyond the concrete cell.

    (a) Identify the main contrast in these images.
    (b) What does the forest setting allow the tiger to do?
    (c) How does the night image develop the poem's criticism of captivity?
    Ans:
      The contrast is between restricted life in a human-made cage and the tiger's natural life in the forest.
      It gives him room to stalk and hunt; he is no longer limited to pacing a few steps.
      His gaze beyond the cell suggests awareness of a larger world he cannot reach, making the bars seem emotionally as well as physically restrictive.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] What does ‘A Tiger in the Zoo' say about freedom and captivity?
    Ans:
      The poem shows that a wild animal in a zoo is not living its life, only existing within a few feet of concrete.
      Freedom is not a luxury for such a creature: it is the condition in which its strength, hunting and instincts have any meaning.
      The tiger's quiet rage and his staring at the stars suggest an awareness of what he has lost.
      By ending with the tiger asleep behind bars while the forest world goes on, Norris quietly criticises the human habit of caging wild animals for amusement.

Q11. [Application · 4 marks] The poet repeats the word ‘brilliant’ for both the tiger's eyes and the stars. What link is he building between them?
    Ans:
      The repeated ‘brilliant’ links the tiger and the stars as two bright, wild, untamed things.
      It suggests the tiger belongs with the stars — in the open night — not in a concrete cell.
      At the same time the link is painful: both shine, but the cage keeps them forever apart. The single repeated word thus carries the poem's whole protest against captivity.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] How does Leslie Norris use movement and setting to make the tiger's captivity feel unjust?
    Ans:
      In the zoo the tiger can only stalk the few steps of his cage; his power has been reduced to repetitive movement behind bars.
      The phrase “quiet rage” captures anger that cannot be acted on. He ignores the visitors rather than performing for them.
      The imagined forest reverses this restriction: shadows, long grass and a water hole give him room to move and hunt as a wild animal.
      Finally, the tiger stares from the concrete cell toward the stars. The image widens the contrast between the confined body and the freedom of the world outside.

B. MCQs (8)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. 'A Tiger in the Zoo' is written by: (a) Robert Frost (b) Vicky Tree (c) Leslie Norris (d) Carolyn Wells
    Ans: (c) Leslie Norris.

M2. In the wild, the tiger would be found near the: (a) village well (b) river bank (c) forest edge only (d) watering hole
    Ans: (d) the watering hole, where plump deer pass.

M3. 'Quiet rage' is an example of: (a) simile (b) oxymoron (c) metaphor (d) alliteration
    Ans: (b) oxymoron — two contradictory words placed together.

M4. What best explains the tiger's “quiet rage”? (a) He is preparing to entertain visitors (b) He is resting after a hunt (c) His strength is confined in the cage (d) He has no interest in the forest
    Ans: (c) His strength is confined in the cage. The tiger's restricted movement is the source of his frustration.

M5. Which activity belongs to the tiger's imagined life in the forest? (a) Pacing a concrete cell (b) Stalking through long grass (c) Performing for visitors (d) Sleeping beside the bars
    Ans: (b) Stalking through long grass. The forest scene contrasts natural movement with the short steps of the cage.

M6. The tiger in the zoo is described as stalking the length of his: (a) cage  (b) jungle  (c) river bank  (d) cave
    Ans: (a) cage. He can take only a few steps, which underlines his captivity.

M7. The poet of ‘A Tiger in the Zoo' is: (a) Robert Frost  (b) Carolyn Wells  (c) John Berryman  (d) Leslie Norris
    Ans: (d) Leslie Norris. The Welsh poet wrote it after seeing a tiger in Regent's Park Zoo.

M8. In the wild, the tiger should be snarling around houses at: (a) the jungle's edge (b) the city centre (c) the zoo gates (d) the river dam
    Ans: (a) the jungle's edge. There he would terrorise the village — the poem's image of his natural power.
