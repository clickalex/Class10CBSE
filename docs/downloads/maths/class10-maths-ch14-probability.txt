A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (13)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 1 mark] A card is drawn from a well-shuffled deck. What is the probability that it is a red king?
    Ans: There are 2 red kings, so P = 2/52 = 1/26.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] A die is thrown once. Find the probability of getting (i) a prime number, (ii) a number greater than 4.
    Ans:
      Primes on a die are 2, 3, 5, so P = 3/6 = 1/2.
      Numbers greater than 4 are 5 and 6, so P = 2/6 = 1/3.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] A bag contains 5 red and 3 green balls. One ball is drawn at random. Find the probability that it is not red.
    Ans: Total outcomes = 5 + 3 = 8. Not red means green, so P(not red) = 3/8. Equivalently, using the complement rule: P(not red) = 1 − P(red) = 1 − 5/8 = 3/8. The complement route is quicker when the event you are asked about has more outcomes than the one you are not.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] A bag contains 4 red, 5 blue and 3 green balls. Find the probability of drawing a ball that is not green.
    Ans: Total balls = 12. Balls that are not green = 4 + 5 = 9. So P(not green) = 9/12 = 3/4.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] Two dice are thrown together. Find the probability that the sum is 9.
    Ans:
      Total outcomes = 36.
      Favourable pairs: (3,6), (4,5), (5,4), (6,3) — 4 outcomes.
      P = 4/36 = 1/9.

Q6. [Short · 3 marks] A card is drawn at random from a well-shuffled deck. Find the probability that it is (i) a face card, (ii) neither a king nor a red card.
    Ans:
      (i) There are 12 face cards, so P = 12/52 = 3/13.
      (ii) Red cards are 26, of which 2 are kings. Neither a king nor red means a black card that is not a king: 26 − 2 = 24.
      P = 24/52 = 6/13.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] Riya is revising “Probability” (Classical probability, complementary events, dice, cards and coins). A classmate lost marks by doing this: Forgetting that a die has 6 outcomes, not 6 faces times something — and that two dice have 36, not 12. Apply the correct method and rewrite the scoring answer.
    Ans:
      Correct method: Always write the total number of outcomes first. Half of all probability errors are wrong totals, not wrong favourable counts.
      Why the mistake costs marks: Forgetting that a die has 6 outcomes, not 6 faces times something — and that two dice have 36, not 12.
      Write the definition or formula first, then the working or example.

Q8. [Application · 3 marks] Amit is given this exam-style task from “Probability”: “A card is drawn from a well-shuffled deck. What is the probability that it is a red king?” Treat it as an application question. Identify the idea, then write the working in points.
    Ans:
      Idea: Classical probability, complementary events, dice, cards and coins.
      There are 2 red kings, so P = 2/52 = 1/26.

Q9. [Short · 3 marks] Two coins are tossed together. Find the probability of getting (i) two heads, (ii) at least one tail, (iii) at most one head.
    Ans:
      Sample space = {HH, HT, TH, TT}: 4 equally likely outcomes.
      (i) Two heads: only HH, so P = 1/4.
      (ii) At least one tail: HT, TH, TT, so P = 3/4.
      (iii) At most one head: HT, TH, TT, so P = 3/4.

Q12. [Application · 4 marks] A box contains 90 discs numbered 1 to 90. One disc is drawn at random. Find the probability that it bears (i) a two-digit number, (ii) a number divisible by 5, (iii) a perfect square number.
    Ans:
      (i) Two-digit numbers are 10 to 90, i.e. 81 discs, so P = 81/90 = 9/10.
      (ii) Multiples of 5 from 5 to 90: 18 numbers, so P = 18/90 = 1/5.
      (iii) Perfect squares: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81 — nine numbers, so P = 9/90 = 1/10.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] Discuss “Probability” in detail. Include the idea that carries marks, one worked example or answer frame, and why students lose marks.
    Ans:
      Classical definition. P(E) = (number of outcomes favourable to E) / (total number of possible outcomes), assuming all outcomes are equally likely.
      Marks lens: The remaining 3–4 marks of the 11-mark unit.
      Method / memory trick: Always write the total number of outcomes first. Half of all probability errors are wrong totals, not wrong favourable counts.
      Do not: Forgetting that a die has 6 outcomes, not 6 faces times something — and that two dice have 36, not 12.

B. MCQs (8)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. The probability of an impossible event is: (a) 1  (b) 0  (c) 1/2  (d) undefined
    Ans: (b) 0.

M2. Two dice are thrown together. The total number of outcomes is: (a) 6  (b) 12  (c) 36  (d) 24
    Ans: (c) 36.

M3. The number of face cards in a deck of 52 cards is: (a) 8  (b) 12  (c) 16  (d) 4
    Ans: (b) 12. Jacks, queens and kings of four suits.

M4. The most exam-ready way to revise “Probability” is: (a) read the heading only  (b) learn the key idea plus one example  (c) skip written practice  (d) memorise a random extra topic
    Ans: (b) learn the key idea plus one example. The chapter is Classical probability, complementary events, dice, cards and coins.

M5. A 3-mark answer on “Probability” should: (a) be one sentence  (b) start with the idea, then a reason or method  (c) copy the whole chapter  (d) leave the example out
    Ans: (b) start with the idea, then a reason or method. Step marks follow that frame.

M6. In one throw of a die, the probability of getting a number less than 7 is: (a) 0  (b) 1/6  (c) 5/6  (d) 1
    Ans: (d) 1. Every outcome 1–6 is less than 7, so it is a sure event.

M7. If P(E) = 0.37, then P(not E) = : (a) 0.37  (b) 0.63  (c) 1  (d) 0.53
    Ans: (b) 0.63. P(not E) = 1 − P(E) = 1 − 0.37 = 0.63.

M8. One card is drawn from a well-shuffled deck. The probability of a black queen is: (a) 1/52  (b) 1/26  (c) 1/13  (d) 2/13
    Ans: (b) 1/26. Two black queens out of 52 cards gives 2/52 = 1/26.
