CHAPTER 1 · The Rise of Nationalism in Europe
Unit: History · India and the Contemporary World II
========================================================================

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (13)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] Who were the Jacobins and what did they do during the French Revolution?
    Ans: The Jacobins were members of a political club led by Maximilian Robespierre. They overthrew the monarchy, declared France a republic, and gave the nation its symbols — the tricolour flag, the red cap (liberty cap) and the new national anthem, the Marseillaise — through which people could identify with the nation.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] Interpret the significance of the allegory of Marianne and Germania.
    Ans: Both were female allegories used to give the abstract idea of the nation a concrete visual form. Marianne, with her red cap, tricolour and cockade, represented the ideals of liberty and the French Republic, and statues of her were installed in public squares to remind citizens of national unity. Germania, wearing a crown of oak leaves, symbolised heroism and the German nation. Allegories made it easy for people to identify with the nation.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] Briefly trace the process of German unification.
    Ans: Middle-class Germans tried to unite the German Confederation through a parliament elected in 1848, but the Frankfurt assembly failed when Friedrich Wilhelm IV rejected the offered crown. Prussia then took over the leadership, and its Chief Minister Otto von Bismarck used the army and the bureaucracy to fight three wars over seven years against Austria, Denmark and France. Prussian victories ended in the proclamation of the German Empire at Versailles in January 1871, with Kaiser Wilhelm I as emperor.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] What steps did the French revolutionaries take to create a sense of collective identity among the French people?
    Ans: They introduced the ideas of la patrie (the fatherland) and le citoyen (the citizen), emphasising a united community with equal rights under a constitution. A new tricolour replaced the royal standard, the Estates General was renamed the National Assembly and elected by active citizens, a uniform system of weights and measures was adopted, internal customs duties were abolished, and French as spoken in Paris became the common language. Oaths of loyalty to the constitution and commemoration of martyrs were also used.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] How did culture play an important role in creating the idea of the nation in Europe?
    Ans: Romantic artists and poets criticised the glorification of reason and science and turned to emotion, intuition and mysticism, using folk songs, folk poetry and folk dances to reach the people. Language too was used as a weapon of national resistance — in Poland, for example, the use of Polish in schools and church gatherings became a symbol of struggle against Russian dominance. Operas and music carried nationalist feelings to a largely illiterate audience.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes: “The Frankfurt Parliament of 1848 proclaimed the German Empire under Kaiser William I.” Correct the statement.
    Ans:
      In 1848 the Frankfurt Parliament, meeting in the Church of St Paul, drafted a constitution for a German nation and offered the crown to King Frederick William IV of Prussia, who rejected it.
      The German Empire was proclaimed in January 1871, at Versailles, after Prussia defeated France.
      William I of Prussia became the German Emperor; the process was led by the Prussian chief minister, Otto von Bismarck, using the army, not by an elected assembly.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] What was the main aim of the Treaty of Vienna (1815)? State two arrangements it made.
    Ans:
      Its aim was to undo most of the changes Napoleon had made and to restore the old monarchies, the conservative order of Europe.
      The Bourbon dynasty was restored to France, and states were set up on France’s boundaries (such as the Kingdom of the Netherlands, including Belgium) to prevent French expansion.
      Prussia received new territories on its western frontier, and Austria received control of northern Italy.

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] Describe the condition of the Balkans in the nineteenth century and why the region caused tension in Europe.
    Ans:
      The Balkans was a region of great ethnic and religious diversity that had been part of the Ottoman Empire.
      As Ottoman power weakened, the Slavic peoples demanded independence, and the Balkan states fought each other over territory.
      Big powers such as Russia, Austria-Hungary, Britain and Germany each wanted influence over the region. These rivalries made the Balkans the powder keg of Europe and helped trigger the First World War in 1914.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Women had formed political associations, started newspapers and joined political meetings in the 1840s. Yet when the Frankfurt Parliament met in 1848, they were allowed in only as observers, standing in the visitors’ gallery.
    
    (a) In which church did the Frankfurt Parliament meet?
    (b) What does the women’s position show about the right to vote at that time?
    (c) What conclusion can be drawn about liberal nationalism in 1848?
    Ans:
      (a) The Church of St Paul in Frankfurt.
      (b) Women had been denied suffrage; the right to vote was reserved for men.
      (c) Liberal nationalism demanded a constitution and representation but was limited: it excluded women (and the propertyless), so it was not fully democratic.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Greece had been part of the Ottoman Empire since the fifteenth century. Greek nationalists abroad and many West Europeans who admired ancient Greek culture supported the fight for independence.
    
    (a) Which treaty of 1832 recognised Greece as an independent nation?
    (b) Name an English poet who helped the Greek cause.
    (c) What does this show about the role of culture in nationalism?
    Ans:
      (a) The Treaty of Constantinople.
      (b) Lord Byron, who raised funds and went to fight in the war; he died of fever in 1824.
      (c) Poets, artists and writers can arouse sympathy and public opinion; admiration for Greece as the cradle of European culture helped a political cause.

Q11. [Long · 4 marks] Explain how the French Revolution spread the idea of nationalism across Europe.
    Ans:
      The revolution transferred sovereignty from the monarch to the nation: the people, not the king, were declared the source of power.
      It gave the French people common symbols — the tricolour, the national anthem and a new calendar — and introduced a uniform administrative system with equal laws and one currency.
      Napoleon's armies carried these ideas into the territories they conquered; although his rule was resented, the idea that people sharing a common language and history should form one nation took root.
      This idea shaped the unification movements of Italy and Germany and the struggles of the Balkan peoples in the nineteenth century.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Write a note on the role of Giuseppe Mazzini, Count Cavour and Giuseppe Garibaldi in the unification of Italy.
    Ans:
      Giuseppe Mazzini — a member of the secret society Carbonari, he founded Young Italy in Marseilles to work for a unitary Italian republic. He provided the ideological inspiration and the programme.
      Count Cavour — Chief Minister of Sardinia-Piedmont, he led the unification movement through diplomacy rather than revolution, and with the help of France and the army defeated Austria in 1859.
      Giuseppe Garibaldi — he led the armed volunteers known as the Red Shirts, marched into the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies in 1860, won the support of the local peasants and drove out the Spanish rulers.
      In 1861 Victor Emmanuel II was proclaimed king of united Italy, with Cavour as his Chief Minister. Much of the peasant population, however, knew nothing of the ideals of liberal nationalism.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] Describe five administrative changes introduced by Napoleon in the territories under his control. Why did people eventually become unhappy with his rule?
    Ans:
      He introduced the Civil Code of 1804 (Napoleonic Code): it abolished privileges based on birth, established equality before the law and secured the right to property.
      He simplified administrative divisions and abolished the feudal system, freeing peasants from serfdom and manorial dues.
      He removed guild restrictions in towns and improved transport and communication.
      Uniform weights and measures and a common national currency made trade and the movement of goods easier.
      At first people welcomed these reforms as liberation, but later they were disillusioned by higher taxes, censorship and forced conscription into the French armies.

    Ans: (c) — it abolished tariff barriers, reduced the number of currencies from over thirty to two, and helped bind the Germans economically.

    Ans: (d) Kaiser Wilhelm I of Prussia, at Versailles in January 1871, with Bismarck as the architect of the process.

    Ans: (a) Germania — oak leaves stand for heroism.

M4. The Treaty of Constantinople of 1832 recognised the independence of: (a) Greece (b) Belgium (c) Italy (d) Poland
    Ans: (a) Greece. After a long struggle against the Ottoman Empire, Greece was recognised as an independent nation in 1832.

M5. The chief minister of Prussia who led the unification of Germany was: (a) Count Cavour (b) Prince Metternich (c) Giuseppe Garibaldi (d) Otto von Bismarck
    Ans: (d) Otto von Bismarck. Bismarck used the army and the bureaucracy in wars against Denmark, Austria and France, leading to the German Empire of 1871.

M6. The Treaty of Vienna was signed in: (a) 1789  (b) 1815  (c) 1848  (d) 1871
    Ans: (b) 1815. Its aim was to undo the changes Napoleon had made and to restore the old monarchies of Europe.

M7. Who is supposed to have said, ‘when France sneezes, the rest of Europe catches cold'? (a) Bismarck  (b) Metternich  (c) Mazzini  (d) Napoleon
    Ans: (b) Metternich. Austria's chief minister, who represented the conservative order established at Vienna.

M8. Italy was unified under: (a) Garibaldi alone  (b) Mazzini's Young Italy alone  (c) Cavour and Victor Emmanuel II, helped by Garibaldi's Red Shirts  (d) Bismarck's armies
    Ans: (c) Cavour and Victor Emmanuel II, helped by Garibaldi's Red Shirts. Diplomacy and popular revolt together completed unification by 1870.

CHAPTER 2 · Nationalism in India
Unit: History · India and the Contemporary World II
========================================================================

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] What was the Rowlatt Act of 1919 and why was it opposed?
    Ans: It allowed the colonial government to imprison any person suspected of sedition without trial for up to two years. Indians saw it as a betrayal after the war and Gandhiji called for a hartal on 6 April 1919 against what he called the ‘black act'.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] Why was the Salt March an effective symbol of resistance against colonialism?
    Ans: Salt was consumed by rich and poor alike and was one of the most essential items of food, yet the tax on it and the government monopoly over its production revealed the most oppressive face of British rule. Choosing it united the whole country behind a single demand, and breaking the salt law by boiling sea water at Dandi was simple, visible and easy for every villager to repeat, so it quickly spread into a nationwide movement.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] Describe the Jallianwala Bagh incident.
    Ans: On 13 April 1919, a large crowd gathered in the enclosed ground of Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar — some to protest against the repressive Rowlatt Act, others to attend the annual Baisakhi fair. Unaware of the martial law imposed in the city, they were trapped when General Dyer entered, blocked the exit points and opened fire on the unarmed crowd, killing hundreds. His object, as he later declared, was to produce a moral effect and create terror in the minds of satyagrahis. News of the massacre spread, and crowds took to the streets in many north Indian towns.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] Why did Gandhiji decide to withdraw the Non-Cooperation Movement in February 1922?
    Ans: Because the movement was turning violent. At Chauri Chaura in Gorakhpur, a peaceful demonstration turned into a violent clash and the crowd burnt a police station with policemen inside. Gandhi felt that satyagrahis needed proper training before they could take part in a mass struggle, and that violence would give the British an excuse to crush the movement, so he called it off.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes that the Salt March began in 1920 and that Gandhiji ended the Non-Cooperation Movement because of the salt tax. Correct both statements.
    Ans:
      The Salt (Dandi) March began on 12 March 1930, from Sabarmati Ashram, and reached Dandi on 6 April, where Gandhiji broke the salt law; it started the Civil Disobedience Movement.
      The Non-Cooperation Movement was begun at the end of 1920 and was withdrawn in February 1922, after the violent incident at Chauri Chaura, where a mob set a police station on fire and killed policemen.
      The salt tax had nothing to do with calling off Non-Cooperation.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] How did the peasants of Awadh and the tribal people of the Gudem Hills each understand Gandhiji’s call for swaraj?
    Ans:
      Awadh: led by Baba Ramchandra, peasants fought talukdars and landlords who demanded high rents and forced labour (begar). For them swaraj meant lower revenue, an end to begar and a social boycott of oppressive landlords.
      Gudem Hills: tribal people, led by Alluri Sitaram Raju, were angered by colonial rules that restricted entry into the forests. For them swaraj meant the freedom to move freely in the forests and use forest produce.
      In both cases people gave the call their own local meaning, and some, like Raju, took to militant methods that Gandhiji did not approve.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Describe the participation of women and students in the national movement.
    Ans:
      Women joined processions, picketed shops selling liquor and foreign cloth, and went to jail; several, such as Sarojini Naidu, became national leaders.
      Students left government schools and colleges during the Non-Cooperation Movement and joined national schools and colleges.
      Their participation widened the social base of the movement, turning it from a middle-class agitation into a mass struggle.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    In December 1929, the Congress session at Lahore, presided over by Jawaharlal Nehru, took a major decision. Earlier, the Simon Commission, which had no Indian member, had been greeted with “Go back, Simon!” slogans.
    
    (a) What goal did the Lahore session adopt?
    (b) On which date was “Independence Day” to be observed?
    (c) Why did the Congress turn more radical after the Simon Commission?
    Ans:
      (a) Purna Swaraj — complete independence from British rule.
      (b) 26 January 1930.
      (c) The Commission, set up to review the constitutional system, had no Indian members, so it was boycotted; the Viceroy’s vague offer of dominion status satisfied no one, and the demand moved to full independence.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    During the Civil Disobedience Movement, Dr B. R. Ambedkar and Mahatma Gandhi disagreed over how the “depressed classes” should be represented.
    
    (a) What did Dr Ambedkar demand?
    (b) Why did Gandhiji oppose it, and what did he do?
    (c) What was the outcome in September 1932?
    Ans:
      (a) Separate electorates for the depressed classes.
      (b) Gandhiji believed separate electorates would slow their integration into society, and he began a fast unto death.
      (c) The Poona Pact: Ambedkar accepted reserved seats for the depressed classes in the provincial and central councils, to be voted for by the general electorate.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] Describe the course of the Civil Disobedience Movement and explain why it was withdrawn.
    Ans:
      It began with the Salt March: on 12 March 1930 Gandhiji set out from Sabarmati with 78 followers and broke the salt law at Dandi on 6 April.
      People refused to pay taxes, boycotted foreign cloth and liquor, and peasants and tribals agitated against forest laws and land revenue.
      Gandhiji suspended it after the Gandhi-Irwin Pact of March 1931, revived it in 1932, and finally withdrew it in April 1934 when mass support had faded and the government had crushed the movement.

Q11. [Long · 5 marks] How did the Non-Cooperation Movement differ from the Civil Disobedience Movement?
    Ans:
      Non-Cooperation (1920–22): people were asked to refuse all cooperation with the British government — students left schools, lawyers gave up practice, foreign cloth was boycotted and burnt. It was launched with the Khilafat issue and withdrawn by Gandhi after the Chauri Chaura violence.
      Civil Disobedience (1930–34): people were asked not merely to refuse cooperation but to break colonial laws — the salt law, forest laws, refusal to pay revenue and chaukidari taxes, boycott of foreign cloth and picketing of liquor shops.
      Participation: Civil Disobedience saw much larger participation by women, who left their homes to join protest marches and manufacture salt.
      Outcome: Non-Cooperation was withdrawn in 1922; Civil Disobedience was suspended by the Gandhi-Irwin Pact in 1931, revived after the failed Round Table Conference, and finally lost momentum by 1934.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Explain the idea of the Sense of Collective Belonging with reference to nationalism in India.
    Ans:
      Nationalism develops when people begin to believe that they are part of the same nation, and this feeling is created through history and fiction, folklore and songs, and popular prints.
      Images: Bharat Mata, first created by Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay and painted by Abhinindranath Tagore as a calm, divine ascetic figure, came to symbolise the nation; devotion to her became a sign of nationalism.
      Songs: Vande Mataram, written by Bankim Chandra in the 1870s and set in Anandamath, was widely sung during the Swadeshi movement.
      Folklore: in Bengal, Natesan Sastri and others collected folk tales and songs, arguing that these were the truest manifestation of national identity.
      Flags: the Swadeshi flag of 1905 and the Swaraj flag of 1921, with the spinning wheel, became symbols of defiance; carrying them was itself an act of rebellion.
      History: Indians began writing of India's ancient achievements in mathematics, science, law and philosophy, to counter the colonial view of Indians as backward and to instil pride in the past.

CHAPTER 3 · The Making of a Global World
Unit: History · India and the Contemporary World II
========================================================================

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] What was the Corn Laws controversy in Britain?
    Ans: The Corn Laws restricted the import of cheap corn into Britain, keeping bread prices high to protect landowners. Workers and industrialists demanded their repeal; after repeal in 1846, food flowed in from the colonies and eastern Europe, bread became cheaper, but many European peasants were forced to migrate to the Americas.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What were the Silk Routes and what did they carry besides goods?
    Ans: The Silk Routes were a network of pre-modern trade and cultural routes linking Asia with Europe and northern Africa. Besides goods such as Chinese silk, Indian cotton textiles and spices going west and silver going east, they carried religions — Buddhism, and later Christianity and Islam — and also carried diseases, since germs travelled with the traders and pilgrims.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] Explain how the Columbian exchange changed diets in the Old World, giving examples.
    Ans: Before the discovery of America, foods such as potatoes, soya, groundnuts, maize, tomatoes, chillies and sweet potatoes were unknown in Europe and Asia. These American crops reached the Old World after Columbus's voyages and changed what people ate. The poor of Europe, who had earlier eaten mostly bread and porridge, now had a more varied and abundant diet, and in Ireland the potato became the staple of the poorest sections.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] Why did the Spanish conquest of America succeed so easily?
    Ans: Mainly because of disease. The Spanish conquerors carried smallpox germs, and the original inhabitants of America had no immunity to them because of their long isolation from the rest of the world. Smallpox spread and killed huge numbers, decimating whole communities, so conquest became easy. Guns could be bought or fought against, but disease could not.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] Describe the role of silver from Peru and Mexico in the global economy of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.
    Ans: The discovery of rich silver mines in Peru and Mexico made silver available in huge quantities. It was shipped to Europe and from there used to finance Europe's wealth and its expanding trade with Asia. Silver thus became the link that connected the Americas, Europe and Asia in a single flow of money and goods.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes that the potato originated in Ireland and that the Irish famine happened because the potato is a poor crop. Correct this.
    Ans:
      The potato came from the Americas and reached Europe after Columbus’s voyages in the sixteenth century.
      It gave poor Europeans cheap, nutritious food, so they ate better and lived longer.
      Poor peasants in Ireland came to depend on the potato so heavily that when a disease destroyed the crop in the mid-1840s, hundreds of thousands starved. The problem was the over-dependence, not that the potato itself was poor.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] Trace how (i) cowrie shells, (ii) noodles and (iii) Buddhism travelled between regions in the pre-modern world.
    Ans:
      (i) Cowries were carried from the Maldives to China and to East Africa and were used as a form of money.
      (ii) Noodles are believed to have travelled from China to the West, where they became spaghetti in Italy (Arab traders also carried pasta to Sicily).
      (iii) Buddhism emerged in eastern India and spread to Central Asia, China, Korea and Japan along the Silk Routes.

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] Explain what indentured labour was and its effects in the nineteenth century.
    Ans:
      After slavery was abolished, plantations in the Caribbean, Fiji, Mauritius, Sri Lanka and Natal needed cheap labour.
      Millions of workers were recruited from India, China and Africa on contracts (girmit) for a fixed number of years, often after being misled about the terms.
      They were carried in crowded ships and worked in harsh conditions, but they also carried Indian languages, festivals and food abroad.
      Indentured migration from India was finally abolished in 1917 after the nationalist movement raised the issue.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Before Columbus, European markets had no tomatoes, potatoes, maize or chillies. A few centuries later, these foods were common in many parts of the world.
    
    (a) Where did these foods come from?
    (b) How did some of them help poor people in Europe?
    (c) How did the arrival of Europeans harm the original inhabitants of the Americas?
    Ans:
      (a) From the Americas.
      (b) The potato and maize gave cheap, nourishing food, so the poor ate better and lived longer.
      (c) The conquerors brought diseases such as smallpox, to which the local people had no immunity, killing millions; they also took over lands and forced people to work.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    The legend of El Dorado, a land of gold, drew many Europeans to the Americas. In the sixteenth century, silver mined in Peru and Mexico began to reach Europe.
    
    (a) What was El Dorado?
    (b) How did American silver affect Europe?
    (c) How did it affect Europe’s trade with Asia?
    Ans:
      (a) A legendary city or land of gold that Portuguese and Spanish explorers hoped to find.
      (b) Silver from the mines of Peru and Mexico added to Europe’s wealth.
      (c) It financed Europe’s trade with Asia, since Europe paid for Asian goods such as textiles and spices largely in silver.

Q11. [Long · 4 marks] Explain how the First World War and the Great Depression changed the world economy.
    Ans:
      The First World War turned Europe from a creditor into a debtor and made the United States the world's main lender.
      The Wall Street Crash of 1929 and the Depression of the 1930s collapsed world trade, output and employment; agricultural prices and farm incomes fell sharply, hitting colonised countries hardest.
      Countries raised tariffs and formed imperial preference blocs, and migration and lending fell.
      The crisis destroyed confidence in unregulated markets and led to state intervention — for example Roosevelt's New Deal — and to a demand for decolonisation.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Describe the role of the Silk Routes in the pre-modern world, with examples of what travelled along them.
    Ans:
      The Silk Routes were a network of land and sea routes linking Asia with Europe and northern Africa; they were named after the Chinese silk carried along them.
      Goods that travelled: Chinese pottery, textiles and spices from India, and precious metals going from Europe to Asia.
      Ideas and religions travelled too: Christian missionaries, Muslim preachers and Buddhists took their faiths along these routes; Buddhism spread from India to Central Asia, China, Korea and Japan.
      Food and technology also moved: noodles are believed to have travelled from China to Italy.
      Thus the routes connected distant regions in trade and culture long before the modern age.

    Ans: (d) potato and maize — along with tomatoes, chillies, soya, groundnuts and sweet potatoes.

M2. The disease that most helped the Spanish conquer America was: (a) cholera (b) influenza (c) plague (d) smallpox
    Ans: (d) smallpox — the native population had no immunity to it because of long isolation.

    Ans: (b) the Silk Routes, named after Chinese silk which travelled along them.

    Ans: (c) Chinese silk that was carried along them. Chinese silk was a prized item carried westward along the land and sea routes.

M5. Cowrie shells, used as currency, were carried from the Maldives to: (a) Peru and Mexico (b) Britain and France (c) China and East Africa (d) Australia
    Ans: (c) China and East Africa. Cowries were widely used as money, and were carried from the Maldives to China and East Africa.

M6. Indentured labourers were recruited mainly from: (a) India, China and Africa  (b) Africa and Japan  (c) Australia and Europe  (d) North America
    Ans: (a) India, China and Africa. They worked on plantations across the tropical colonies after slavery was abolished.

M7. The Bretton Woods institutions are: (a) the IMF and the World Bank  (b) the WTO and UNICEF  (c) the League of Nations and the UN  (d) UNESCO and WHO
    Ans: (a) the IMF and the World Bank. They were set up in 1944 to manage the post-war international economy.

M8. The Great Depression of the 1930s began with: (a) the First World War  (b) the Wall Street Crash of 1929  (c) the oil shock  (d) the Russian Revolution
    Ans: (b) the Wall Street Crash of 1929. It spread to the rest of the world through trade and finance.

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (13)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] What were the penny dreadfuls and what do they tell us about the readers of print?
    Ans: Penny dreadfuls were cheap serial novels sold in Victorian Britain for a penny. They show that print had begun to reach working-class readers, that publishers were producing for a mass market rather than for the elite, and that the new readers wanted exciting, escapist stories.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] How did Gutenberg develop the printing press?
    Ans: Gutenberg grew up on a large agricultural estate and from childhood had seen wine and olive presses. By the 1430s he had perfected the printing press by adapting the olive press, and he developed a metal mould for casting types. His press could print far more copies than hand copying — his first book, the Bible, was printed in about three years, producing 180 copies.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] Why did Martin Luther's ideas spread so rapidly across Europe?
    Ans: Because his writings were printed. When he posted his Ninety-Five Theses on the church door at Wittenberg in 1517, printers reproduced them in vast numbers within days, and they were read widely and discussed everywhere. His German translation of the New Testament sold 5,000 copies within a few weeks — something impossible in a manuscript culture.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] Why were manuscripts not widely used in everyday life in pre-colonial India?
    Ans: Because they were written on palm leaf or fragile handmade paper, were expensive to produce, and could not be handled or read easily. They were also copied by hand, so copies were few and easily damaged, and the script was written in a variety of styles that made reading difficult. Knowledge was therefore largely transmitted orally — texts were recited and memorised rather than read.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] What was the Vernacular Press Act of 1878?
    Ans: It was a law modelled on Irish press laws that gave the government extensive powers to censor reports and editorials in the vernacular press. If a newspaper published anything judged seditious and did not heed an initial warning, its press could be confiscated, including its printing machinery. It was widely resented and became a symbol of colonial repression of the Indian press.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes: “The printing press was invented in India by the British in the nineteenth century.” Correct the statement.
    Ans:
      Printing technology came from China and Japan (woodblock printing); Marco Polo brought knowledge of it to Italy in 1295.
      Johann Gutenberg developed the modern printing press in Strasbourg, Germany, in the 1430s.
      The press first came to India with Portuguese missionaries in Goa in the mid-sixteenth century, long before the British press.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] What was the Index of Prohibited Books, and what happened to the Italian miller Menocchio?
    Ans:
      From 1558, the Roman Catholic Church kept an Index of Prohibited Books and controlled printers and booksellers, because print was spreading ideas it did not accept.
      Menocchio, a miller, read the books available to him and formed his own interpretation of the Bible and the nature of God.
      The Church charged him with heresy and he was executed. His case shows both the power of print to spread ideas and the Church’s effort to control it.

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] Describe how print came to India and the role it played in social reform.
    Ans:
      Printing presses were first set up in Goa by Christian missionaries in the mid-sixteenth century; from the 1780s Bengali and other vernacular presses multiplied.
      Reformers used print: Raja Rammohun Roy published tracts against sati and idolatry, and Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar wrote in favour of widow remarriage.
      Women read and wrote in the popular Bengali almanacs and domestic magazines, and public libraries and cheap books carried these debates to ordinary readers.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Rashsundari Debi, a young married woman in an orthodox household in Bengal, taught herself to read in secret in her kitchen. In 1876 she published her life story.
    
    (a) Name her book.
    (b) Why is it historically important?
    (c) Why did she have to learn in secret?
    Ans:
      (a) Amar Jiban.
      (b) It was the first full-length autobiography published in the Bengali language.
      (c) Many families strongly opposed education for women, so she learned secretly in the kitchen.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    In 1907, when revolutionaries in Punjab were deported, Bal Gangadhar Tilak wrote with great sympathy about them in his newspaper.
    
    (a) Name the newspaper.
    (b) What happened to Tilak in 1908?
    (c) What does the episode show about the press and nationalism?
    Ans:
      (a) Kesari.
      (b) He was imprisoned, which provoked widespread protests all over India.
      (c) Nationalist newspapers openly criticised colonial rule and roused public feeling; the government in turn tried to punish and control them.

Q11. [Long · 4 marks] Explain why colonial governments tried to control the press and how nationalist newspapers responded.
    Ans:
      The colonial state feared that criticism in the vernacular press would spread sedition and unite people against British rule.
      It used measures such as the Vernacular Press Act of 1878, which allowed it to confiscate the assets of newspapers that published objectionable material, and it monitored Indian-owned presses.
      Nationalist papers such as Kesari (Tilak), Amrita Bazar Patrika and The Hindu evaded these controls, reported official repression, and used cartoons, ballads and vernacular prose to reach ordinary readers.
      Print thus created a public opinion outside the government's control and became a major force in the freedom struggle.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] How did print culture help the growth of nationalism in India?
    Ans:
      Newspapers carried nationalist criticism. Vernacular newspapers such as Tilak's Kesari and Maratha reported colonial misrule and supported the Punjab revolutionaries; Tilak's imprisonment in 1908 provoked widespread protest.
      Religious and social reform debates were printed. Raja Rammohun Roy's Sambad Kaumudi, the Mirat-ul-Akhbar, and the fatwas of the Deoband seminary spread reformist and religious ideas to a wide readership.
      Cheap books and libraries made knowledge available to the poor and to workers, and novels and pamphlets carried political ideas in an easily readable form.
      Writings by and about women — Tarabai Shinde's Stripurushtulna, Pandita Ramabai on widowhood — created a new public debate on social questions.
      Censorship itself proved the point. The Vernacular Press Act of 1878, which allowed the government to seize presses, showed how powerful print had become, and made the press a recognised weapon of the national movement.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] Explain how print culture created the conditions for the French Revolution.
    Ans:
      Print popularised the ideas of Enlightenment thinkers such as Voltaire and Rousseau, who criticised tradition, superstition and despotism and stressed reason.
      Print created a new culture of dialogue and debate; people began to question old values and norms and to discuss reform.
      By the 1780s there was a flood of literature that mocked the royalty and criticised their morals; cartoons and writings made people hostile to the monarchy.
      Together, these developments weakened the authority of the king and the Church and helped the Revolution to take place.
      Historians therefore argue that print did not cause the Revolution on its own, but made it possible.

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] Why is it said that a resource is a function of human wants and technology?
    Ans: A substance becomes a resource only when human beings find a use for it and have the technology and the will to use it. Petroleum was just a black liquid until refining technology made it valuable; as technology and wants change, substances move in and out of being resources.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] Distinguish between renewable and non-renewable resources with examples.
    Ans: Renewable resources can be renewed or reproduced by physical, chemical or mechanical processes and are inexhaustible in the long run — solar and wind energy, water, forests and wildlife. Non-renewable resources occur over very long geological periods and cannot be replenished once used up — coal, petroleum and natural gas, and metallic minerals such as iron ore.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] Explain the main features of black soil.
    Ans: It is also called regur soil and is ideal for growing cotton. It is made of extremely fine clayey material, is well known for its capacity to hold moisture, and is rich in soil nutrients such as calcium carbonate, magnesium, potash and lime, though poor in phosphoric content. It develops deep cracks during hot weather, which helps aeration, and becomes sticky when wet. It is found in the Deccan trap region — Maharashtra, Saurashtra, Malwa and parts of Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] What are the causes of land degradation in India and how can it be controlled?
    Ans:
      Causes: mining in Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, MP and Odisha has left deep scars; overgrazing in Gujarat, Rajasthan, MP and Maharashtra; over-irrigation in Punjab, Haryana and western UP causing waterlogging and increased salinity and alkalinity; and industrial effluents polluting land and water.
      Control: large-scale afforestation, proper management of grazing, shelter belts of plants, stabilisation of sand dunes by growing thorny bushes, proper discharge and treatment of industrial effluents, and control on mining.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes that land degradation in Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh is caused by mining. Correct this, name the real cause and give one remedy.
    Ans:
      In these states the main cause is over-irrigation, which leads to waterlogging and increases the salinity and alkalinity of the soil.
      Mining causes land degradation elsewhere, in states such as Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh and Odisha.
      Remedies include controlled irrigation, drainage of waterlogged fields and growing salt-tolerant crops.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] Classify solar energy, iron ore, fish and wind as renewable or non-renewable, and as biotic or abiotic.
    Ans:
      Solar energy: renewable and abiotic (non-living).
      Iron ore: non-renewable and abiotic; it takes millions of years to form.
      Fish: renewable and biotic (it comes from the living world, and stocks regenerate if not over-fished).
      Wind: renewable and abiotic.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Suggest any three methods of soil conservation used in India.
    Ans:
      Contour ploughing and terracing on hill slopes slow down the flow of water and check soil erosion.
      Strip cropping and planting shelterbelts of trees reduce erosion caused by wind, especially in dry regions.
      Controlling overgrazing, preventing deforestation and practising crop rotation and gully plugging keep the soil covered and fertile.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    The land use of a state is as follows (illustrative figures, % of reporting area): forests 22, net sown area 44, current fallow 8, other fallow 5, pastures 4, barren and waste land 7, land under non-agricultural uses 10.
    
    (a) Which is the largest category?
    (b) What percentage of the land is unavailable for cultivation (barren, waste and non-agricultural uses)?
    (c) Suggest one way to improve this land-use pattern.
    Ans:
      (a) Net sown area, at 44%.
      (b) 7% + 10% = 17%.
      (c) Bring fallow land (13%) into use through irrigation and fertilisers, or raise forest cover towards the 33% target set by the National Forest Policy.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A farmer finds that the soil in his field is reddish, gets its colour from iron compounds, is poor in humus, nitrogen and phosphorus, and gives better yields with fertilisers and irrigation. It is common in the eastern and southern parts of the Deccan Plateau.
    
    (a) Name this soil.
    (b) Why is it red?
    (c) How can its fertility be improved?
    Ans:
      (a) Red (and yellow) soil.
      (b) It forms on crystalline igneous and metamorphic rocks, and the red colour is due to the diffusion of iron; it looks yellow when it is hydrated.
      (c) By adding manure and fertilisers (to supply humus, nitrogen and phosphorus) and by irrigating.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] Compare alluvial soil and laterite soil — how each is formed, where it is found and what it grows.
    Ans:
      Alluvial soil is deposited by rivers and is very fertile, rich in potash and lime but poor in nitrogen and humus; it covers the northern plains and the deltas and grows rice, wheat, sugarcane and pulses.
      Laterite soil forms in hot, wet areas where heavy rain leaches away lime and silica, leaving iron and aluminium oxides; it is acidic and low in humus.
      It is found on the summits of the Western Ghats, eastern Maharashtra, Odisha, parts of Chhattisgarh and the north-east.
      With manures and irrigation it grows tea, coffee, rubber, cashew and tapioca.

Q11. [Long · 5 marks] What is sustainable development and why is it important? Refer to Agenda 21.
    Ans:
      Sustainable development is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. It requires that economic growth go hand in hand with environmental protection and social equity.
      Why it matters: indiscriminate use of resources has led to depletion, concentration of resources in a few hands, land degradation and global ecological crises such as global warming and ozone depletion.
      Agenda 21 was adopted by the first Earth Summit held at Rio de Janeiro in 1992. It is a declaration of global agreement and international cooperation to combat environmental damage, poverty and disease through sustainable development, and it also provides that every local government should draw its own local Agenda 21.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] What is resource planning? Describe the three steps involved in resource planning in India and explain why it is essential.
    Ans:
      Resource planning is the widely accepted strategy for the judicious use of resources.
      Step 1: identify and make an inventory of resources across the regions, by surveying, mapping and estimating their quality and quantity.
      Step 2: evolve a planning structure with suitable technology, skill and institutions to carry out the plans.
      Step 3: match the resource development plans with the national development plans.
      It is essential because resources are unevenly distributed (for example, minerals in Jharkhand, solar and wind power in Rajasthan) and many are limited, so planning is needed for balanced regional development and to prevent over-use.

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] What is biodiversity and why is it important?
    Ans: Biodiversity is the variety of plant and animal life found in a region or in the world. It keeps ecosystems stable, recycles nutrients, maintains the climate and water cycle, and supplies food, medicines, fibre and other raw materials on which human life depends.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What are the categories of species recognised for conservation? Give one example of each.
    Ans:
      Normal species — population levels are normal, e.g. cattle and rodents.
      Endangered species — in danger of extinction, e.g. the black buck and the Indian rhino.
      Vulnerable species — declining and likely to become endangered, e.g. the Asiatic elephant.
      Rare species — a small population in a limited area, e.g. the Himalayan brown bear.
      Extinct species — no longer found, e.g. the Asiatic cheetah.
      Endemic species — found only in one area, e.g. the Andaman teal.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] How have local communities conserved forests and wildlife? Give two examples.
    Ans: Villagers around the Sariska Tiger Reserve in Alwar (Rajasthan) fought against mining in the reserve, and villages of the Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri' in the same district framed their own rules banning hunting and protecting wildlife. The Chipko movement stopped felling in the Himalayas when women hugged the trees, the Beej Bachao Andolan saved indigenous seeds, and in sacred groves such as the Sarna and devrai, forests are protected by religious custom rather than by law.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] What is Joint Forest Management and why does it matter?
    Ans: Joint Forest Management, introduced in 1988 and first adopted in Odisha, is a programme in which local communities protect and regenerate forests together with the Forest Department, in return for a share of the produce of the forest. It matters because protection works only when the people who live next to the forest have an interest in it.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes that the Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act was passed in 1973, the same year as Project Tiger. Correct the dates and state two things the Act did.
    Ans:
      The Wildlife (Protection) Act was passed in 1972; Project Tiger was launched in 1973.
      The Act banned hunting and gave legal protection to wildlife habitats.
      It also set up protected areas such as national parks and sanctuaries, and drew up lists of protected species.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A forest officer finds that in a district more than half of the forest land is permanently reserved for conservation, about one-third is protected from further depletion, and the rest belongs to the government, individuals or communities. Name the three categories and say which is most valuable for conservation.
    Ans:
      The three categories are reserved forests, protected forests and unclassed forests.
      Reserved forests are more than half of the forest land and are regarded as the most valuable for conservation.
      Protected forests (almost one-third) are guarded from further depletion; unclassed forests are the rest, owned by the government, individuals and communities.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Distinguish between a wildlife sanctuary, a national park and a biosphere reserve.
    Ans:
      In a wildlife sanctuary, hunting is banned and some human activity such as grazing or collection of forest produce is allowed under restrictions.
      In a national park, no human activity at all is permitted inside the core area; grazing, forestry and cultivation are prohibited.
      A biosphere reserve is a larger area divided into core, buffer and transition zones, in which conservation, research and limited human use go together; a reserve may include several sanctuaries and parks.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    In some villages, a patch of forest is left untouched as a sacred grove: no tree may be cut and no animal killed, because the grove is considered the home of a deity.
    
    (a) What does this show about traditional attitudes to nature?
    (b) How does a sacred grove help biodiversity?
    (c) How can the Forest Department work with such communities?
    Ans:
      (a) Many communities protect nature out of religious and cultural belief and see themselves as part of it.
      (b) The undisturbed grove keeps the habitat intact, so plants and animals, including rare species, survive there.
      (c) Through Joint Forest Management, in which villagers help protect the forest and in return receive a share of the benefits.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    In the Tehri region, some farmers have stopped using synthetic chemicals and instead grow a variety of local crops, conserving traditional seeds.
    
    (a) Name the movement associated with this.
    (b) What are the benefits of such crop diversity?
    (c) How is it linked to forest and wildlife conservation?
    Ans:
      (a) The Beej Bachao Andolan (the Navdanya movement is similar).
      (b) It preserves local varieties of seeds, keeps soil healthy, lowers costs and reduces the risk of losing a whole crop to one pest or drought.
      (c) It shows that people can earn a living without depending on destroying forests, and it protects agricultural biodiversity.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] Describe the Chipko movement and what it achieved.
    Ans:
      It began in 1974 in the Garhwal Himalayas when villagers, mostly women, hugged trees to stop contractors from felling them.
      Led by Sunderlal Bahuguna and Gaura Devi, it spread across Uttarakhand and used songs and satyagraha to protect the forests.
      The government was forced to ban commercial felling in the Himalayan forests for fifteen years.
      The movement showed that local communities can manage forests better than distant contractors, and it inspired later conservation struggles in India.

Q11. [Long · 5 marks] Explain any five causes of the depletion of forests and wildlife in India.
    Ans:
      Agricultural expansion and shifting cultivation have cleared large forest tracts.
      Large development projects — dams, roads, railways and mines — submerge or cut through forests.
      Mining and quarrying destroy habitats and pollute streams.
      Grazing and the collection of fuel wood prevent regeneration.
      Poaching and illegal trade in skins, tusks and horns target the most threatened species.
      Habitat destruction and fragmentation leave small populations that a single fire or disease can wipe out.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Why should we conserve forests and wildlife? Give five reasons.
    Ans:
      Ecological balance: forests and wildlife maintain the life-support systems of air, water and soil.
      Biodiversity: the variety of plants and animals provides the genetic resources for crops and medicines.
      Livelihood: forests give timber, fuel, fodder, fruits and medicinal plants, on which many communities depend.
      Climate and water: forests regulate rainfall and temperature, and recharge groundwater.
      Culture and heritage: forests and wildlife have spiritual and cultural value, and future generations deserve to inherit them.

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] Why is water scarcity in India often a problem of management rather than of availability?
    Ans: India receives plenty of monsoon rain, but most of it flows away unused. Scarcity is caused by over-extraction of groundwater, unequal access between rich and poor, pollution of rivers and tanks by domestic and industrial waste, and wasteful irrigation and domestic use — so the problem is how water is stored, distributed and used.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] Why is water scarcity increasing in India?
    Ans: Because of a growing population, which raises the demand for water for drinking and for growing more food and cash crops; rapid urbanisation and changing lifestyles that consume more water; over-exploitation of groundwater by commercial farming; and unequal access between social groups. In many regions water is abundant but polluted by domestic and industrial waste and by pesticides and fertilisers, so it cannot be used without treatment.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] Describe the traditional rainwater harvesting system of Rajasthan.
    Ans: In the semi-arid regions of Rajasthan, particularly in Bikaner, Phalodi and Barmer, most houses traditionally had underground tanks or tankas for storing drinking water. The tanks were built inside the house or the courtyard and were connected to the sloping roofs. The first spell of rain was not collected, as it cleans the roof and the pipes; from the second shower onward the water was stored. The stored water lasted through the dry season and was treated as pure enough for religious use.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] What is rooftop rainwater harvesting and where has it been made compulsory?
    Ans: It is a method in which rainwater falling on rooftops is collected through pipes and stored in underground tanks or used to recharge groundwater. It is common in Meghalaya, where bamboo pipes are used, and in Tamil Nadu it has been made compulsory for all houses, with legal provisions to punish defaulters.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A student answers “Multipurpose river projects help by providing irrigation, electricity and flood control.” Why would this answer lose marks if the question asks for an evaluation? Add three drawbacks.
    Ans:
      It lists only the benefits. An evaluation question expects both advantages and disadvantages.
      Drawback 1: large numbers of people are displaced, and their land and homes are submerged.
      Drawback 2: dams change the natural flow of the river, cause sedimentation, and harm fish and aquatic life.
      Drawback 3: they can cause floods or waterlogging and lead to disputes between states over sharing water.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] Name one traditional rainwater-harvesting or water-saving method each suited to (i) the hills and (ii) the arid plains of Rajasthan, and say how each works.
    Ans:
      (i) Hills: guls or kuls, diversion channels in the Western Himalayas that carry water from streams to fields (in Meghalaya, bamboo pipes do a similar job).
      (ii) Rajasthan: tankas (underground tanks) that store rainwater collected from roofs and courtyards, or johads and khadins, earthen structures that hold rain for crops and recharge groundwater.
      Both capture water when it is plentiful so that it can be used in the dry months.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Mention any three problems caused by large multi-purpose river projects.
    Ans:
      Large numbers of tribal and peasant families are displaced and often inadequately resettled and rehabilitated.
      Forests and biodiversity are submerged, and the flow and ecology of the river are disturbed.
      Benefits are unequal: canals usually serve the richer farmers in the command area, causing waterlogging and salinity, while the poor and the downstream areas gain little.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Villagers living along a river protest against a proposed dam that would submerge their fields and homes. Engineers say it will supply water and electricity to distant cities.
    
    (a) Whose interests conflict here?
    (b) Name two movements in India against large dams.
    (c) How can such conflicts be reduced?
    Ans:
      (a) The interests of the displaced local people and the interests of the cities and farmers who would benefit from the water and power.
      (b) The Narmada Bachao Andolan (against the Sardar Sarovar project) and the movement against the Tehri Dam on the Bhagirathi.
      (c) By proper rehabilitation and compensation, by involving the people affected in decisions, and by using small projects such as check dams and rainwater harvesting.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    In Meghalaya, farmers use a 200-year-old system in which bamboo pipes carry water from hill springs over long distances to plantations, delivering it to the plants as slow drops.
    
    (a) Which type of irrigation does this resemble?
    (b) Why is it efficient?
    (c) Why is it relevant today?
    Ans:
      (a) Drip irrigation.
      (b) Water goes straight to the plants, drop by drop, so very little is lost to evaporation or seepage.
      (c) It saves scarce water, is cheap and eco-friendly, and suits hilly terrain, so it is a model for conservation.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] Write one important fact each about the Hirakud, Bhakra Nangal and Sardar Sarovar projects.
    Ans:
      Hirakud, on the Mahanadi in Odisha, is one of the longest dams in the world and was built mainly to control floods in the Mahanadi delta.
      Bhakra Nangal, on the Sutlej, is India's highest straight gravity dam; it irrigates Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan and generates hydel power.
      Sardar Sarovar, on the Narmada in Gujarat, has been at the centre of a long struggle over the displacement of adivasis and peasants, led by the Narmada Bachao Andolan.

Q11. [Long · 5 marks] Multi-purpose river projects have several advantages but also several drawbacks. Explain.
    Ans:
      Advantages: they provide water for irrigation, generate hydroelectricity, control floods, help inland navigation, supply water to industries and cities, and provide fish breeding, recreation and landscape value. Nehru proudly called dams the temples of modern India.
      Drawbacks: (i) large dams submerge forests and agricultural land and displace local communities, who are often not properly rehabilitated — the Narmada Bachao Andolan and the Tehri Dam Andolan grew out of this; (ii) they disturb the natural flow of rivers, causing poor sediment flow and excessive sedimentation at the bottom of the reservoir, which destroys habitats; (iii) they cause waterlogging and soil salinity in the command area; (iv) they trigger inter-state disputes over the sharing of costs and benefits, as between Karnataka, Maharashtra and Andhra Pradesh over the Krishna-Godavari waters; (v) they have induced earthquakes and, in the case of the Sardar Sarovar project, sparked protests when urban water supply was given priority over irrigation for farmers.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Describe any four traditional rainwater-harvesting methods and the regions where they are used.
    Ans:
      Guls and kuls: diversion channels built in the hills of the Western Himalayas to carry stream water to fields.
      Rooftop harvesting and tankas: in Rajasthan, rain from roofs flows into underground tanks that give drinking water through the dry season.
      Khadins and johads: in Jaisalmer and other dry parts of Rajasthan, earthen embankments hold rain on farmland and recharge groundwater.
      Inundation channels: in the flood plains of West Bengal, channels carry floodwater to fields. (Meghalaya’s bamboo drip irrigation is another example.)

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] What is mixed cropping? Why do farmers practise it?
    Ans: Mixed cropping is growing two or more crops together on the same field in the same season, for example wheat and gram. It reduces the risk of total crop failure, keeps the soil fertile because different crops use different nutrients, and checks the spread of pests.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] Describe the geographical conditions required for the growth of rice.
    Ans: Rice is a kharif crop that requires a temperature above 25 °C and high humidity, with annual rainfall above 100 cm. It grows best in the plains of north and north-eastern India, the coastal areas and the deltaic regions. In areas of less rainfall, such as Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh, it is grown with the help of irrigation and canal networks.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] Distinguish between kharif and rabi crops with two examples of each.
    Ans: Kharif crops are sown with the onset of the monsoon in June–July and harvested in September–October — for example paddy, maize, cotton and jute. Rabi crops are sown in winter from October to December and harvested in summer from April to June — for example wheat, barley, peas, gram and mustard.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] What is plantation agriculture? Give one example.
    Ans: It is a type of commercial farming in which a single crop is grown on a large area, using capital-intensive inputs and migrant labour, and the produce is used as raw material in industries. Tea in Assam and North Bengal, coffee in Karnataka, rubber in Kerala and banana in southern India are examples; the production of tea in Assam was developed by the British with capital, technology and indentured labour.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] What is buffer stock and why is it created?
    Ans: Buffer stock is a stock of foodgrains, mainly wheat and rice, procured by the government through the Food Corporation of India. It is created to distribute foodgrains in deficit areas and among the poorer strata of society at a price lower than the market price, and to meet the shortage during a bad harvest or a calamity.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes that wheat, gram and mustard are kharif crops, while rice and maize are rabi crops. Correct this and state the seasons of sowing and harvest.
    Ans:
      Rabi crops — wheat, barley, peas, gram and mustard — are sown in winter (October to December) and harvested in summer (April to June).
      Kharif crops — rice, maize, jowar, bajra, cotton and jute — are sown with the onset of the monsoon (June–July) and harvested in September–October.
      A short summer season between the two is called zaid, when crops such as watermelon and cucumber are grown.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] Which crop would you advise for each case: (i) hot, humid climate, more than 100 cm of rain, alluvial soil; (ii) cool growing season, bright sunshine at ripening, 50–75 cm of rain, well-drained loamy soil; (iii) black soil, high temperature, light rain and frost-free days? Give reasons.
    Ans:
      (i) Rice: it needs high temperature, high humidity and heavy rainfall, with water standing in the fields.
      (ii) Wheat: a rabi crop that needs a cool growing season, bright sunshine at ripening, moderate rain and well-drained loamy soil.
      (iii) Cotton: it grows well in black soil, needs high temperature, light rain and a long frost-free period.

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] Describe the geographical conditions required for growing sugarcane and name the main producing states.
    Ans:
      Sugarcane needs a hot and humid climate with a temperature of about 21°C to 27°C.
      It requires 75 to 100 cm of rainfall, plenty of sunshine and a long growing season of 10 to 12 months.
      It grows best in well-drained fertile soil and needs a large supply of cheap labour for harvesting and crushing.
      The main producers are Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Gujarat and Bihar.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    After high-yielding seeds, fertilisers and irrigation were introduced, wheat production in some states rose sharply, but groundwater levels fell and the soil lost fertility.
    
    (a) Name this change in Indian agriculture.
    (b) State two benefits.
    (c) State two problems it created.
    Ans:
      (a) The Green Revolution.
      (b) Foodgrain production rose and India became nearly self-sufficient in cereals; farmers’ incomes increased.
      (c) The water table fell and soil quality worsened from heavy use of chemicals; benefits stayed mostly in a few regions, increasing regional inequality.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A tribal family clears a patch of forest by cutting and burning the trees, grows crops there for a few years and then moves on to a new patch.
    
    (a) Name this type of farming.
    (b) Why does the family move on?
    (c) Why is this practice declining or discouraged?
    Ans:
      (a) Slash-and-burn (shifting) cultivation, called jhumming in north-eastern India.
      (b) After a few years the soil loses fertility, so the family clears a new patch and lets the old one recover.
      (c) With more people and less forest land, there is not enough time for the land to recover; it destroys forest and causes soil erosion, so governments discourage it.

Q11. [Long · 4 marks] Explain any three institutional and technological reforms that changed Indian agriculture.
    Ans:
      Land reforms: abolition of zamindari, tenancy regulation, land ceilings and consolidation of holdings gave cultivators security and incentive to invest.
      Technological reforms: the Green Revolution brought high-yielding variety seeds, chemical fertilisers, pesticides, tube-well irrigation and mechanisation, which raised output sharply.
      Institutional support: cooperative credit societies, the minimum support price and procurement by the FCI, buffer stock operations, crop insurance and the Kisan Credit Card reduced the farmer's dependence on moneylenders.
      There is now a growing shift towards organic farming, contract farming and diversification into horticulture and dairy.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Describe the impact of globalisation on Indian agriculture.
    Ans:
      Indian agriculture has faced the impact of globalisation since colonial times — indigo was grown for export and forced upon farmers, leading to the Champaran revolt.
      Today, Indian farmers face competition from highly subsidised agriculture in developed countries, so the prices of agricultural produce are unstable and many small farmers find cultivation unviable.
      Liberalisation has removed restrictions on imports, so cheaper imports of edible oils and grains have reduced demand for Indian produce.
      Positive side: improved technology, HYV and genetically modified seeds, better irrigation and access to information have raised productivity in some regions, and organic farming is opening new markets.
      Result: a large number of small and marginal farmers have withdrawn from farming, and agriculture is no longer a rewarding occupation for them.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] Describe the geographical conditions required for growing wheat and name the major wheat-producing regions of India.
    Ans:
      Wheat is a rabi crop, sown in winter and harvested in the next summer.
      It needs a cool growing season and bright sunshine at the time of ripening.
      It needs 50–75 cm of annual rainfall, evenly distributed over the growing season, and well-drained loamy soil.
      Major producing states: Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh and Rajasthan.
      Two main wheat zones are the Ganga–Satluj plains in the north-west and the black soil region of the Deccan.

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] What is meant by a conventional source of energy? Give two examples.
    Ans: Conventional sources are those that have been in use for a long time and are non-renewable, so their stock is limited and cannot be replaced within a human lifetime. Examples: coal, petroleum, natural gas and firewood.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] Distinguish between conventional and non-conventional sources of energy with two examples each.
    Ans: Conventional sources have been in use for a long time, are largely exhaustible, cause pollution and are costly to obtain — for example coal and petroleum. Non-conventional sources are recently developed, are renewable and eco-friendly, and have lower running costs — for example solar and wind energy.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] Why is there a pressing need to use non-conventional sources of energy in India?
    Ans: Because the growing consumption of energy has resulted in a rising dependence on coal and petroleum, whose prices are rising and whose reserves are limited; importing petroleum puts a heavy burden on foreign exchange. Conventional sources also cause environmental degradation and global warming. India has abundant potential for solar, wind, tidal, geothermal and biogas energy, which is renewable, eco-friendly and can be generated locally, so developing it improves energy security and reduces pollution.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] Describe the distribution of iron ore in India.
    Ans: Iron ore is the basic mineral and the backbone of industrial development. Hematite, the finest quality with up to 70% iron, and magnetite, with 60–70% iron and excellent magnetic properties, are found in four belts: the Odisha-Jharkhand belt (Badampahar and Mayurbhanj in Odisha, Kiriburu in Jharkhand); the Durg-Bastar-Chandrapur belt in Chhattisgarh and Maharashtra, including the famous Bailadila mines whose ore is exported to Japan and South Korea; the Bellary-Chitradurga-Chikkamagaluru-Tumakuru belt in Karnataka with the large Kudremukh mines; and the Maharashtra-Goa belt, whose ore is exported through the Marmagao port.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A student says lignite is the best quality coal because it is mined at Neyveli. Correct this and arrange the four types of coal from best to poorest quality.
    Ans:
      Lignite is a low-grade brown coal with a lot of moisture, so it is not the best; the best quality is anthracite.
      Order from best to poorest: anthracite → bituminous → lignite → peat.
      Anthracite has the highest carbon content; bituminous is the most popular in commercial use; peat has the lowest carbon content and heat value.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] Which non-conventional source of energy would you recommend for (i) a sunny desert region, (ii) a windy coastal region and (iii) a village with plenty of cattle dung and farm waste? Give reasons.
    Ans:
      (i) Solar energy: strong sunshine all year can be converted into electricity with photovoltaic cells.
      (ii) Wind energy: steady winds can turn turbines; the Nagercoil–Madurai belt of Tamil Nadu is a large wind-farm cluster.
      (iii) Biogas: cattle dung and farm waste produce a clean cooking fuel and good manure.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Describe the distribution of coal in India and the types of coal found.
    Ans:
      Most of India's coal comes from the Gondwana rocks of the Damodar valley: Raniganj, Jharia, Bokaro and Karanpura in Jharkhand and West Bengal.
      Other important coalfields are Korba in Chhattisgarh, Talcher in Odisha, Singrauli in Madhya Pradesh and Singareni in Telangana.
      Anthracite, the highest-quality coal with over 80% carbon, occurs only in Reasi district of Jammu and Kashmir.
      Bituminous coal is the most common and is mined in Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh and West Bengal; lignite (brown coal) is mined at Neyveli in Tamil Nadu, and in Gujarat and Rajasthan.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A mining company discards low-grade ore as waste and uses only rich ore. A geologist warns that, at this rate, the rich deposits will run out within a few decades.
    
    (a) Why is the conservation of minerals necessary?
    (b) Suggest two ways to conserve minerals.
    (c) State one way in which mining harms miners or the environment.
    Ans:
      (a) Minerals are non-renewable: they take millions of years to form, while the rate of use is very high.
      (b) Develop technology that can use low-grade ores cheaply; recycle metals and use scrap and substitutes.
      (c) Dust and fumes cause lung diseases in miners, mine roofs can collapse, and waste and slurry pollute water and degrade land.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    State X has fast-flowing rivers and heavy rainfall in hilly country. State Y has large coal fields and a flat terrain.
    
    (a) Which state suits hydel power and which suits thermal power?
    (b) Which of the two sources is renewable?
    (c) Why must hydel projects be planned carefully?
    Ans:
      (a) State X suits hydroelectric power; State Y suits thermal power.
      (b) Hydroelectric power is renewable (water is renewed by the water cycle); coal is non-renewable.
      (c) Dams submerge land and forests, displace people and change the flow of rivers, so the social and environmental costs must be weighed.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] Explain why non-conventional sources of energy are being promoted, and describe solar and wind energy.
    Ans:
      Coal and petroleum are limited and their use pollutes the air and adds to global warming, while demand keeps rising; non-conventional sources are renewable and much cleaner.
      Solar energy is captured by photovoltaic cells that convert sunlight directly into electricity and by solar thermal plants; India's largest solar parks are in Gujarat, Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh.
      Wind energy uses clusters of windmills in windy areas; the biggest wind farms are in Tamil Nadu, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Rajasthan and Andhra Pradesh.
      India is now among the world's leading producers of wind power, and the government supports both through subsidies and renewable purchase obligations.

Q11. [Long · 5 marks] Explain any five non-conventional sources of energy available in India.
    Ans:
      Solar energy: converted using photovoltaic technology; rapidly becoming popular in rural India, used for lighting, cooking and water heating — the largest solar plants are in Rajasthan (Bhadla) and Tamil Nadu (Neyveli).
      Wind energy: India is a superpower in wind power, with the largest cluster of wind farms in Tamil Nadu from Nagercoil to Madurai.
      Biogas: produced from farm waste, animal and human waste in gobar gas plants; it has higher thermal efficiency than charcoal and is used for lighting and cooking in villages.
      Tidal energy: generated by using floodgate dams across inlets — potential in the Gulf of Khambhat, the Gulf of Kuchchh and the Sundarbans.
      Geothermal energy: heat from the earth's interior used to generate power — the Manikaran hot springs in Himachal Pradesh and the Puga Valley in Ladakh are promising; six plants have been set up there.
      Atomic energy: produced from uranium and thorium; uranium is found in Jharkhand and the Aravalli range, and thorium in the monazite sands of Kerala.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] In what ways do minerals occur in nature? Explain with an example for each.
    Ans:
      In veins and lodes: minerals fill cracks and joints of igneous and metamorphic rocks, for example tin, copper, zinc and lead.
      In beds or layers: minerals formed in sedimentary rocks, for example coal, iron ore, gypsum and potash salt.
      By decomposition of surface rocks: the soluble parts are removed and a residual mass is left, for example bauxite.
      As alluvial deposits (placers): minerals washed down and deposited in valley sands, for example gold, silver and tin.
      In ocean waters: common salt, magnesium and bromine are obtained from sea water.

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] Why is the jute industry concentrated in the Hooghly basin?
    Ans: The Hooghly basin grows raw jute in its delta, has cheap labour from the neighbouring states of Bihar, Odisha and Uttar Pradesh, a humid climate suitable for spinning, plenty of water for retting and washing, Kolkata port for export, and the Hooghly river for transport.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] Why is the iron and steel industry called a basic industry? Give three reasons.
    Ans: It is called a basic industry because all other industries — heavy, medium and light — depend on it for their machinery, and because production and consumption of steel are regarded as the index of a country's level of development. It is also a heavy industry, since both its raw materials and its finished products are heavy and bulky, so transport costs are a major part of its total cost.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] Why has the sugar industry shifted to southern and western states, especially Maharashtra?
    Ans: Because the sugarcane grown there has a higher sucrose content, the cooler climate ensures a longer crushing season, and the cooperatives in these states are more successful, so the mills get a better and more regular supply of cane than in the older north Indian centres.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] How has the information technology industry contributed to India's economy?
    Ans: It has generated large employment, especially for women, and has been a major earner of foreign exchange through the rapidly growing business process outsourcing (BPO) sector. Software technology parks provide single-window services and high data communication facilities, and Bengaluru has become the electronic capital of India. Continued growth in hardware and software depends on a skilled workforce and good connectivity.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes that iron and steel is a consumer industry because steel utensils are used at home. Correct this by classifying the industry and giving two reasons.
    Ans:
      Iron and steel is a basic (key) industry, not a consumer industry.
      Many other industries — machine tools, construction, transport, heavy engineering — depend on it for their raw material or machinery.
      Consumer industries, such as sugar, paper or toothpaste, make goods that people buy for their own use.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] Name the main raw materials of the cement industry and give two factors that influence where a cement plant is located.
    Ans:
      Raw materials: limestone, silica, alumina and gypsum, plus coal and electricity for the kilns.
      Limestone is bulky and the cement is heavy, so plants are set up near limestone deposits to keep transport costs low.
      Cement is needed everywhere for construction, so good road and rail links and a nearby market also matter.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Name the different types of industrial pollution and suggest two ways of reducing air pollution.
    Ans:
      Types: air pollution, water pollution, thermal pollution, land pollution and noise pollution.
      Air pollution can be reduced by fitting electrostatic precipitators, scrubbers and filters in chimneys to remove particulates and gases.
      Other measures: using cleaner fuels such as CNG and low-sulphur coal, building tall chimneys so pollutants disperse, and keeping industries away from residential areas.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Four factories are: a cotton mill, a sugar mill, an iron and steel plant and an aluminium smelter.
    
    (a) Which are agro-based and which are mineral-based?
    (b) Name the main raw material of each.
    (c) Which of them is called a basic industry, and why?
    Ans:
      (a) Agro-based: the cotton mill and the sugar mill. Mineral-based: the iron and steel plant and the aluminium smelter.
      (b) Cotton; sugarcane; iron ore with coking coal and limestone; bauxite.
      (c) Iron and steel, because other industries depend on its products.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    India’s cotton textile industry has two parts: spinning, which is mostly done in mills, and weaving, which is largely decentralised in handlooms, powerlooms and hosiery units.
    
    (a) Which part is organised and which is decentralised?
    (b) Why is weaving kept decentralised?
    (c) Name two problems faced by the spinning sector.
    Ans:
      (a) Spinning is in the organised mill sector; weaving is decentralised.
      (b) Decentralised weaving gives scope for handloom and powerloom units, and keeps cloth production flexible and employment widespread.
      (c) Erratic power supply, outdated machinery, low labour productivity and stiff competition from synthetic fibres.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] Explain why the cotton textile industry shifted from Maharashtra to Gujarat and Tamil Nadu.
    Ans:
      Gujarat has the nearby cotton belt, a humid coastal climate suitable for weaving, cheap land and power, a tradition of trade and enterprise, and good port facilities.
      Ahmedabad grew as the ‘Manchester of India' on the basis of the spinning and weaving mills set up by Gujarati entrepreneurs.
      In Tamil Nadu, cotton comes from the black soil region, cheap power is available from hydroelectricity, and labour is plentiful; the Coimbatore–Madurai belt has integrated ginning, spinning and weaving.
      The Mumbai mills, meanwhile, faced high land costs, old machinery, power shortages and militant trade unionism.

Q11. [Long · 5 marks] Describe the factors responsible for the location of the jute textile industry along the banks of the Hooghly.
    Ans:
      Proximity of the jute-producing areas — the jute belt of West Bengal, Bihar, Odisha and Assam lies close by.
      Inexpensive water transport, supported by a good network of railways, roadways and waterways for the movement of jute to the mills.
      Abundant water for processing raw jute, which the Hooghly supplies.
      Cheap labour from West Bengal and the adjoining states of Bihar, Odisha and Uttar Pradesh.
      Kolkata as a large urban centre provides banking, insurance, port facilities for export and other commercial services.
      As a result India is the largest producer of raw jute and jute goods in the world, though it faces competition from synthetic substitutes and from other producers such as Bangladesh.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] What are the different types of industrial pollution? How can it be controlled?
    Ans:
      Types: (1) Air pollution — caused by chemical and paper factories, brick kilns, refineries and smelting plants burning fossil fuels, and by toxic gas leaks such as the Bhopal tragedy; (2) Water pollution — caused by organic and inorganic industrial wastes and effluents discharged into rivers by paper, pulp, chemical, textile and dyeing, petroleum refineries and tanneries; (3) Thermal pollution — hot water from factories and power plants drained into rivers and ponds before cooling; (4) Noise pollution — from industrial and construction activity, machinery and generators, causing hearing impairment and stress.
      Control: particulate matter can be controlled by fitting electrostatic precipitators, fabric filters, scrubbers and inertial separators to smokestacks; smoke can be reduced by using oil instead of coal; water pollution can be minimised by reusing and recycling water in two or more successive stages, harvesting rainwater, and treating hot water and effluents before releasing them, in three stages — primary (mechanical), secondary (biological) and tertiary (chemical and physical); noise can be reduced by fitting silencers on generators and machinery, redesigning machines and providing workers with earplugs.

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] Why is the density of roads higher in the northern plains than in the Himalayan region?
    Ans: The northern plains are level, densely populated and rich in agriculture and industry, so roads are cheap to build and carry heavy traffic. The Himalayas are rugged, prone to landslides and sparsely populated, so construction costs are high and the volume of traffic is low.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] Why are roadways considered more efficient than railways in India?
    Ans: Because the cost of constructing roads is much lower than that of railway lines; roads can traverse more dissected and undulating topography and negotiate higher gradients, such as in the Himalayas; they provide door-to-door service, which reduces the loading and unloading cost; and they act as feeders to other modes of transport, connecting railway stations, airports and sea ports.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] Why are means of transport and communication called the lifelines of a nation and its economy?
    Ans: Because goods and services cannot move from supply locations to demand locations without them. A dense and efficient transport and communication network is a prerequisite for local, national and international trade, it enables industries to reach their markets and raw materials, it creates employment, and it has made the world a global village by allowing information to move instantly.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] What are the advantages of pipeline transport?
    Ans: Pipelines can transport crude oil, petroleum products and natural gas from the fields to refineries, fertiliser plants and thermal power stations, and solids can be moved as slurry. Once laid, they have minimal running cost, they eliminate transhipment losses and delays, and they can pass through difficult terrain including rivers and hills, so they provide a continuous and reliable supply. Their only major drawback is the high initial cost of laying the line.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] Describe the importance of waterways in India.
    Ans: Waterways are the cheapest means of transport and are best suited for carrying heavy and bulky goods. They are a fuel-efficient and environment-friendly mode of transport. India has about 14,500 km of inland waterways, of which six stretches have been declared National Waterways, including the Ganga between Allahabad and Haldia and the Brahmaputra between Sadiya and Dhubri. These waterways also support tourism and connect remote regions to the national market.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes that pipelines are cheap to set up but expensive to run. Correct this and give two advantages of pipeline transport.
    Ans:
      Pipelines have a high initial cost but a very low running cost, not the other way round.
      Advantage 1: they carry crude oil, natural gas and refined products continuously over long distances, even through rugged terrain, with no loading or trans-shipment losses.
      Advantage 2: they reduce the need for road and rail tankers, so traffic and delays are less (solids can also be moved as slurry).

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] Suggest the most suitable mode of transport for (i) a large quantity of coal for a power plant 1,500 km away, (ii) fresh flowers for a city 1,500 km away and (iii) goods for a village that has no railway station. Give reasons.
    Ans:
      (i) Railways (or waterways/pipelines where available): cheapest for bulky goods over long distances.
      (ii) Airways: fastest, so perishable and high-value goods arrive fresh, though costly.
      (iii) Roadways: they give door-to-door service and can reach villages and remote areas that rail cannot.

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] Explain the importance of railways in India and any two problems they face.
    Ans:
      Railways carry a huge number of passengers and bulky goods over long distances cheaply, link markets, ports and pilgrim centres, and promote national integration.
      Problem 1: a large backlog of investment — tracks, bridges and rolling stock are old and the network is congested on trunk routes.
      Problem 2: losses on passenger services, theft and damage to railway property, and the slow pace of gauge conversion and electrification.

Q11. [Long · 4 marks] Describe the importance of means of communication in India's economy.
    Ans:
      The Indian postal network, the largest in the world, reaches even remote villages through nearly 1.55 lakh post offices and provides mail, savings, money-order and insurance services.
      Telecom has grown rapidly: STD, ISD, mobile phones and satellite communication connect people across the country and the world.
      The internet and mobile telephony have made e-banking, e-commerce, e-governance, telemedicine and online education possible.
      Together, transport and communication link producers with markets, buyers with sellers, and citizens with the government, which is why they are called the lifelines of the economy.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Why is international trade called the economic barometer of a country? Name four major items each of India’s exports and imports.

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] Why is power sharing desirable in a democracy?
    Ans: It ensures that no single community or group can dominate the others, reduces the fear of oppression and the chances of conflict, respects the claims of minorities and regions, and makes the government more stable, accountable and legitimate in the eyes of the people.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What is the difference between prudential and moral reasons for power sharing?
    Ans: Prudential reasons stress the good consequences of power sharing — it reduces conflict between social groups, ensures the stability of political order and prevents violence and political instability. Moral reasons stress the intrinsic worth of power sharing — that people have a right to be consulted on how they are to be governed, and that a legitimate government is one in which citizens acquire a stake in the system through participation. Prudential reasons are about outcomes; moral reasons are about principles.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] What are the different forms of power sharing in modern democracies? Give one example of each.
    Ans: Horizontal distribution among organs of government — legislature, executive and judiciary, as in India, where the judiciary can review the acts of the legislature. Vertical distribution among governments at different levels — Union, state and local governments in India. Distribution among social groups — the community government in Belgium and reserved constituencies for weaker sections and women in India. Distribution among political parties — coalition governments formed by two or more parties when no single party gains a clear majority.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] What is a system of checks and balances?
    Ans: It is the arrangement in which the organs of government — legislature, executive and judiciary — are placed at the same level but each exercises a check on the others. The legislature makes laws, the executive implements them and the judiciary can declare a law invalid if it violates the constitution. Because of these checks no organ can exercise unlimited power, and this horizontal distribution of power is a basic feature of democracy.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes that 59% of Belgium’s people speak French and 40% speak Dutch. Correct this, and say why the figures mattered in Belgian politics.
    Ans:
      In Belgium, 59% live in the Flemish region and speak Dutch, 40% live in the Wallonia region and speak French, and about 1% speak German.
      In the capital Brussels, about 80% speak French and 20% speak Dutch.
      The Dutch-speaking majority had been the poorer community, while the French-speaking minority was richer and more powerful; this created tension, especially over Brussels.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] Identify the form of power sharing in each case: (i) the Constitution divides powers among the legislature, the executive and the judiciary; (ii) the Union, the states and the local governments each have their own powers; (iii) Belgium’s central government must have equal numbers of Dutch- and French-speaking ministers; (iv) several parties together form a government.
    Ans:
      (i) Horizontal power sharing: power is shared among different organs of government at the same level.
      (ii) Vertical power sharing (federal division): power is shared among governments at different levels.
      (iii) Power shared among social groups, such as religious and linguistic communities.
      (iv) Power shared among political parties, as in a coalition government.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Describe the ethnic composition of Belgium and Sri Lanka and how it shaped their politics.
    Ans:
      In Belgium about 59% of the people speak Dutch (Flemish), 40% speak French (Walloon) and about 1% speak German; the French-speakers were historically richer and more powerful although fewer in number, which caused tension.
      In Brussels the situation is reversed, with a French-speaking majority among a Dutch-speaking region.
      In Sri Lanka the Sinhala-speakers are about 74% and the Tamil-speakers about 18%; the Sinhala-majority government imposed majoritarian measures such as making Sinhala the only official language (1956), which alienated the Tamils and led to a long civil war.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    In a country, 70% of the citizens belong to Group A, 25% to Group B and 5% to Group C. The government is formed only by Group A and passes laws favouring A’s language and religion.
    
    (a) What is this policy called?
    (b) State one likely consequence.
    (c) Suggest one power-sharing arrangement that could help.
    Ans:
      (a) Majoritarianism.
      (b) Groups B and C feel alienated and ignored, which can lead to protest, conflict and a threat to the country’s unity, as happened in Sri Lanka.
      (c) Share power with minorities, for example through a coalition government, reserved representation or equal language rights.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    In a country, trade unions, farmers’ groups and business associations influence the government’s policies, and several parties together run the government.
    
    (a) Which forms of power sharing are seen here?
    (b) How does each help?
    (c) Why is it desirable not to concentrate all power in one place?
    Ans:
      (a) Power shared among political parties (coalition government) and among pressure groups and movements.
      (b) Parties compete and cooperate, so no single party dominates; pressure groups voice the interests of citizens and keep the government answerable.
      (c) Concentrated power can be misused and ignores minorities; sharing power makes government accountable and stable.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] Describe the different forms of power sharing in modern democracies, with an example of each.
    Ans:
      Horizontal distribution among the legislature, executive and judiciary, so that each organ checks the others — for example, the Indian Parliament makes laws while the courts can declare them unconstitutional.
      Vertical division between the Union, States and local governments — federalism, as in India, Belgium and the USA.
      Sharing among different social groups, such as the community government in Belgium, where Dutch-, French- and German-speaking communities have equal representation on some matters.
      Sharing among political parties, pressure groups and movements through coalitions and competitive politics, so that power is not concentrated in one party.

Q11. [Long · 5 marks] Describe the major steps taken towards power sharing in Belgium.
    Ans:
      Equal representation in the central government: the constitution prescribes an equal number of Dutch-speaking and French-speaking ministers, so no single community can take decisions unilaterally.
      Devolution to the regions: many powers of the central government were given to the state governments of the Flemish and Walloon regions, and these regional governments are not subordinate to the central government.
      Brussels: the capital was given a separate government with equal representation for both the French- and Dutch-speaking groups.
      Community government: a third kind of government was set up, elected by people belonging to one language group — Dutch, French or German-speaking — irrespective of where they live, with power over cultural, educational and language-related issues.
      This arrangement, worked out between 1932 and 1968 through four constitutional amendments, prevented civic strife and the division of the country on linguistic lines.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] What were the majoritarian measures adopted by the Sri Lankan government? What were their consequences?
    Ans:
      In 1956 an act was passed to recognise Sinhala as the only official language, disregarding Tamil.
      Successive governments followed preferential policies that favoured Sinhala applicants for university positions and government employment.
      The new constitution stipulated that the state shall protect and foster Buddhism.
      Consequences: the Sri Lankan Tamils felt that none of the major political parties, led by the Buddhist Sinhala leaders, was sensitive to their language and culture. Their demand for recognition of Tamil as an official language, regional autonomy and equal opportunity was repeatedly denied, and the demand for a separate Tamil Eelam grew. This led to a civil war that lasted until 2009, in which thousands of people were killed and the social, cultural and economic life of the country was badly affected.

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] Distinguish between the Union List and the Concurrent List with two examples each.
    Ans: The Union List contains subjects of national importance on which only Parliament can make laws, such as defence, foreign affairs, currency, railways and banking. The Concurrent List contains subjects on which both Parliament and the state legislatures can legislate, such as education, forests, trade unions, marriage and adoption; if the two laws conflict, the Union law prevails.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] Distinguish between 'coming together' and 'holding together' federations with two examples each.
    Ans: In a coming-together federation, independent states come together on their own to form a bigger unit, pooling sovereignty while retaining their identity — the USA, Switzerland and Australia. In a holding-together federation, a large country divides power between the constituent states and the national government, and the centre tends to be more powerful — India, Spain and Belgium.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] Explain any three features of federalism practised in India.
    Ans: Linguistic states — the boundaries of old states were changed and new states created so that people who speak the same language live in the same state; this made administration easier and strengthened national unity. Language policy — Hindi was declared the official language, but because only about 40% of Indians speak it, the constitution recognised 21 other languages as scheduled languages and allowed English to continue as an additional official language. Centre-state relations — the rise of coalition governments at the centre since the 1990s has created a new culture of power sharing and respect for the autonomy of state governments, and the judiciary has made it harder to dismiss state governments for political reasons.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] Why is decentralisation considered a good idea in a democracy?
    Ans: Because there are a large number of problems and issues that are best settled at the local level, such as local planning, sanitation and water supply; people have better knowledge of the problems in their own localities and can take better decisions about how to spend money; and direct participation in local decision-making is a way to train citizens in the habits of democracy and to build a democratic culture.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes that education is a State List subject. Correct this, and give two examples each of the Union, State and Concurrent Lists.
    Ans:
      Education was moved from the State List to the Concurrent List by the 42nd Amendment in 1976.
      Union List: defence, foreign affairs, banking, currency. State List: police, agriculture, trade, irrigation.
      Concurrent List: education, forests, marriage and adoption, trade unions. If a state law and a Union law on a Concurrent subject conflict, the Union law prevails.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] Computer software and the Internet were not known when the Constitution was drafted. Which government can make laws on them, and why? What are residuary subjects?
    Ans:
      Residuary subjects are those that came up after the Constitution was made and so appear in none of the three lists.
      The Constitution gives the Union government the power to legislate on them.
      So the Union government makes laws on computer software, while states legislate on State List subjects.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Describe the structure of rural local government in India after 1992.
    Ans:
      A three-tier Panchayati Raj system: the gram panchayat at the village level, the panchayat samiti or block samiti at the block level, and the zila parishad at the district level.
      All members are elected directly or indirectly for a term of five years, and elections are conducted by the state election commission.
      One-third of all seats and of the chairperson's posts are reserved for women, and seats are reserved for SCs, STs and OBCs in proportion to their population.
      The state finance commission reviews the finances of the panchayats and the state government shares taxes with them.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    India has many languages, and the Constitution did not name any one language as the “national language”.
    
    (a) Which language is the official language of the Union?
    (b) How many languages are listed in the Eighth Schedule?
    (c) How does this arrangement support federalism?
    Ans:
      (a) Hindi, though only about 40% of Indians speak it as their mother tongue; English is also used for official purposes.
      (b) Twenty-two languages are listed as scheduled languages.
      (c) States have their own official languages and the Union recognises many languages, so no group’s language is imposed on others and diversity is respected.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Two neighbouring states quarrel over the sharing of river water, and the Union government and a state disagree over which of them may make a law on a subject.
    
    (a) Who decides such disputes?
    (b) Why is such a body needed in a federal system?
    (c) Name two features of Indian federalism.
    Ans:
      (a) The judiciary: the Supreme Court and High Courts, which interpret the Constitution.
      (b) Power is divided between levels of government, so an independent umpire is needed to see that each stays within its limits.
      (c) Two (or three) levels of government with separate jurisdictions listed in the Constitution; a written constitution that cannot be changed by one level alone; an independent judiciary; revenue sources for each level.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] How has the nature of Indian federalism changed since 1990?
    Ans:
      Before 1990, one party usually ruled at the Centre and the Union dismissed state governments frequently, so the states were weak.
      The era of coalitions from 1989 onwards gave regional parties a share of power at the Centre; no party could govern alone, so states became stronger partners.
      The 73rd and 74th Amendments (1992) made local government a third tier of government, and the S.R. Bommai judgement (1994) restricted the misuse of Article 356.
      More recently, institutions such as the GST Council, the Inter-State Council and centrally-sponsored schemes have made Indian federalism more cooperative, though disputes over resources and language remain.

Q11. [Long · 5 marks] Describe how legislative powers are distributed between the Union and the states in India.
    Ans:
      Union List: about 100 subjects of national importance such as defence, foreign affairs, banking, communications and currency. Only Parliament can make laws on them, because uniform policy is needed for the whole country.
      State List: about 61 subjects of state and local importance such as police, trade, commerce, agriculture and irrigation. Ordinarily only the state legislature can make laws on these.
      Concurrent List: about 52 subjects of common interest to both, such as education, forest, trade unions, marriage, adoption and succession. Both Parliament and the state legislatures can make laws, but if the two conflict, the central law prevails.
      Residuary subjects: subjects that came up after the constitution was made, such as computer software, fall under the jurisdiction of the Union government.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] What major step was taken in 1992 towards decentralisation in India?
    Ans:
      Before 1992, elections to local bodies were not held regularly, and local bodies did not have adequate powers or resources of their own. The constitutional amendment of 1992 made the third tier of democracy far more powerful and effective.
      Regular elections to local government bodies are now constitutionally mandatory.
      Seats are reserved in these bodies for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Other Backward Classes.
      At least one-third of all positions are reserved for women.
      An independent institution called the State Election Commission has been created in each state to conduct panchayat and municipal elections.
      State governments are required to share some powers and revenue with local government bodies, so that local bodies have the means to function.

Unit: Political Science · Democratic Politics II

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] What is the feminist movement and what did it demand?
    Ans: Feminism is a movement and a way of thinking that questions the unequal and oppressive treatment of women simply because they are women. It demanded equal rights in the family, at work and in politics, an end to violence and discrimination, and a change in the way society defines the roles of men and women.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What is the sexual division of labour and how does it lead to gender inequality?
    Ans: The sexual division of labour is the arrangement in which women do the housework, cook and look after children while men work outside the home for wages. It rests on social expectation, not on biology. Because paid work outside is treated as real work while the unpaid work at home is not counted at all, women's contribution is undervalued. That is where gender inequality begins: it keeps women out of the public sphere and out of better-paid, higher-status work.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] What does it mean to say that India is a secular state?
    Ans:
      The Constitution gives no special status to any religion — there is no official religion.
      Every person has the freedom to profess, practise and propagate any religion of their choice.
      Discrimination on the ground of religion is prohibited, so no community can be treated as inferior.
      The state may intervene in religious matters to ensure equality — for example, by abolishing untouchability and making laws for social reform. India is therefore not anti-religious: the state keeps a principled distance from religion.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] Name any three steps taken to increase women's representation in India.
    Ans: One-third of the seats in panchayats and municipalities are reserved for women, and several states have raised this to half, so more than a million women now hold elected positions in local bodies. In 2023 Parliament passed the 106th Constitutional Amendment reserving one-third of the seats in the Lok Sabha and the state assemblies for women, to take effect after the next census and delimitation. The Equal Remuneration Act, 1976 — equal wages for equal work — supports women's participation outside the home as well.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes: “India is a secular state, so the government is against all religions.” Correct the statement and give two features of Indian secularism.
    Ans:
      A secular state is not anti-religious. It has no official religion and treats all religions equally.
      Every citizen is free to practise, profess and propagate any religion, and the state does not discriminate on the ground of religion.
      The state can still intervene in religious matters to ensure equality, for example by banning untouchability.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] Women labourers at a construction site are paid less than men for the same work. Which Act does this violate, what does the Act provide, and what is this an example of?
    Ans:
      It violates the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976.
      The Act provides for equal wages for equal work for men and women.
      This is an example of gender discrimination, which comes from patriarchy and the sexual division of labour.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Distinguish between communalism and secularism, with examples from India.
    Ans:
      Communalism is the belief that people of one religion share common political and economic interests that are opposed to those of other religions; it turns religious identity into political rivalry.
      Examples: the partition riots of 1947 and the 2002 Gujarat riots, in which members of one community were targeted by another.
      Secularism means that the state has no official religion, treats all religions equally and does not discriminate on grounds of religion.
      In India this is expressed in the ban on religious instruction in government-funded schools, the right of every person to profess and practise any religion, and the state's duty to protect all communities.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    In some regions, the number of girls per 1000 boys in the age group 0–6 is far below the national average.
    
    (a) What is this ratio called?
    (b) Which practice has contributed to it?
    (c) State one measure to address the problem.
    Ans:
      (a) The child sex ratio.
      (b) Sex-selective abortion, arising from a preference for sons in a patriarchal society.
      (c) Laws against sex-determination tests, awareness campaigns, and promoting girls’ education and equal opportunities.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    In a constituency, voters belong to many castes and no single caste has a majority. A candidate says, “Vote for me because I belong to your caste.”
    
    (a) Can a candidate win on caste appeal alone?
    (b) Why not?
    (c) Is the effect of caste politics only negative? Explain.
    Ans:
      (a) No.
      (b) No constituency has a clear majority of one caste, so every party needs the support of more than one caste or community; voters also judge candidates and governments on performance, not caste alone.
      (c) No. It has given disadvantaged castes a voice and a share in power, but when caste dominates politics it can distract from issues of poverty and development and cause tension.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] Explain how caste continues to influence Indian politics, and whether this is desirable.
    Ans:
      Caste shapes political behaviour: parties choose candidates with an eye on caste arithmetic, voters often prefer candidates of their own caste, and caste associations influence policy and mobilise support.
      Caste can also be a means of asserting the dignity of disadvantaged groups — for example, the Dalit Panthers and the Bahujan Samaj Party brought Dalit concerns into the political mainstream.
      Used positively, caste-based mobilisation gives a voice to the excluded and increases their share in power, which is not undemocratic.
      It becomes a problem when it turns into casteism — the oppression or exclusion of other castes and violence against them — and when it replaces all discussion of development and governance.

Q11. [Long · 5 marks] What is communalism? Explain any three forms in which it appears in politics.
    Ans:
      Communalism is the belief that people who follow the same religion have common secular interests, and that people of different religions have separate and opposed interests. It is the most common form in which religion is used in politics.
      Everyday prejudice: religious prejudices, stereotypes about other communities and the belief that one's own religion is superior.
      Domination: the desire to establish the dominance of one's own religious community over others and to use state power for it.
      Majoritarian dominance: the belief that the majority community should rule, which turns religion into the basis of political power.
      Separate political units or parties: demanding a separate state, or forming parties on religious lines.
      Communal politics turns violent when religion is treated as the only identity and elections are fought on religious appeals.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Explain the relationship between caste and politics in India.
    Ans:
      Caste in politics: parties take caste into account while choosing candidates, since the dominant caste of a constituency influences the result; voters' preferences are shaped by caste; and governments are formed keeping caste arithmetic in mind.
      Politics in caste: politics has sharpened caste identities — castes have formed associations and asserted themselves to claim a share of political power, which is how OBCs and Dalits entered politics in large numbers.
      But caste is not everything: no parliamentary constituency has a majority of one caste, so every party must appeal across castes; no caste votes as a single block; and voters also judge the government's performance and the leader. Caste inequality persists, but caste alone does not win an election.

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] What is a political party and why are parties necessary in a democracy?
    Ans: A political party is a group of people who come together to contest elections and hold power in government. Parties are necessary because representatives must be elected, because they offer voters different policies to choose from, because they form and run governments, shape public opinion and give people access to government schemes and welfare measures.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What is the criterion for a party to be recognised as a national party?
    Ans: A party is recognised as a national party by the Election Commission if it secures at least six per cent of the total votes in Lok Sabha elections or Assembly elections in four states, and wins at least four seats in the Lok Sabha. State parties need at least six per cent of the votes in the state Assembly elections and at least two seats.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] How do political parties shape public opinion?
    Ans: Parties raise and highlight issues through their lakhs of members and workers spread over the country, and through the pressure groups they support. They also use newspapers, meetings and social media to explain their stand. When a party is not able to solve a problem, it highlights the issue and mobilises opposition, which forces the government to respond.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] Name any three national parties of India with their symbols and year of founding.
    Ans: Indian National Congress, founded in 1885, symbol hand; Bharatiya Janata Party, founded in 1980, symbol lotus; Bahujan Samaj Party, founded in 1984, symbol elephant. (CPI-M, 1964 — hammer, sickle and star; CPI, 1925 — ears of corn and sickle; NCP, 1999 — clock.)

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes that the anti-defection law completely stops legislators from changing parties. Correct this and mention one criticism of it.
    Ans:
      A legislator who leaves the party on whose ticket he or she was elected can lose the seat; the 2003 amendment also bars defectors from holding ministerial posts.
      The law has reduced defection but has not ended it; parties have found other ways around it.
      Critics say it makes dissent within a party difficult, since legislators must follow the party leaders’ decisions.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] In an election to a state Assembly, Party X wins 7% of the votes and 3 seats. In the Lok Sabha elections it wins 3% of the votes nationally and 2 seats. Which type of party can it be recognised as? State the Election Commission’s rules.
    Ans:
      State party: at least 6% of the votes in the Assembly election and at least two seats.
      National party: at least 6% of the votes in Lok Sabha or Assembly elections in four states and at least four Lok Sabha seats.
      Party X meets the first rule (7% and 3 seats), so it is a state party. It does not meet the national-party rule.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Distinguish between a two-party system and a multi-party system.
    Ans:
      In a two-party system, such as in the USA or the UK, only two parties are strong enough to win power, and one of them usually wins a majority on its own.
      In a multi-party system, such as in India, several parties compete and none usually gets a majority, so they must form coalitions to govern.
      A multi-party system represents the diversity of a large and varied society better; a two-party system gives more stable but less representative government.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A party in power receives huge donations from a few rich businessmen, while another party depends on small contributions from many ordinary people. Some candidates in the state have criminal cases against them.
    
    (a) Which challenge to political parties is shown?
    (b) How does it harm voters and elections?
    (c) Name one reform that the courts or the Election Commission introduced.
    Ans:
      (a) The growing role of money and muscle power in politics.
      (b) Rich donors and criminals can gain influence, honest candidates and poor voters are at a disadvantage, and voters’ real choice is reduced.
      (c) The Supreme Court made it compulsory for candidates to file an affidavit giving details of their property and criminal cases; the Election Commission also required parties to hold organisational elections and file income-tax returns.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    In country P only one party is allowed to contest elections. In country Q two main parties alternate in power. In country R many parties compete and often form alliances.
    
    (a) Name each party system.
    (b) Which of these is not suitable for democracy, and why?
    (c) Which system exists in India?
    Ans:
      (a) One-party system (P), two-party system (Q) and multi-party system (R).
      (b) The one-party system, because voters have no choice and there is no free competition, which democracy needs.
      (c) A multi-party system, in which parties often form alliances to contest elections and run governments.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] Suggest any four reforms that would strengthen political parties in India.
    Ans:
      A law to regulate the internal affairs of parties: they should maintain a register of members, hold regular organisational elections and follow their own constitutions.
      State funding of elections to reduce the dependence of parties on big donors and on black money, along with the filing of audited accounts and income-tax returns.
      Disclosure of the assets and criminal records of candidates, as required by the Supreme Court and the Election Commission, so that voters can make an informed choice.
      Reservation for women in the party organisation as well as in the allotment of tickets, and an end to dynastic succession through inner-party democracy.

Q11. [Long · 5 marks] Describe the main functions of political parties in a democracy.
    Ans:
      Contesting elections: parties select candidates and campaign for them; in India, top leaders usually choose the candidates.
      Putting forward policies and programmes: parties present different sets of policies, and voters choose between them.
      Making laws: legislatures have many members, so laws are debated and passed along party lines, and the party leadership effectively decides.
      Forming and running the government: the winning party or coalition forms the government, and its top leaders become ministers and run the administration.
      Playing the opposition: losing parties criticise the government for its failures and wrong policies, mobilise opposition and voice different views.
      Shaping public opinion: parties raise and highlight issues through lakhs of members and workers and through pressure groups.
      Providing access to government machinery and welfare schemes: people approach local party leaders to get work done or to access government programmes.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] What are the main challenges faced by political parties in India? Suggest any three reforms.
    Ans:
      Challenges: (1) Lack of internal democracy — power is concentrated in the hands of a few top leaders, there are no regular organisational elections or membership registers, and ordinary members lack information; (2) Dynastic succession — top posts are controlled by family members of the leaders, which is unfair and undemocratic; (3) Money and muscle power — parties nominate candidates who can raise money and sometimes candidates with criminal cases, because winning matters more than principles; (4) Lack of meaningful choice — parties offer very similar policies and ideologies, and leaders shift freely between parties.
      Reforms: the constitution was amended to prevent defection, so elected representatives cannot change parties for ministerial posts or cash; every candidate must file an affidavit declaring property and pending criminal cases, which the Supreme Court made mandatory; parties must hold organisational elections and file income tax returns; and the Election Commission has ordered parties to keep audited accounts and to distribute tickets on merit.

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] What is meant by a legitimate government?
    Ans: A legitimate government is one whose right to rule is accepted by the people. In a democracy this legitimacy comes from free and fair elections, an open and consultative process of decision-making, and the consent of the governed, so people are willing to accept even decisions they disagree with.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] Why is a democratic government regarded as legitimate?
    Ans: Because it is the people's own government, chosen by them through regular, free and fair elections. Even where democracy performs poorly or is slow, it cannot be dismissed as illegitimate, because the authority to govern comes from the consent of the governed. A dictatorship may be more efficient, but it is never the people's own government.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] Why does democracy stand superior to other forms of government in promoting the dignity and freedom of citizens?
    Ans: Because democracy is based on the recognition of the equal worth of all citizens. It gives legal equality to women and to disadvantaged and discriminated castes, and it allows people to claim respect and freedom openly. In a non-democratic state, the claims of women and of disadvantaged groups cannot be raised publicly, so their dignity is not legally protected.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] Why is it wrong to blame democracy for slower economic growth?
    Ans: Because the rate of economic growth depends on many factors other than the form of government — the country's population size, the global economic situation, cooperation from other countries and the economic priorities chosen by the ruling party. Studies show that the difference in growth rates between democracies and dictatorships is small, and among poorer countries democracies have caught up with dictatorships in recent decades.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes: “Democracies always grow faster than dictatorships.” Correct this using the evidence studied.
    Ans:
      The evidence shows that dictatorships had a slightly higher average rate of economic growth than democracies over the period studied.
      The difference is small and is mainly due to poor democracies; among countries at the same level of development, the difference is negligible.
      So the right claim is that democracy does not guarantee faster growth, but it is not a reason to prefer dictatorship, since democracy secures other things such as freedom and accountability.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] Explain how the Right to Information Act, 2005 makes the government more accountable. Give one example of how a citizen can use it.
    Ans:
      The Act gives citizens the right to seek information from government offices about their working, such as files, bills, contracts and the records of public works.
      This makes officials answerable and helps to reduce corruption, because decisions and spending can be examined.
      Example: villagers can ask for the list of workers and payments for a road built in their village to check whether the money was properly spent.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Explain the argument that democracy improves the quality of decision-making.
    Ans:
      Democracy is based on consultation and discussion, so a decision passes through several stages of debate in the legislature, the media and public opinion.
      Because the opposition and the people can criticise, mistakes become visible and can be corrected.
      This makes the process slower than in a dictatorship, but it reduces the chances of rash and arbitrary decisions and produces outcomes that people are more willing to accept.

Q8. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    In country P, the richest 20% of the people receive 55% of the national income and the poorest 20% receive only 4% (illustrative figures).
    
    (a) Is income distributed equally?
    (b) What does this show about the economic outcomes of democracy?
    (c) Why is democracy still preferred?
    Ans:
      (a) No; income is very unequal.
      (b) Democracy does not automatically remove economic inequality; many democracies still have large gaps between rich and poor.
      (c) Because it makes inequality visible and allows the poor to vote, protest and demand change, which other forms of government usually forbid.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    In a village, women and Dalits who were once not allowed to speak at the gram sabha now vote and some have been elected to the panchayat.
    
    (a) Which outcome of democracy does this show?
    (b) Why is the dignity of citizens important for a government?
    (c) Why is democracy better than other systems at securing dignity?
    Ans:
      (a) Democracy promotes equality and dignity: every adult has one equal vote, and weaker sections can claim their rights.
      (b) A government that respects its people’s dignity earns their trust, so its rule is seen as legitimate.
      (c) In a democracy equal rights are recognised in law and struggles by women and disadvantaged groups for respect have been able to succeed.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] Democracy has not removed economic inequality in India. Does that mean democracy has failed? Give reasons.
    Ans:
      No. Democracy's main promise is political equality and the right to change the government, not an instant economic transformation; economic outcomes depend on policies and on how actively citizens use democratic institutions.
      Economic inequality persists because the poor are not organised enough to press their demands, wealthier groups resist redistribution, and governments have often shaped economic policy in favour of powerful interests.
      Democracy has, however, given the poor the vote, the right to information, the right to employment under MGNREGA and a voice in public debate, which no dictatorship guarantees.
      The remedy for unmet expectations is deeper democracy — better organised and informed citizens, greater transparency and accountability — not less of it.

Q11. [Long · 5 marks] 'Democracy is a better form of government than dictatorship.' Support the statement with five arguments.
    Ans:
      It is accountable and responsive — citizens can examine how decisions are made, and the government must attend to their needs.
      It improves the quality of decision-making — consultation and negotiation among many people reduce the chance of rash or irresponsible decisions.
      It provides a method to deal with differences and conflicts — competition is conducted through agreed procedures rather than through force, so social diversity can be accommodated.
      It enhances the dignity of citizens — it recognises the equal worth of all, and gives women and disadvantaged castes legal equality and a voice.
      It allows its own mistakes to be corrected — a democratic government can be replaced at the next election, and public criticism of its functioning is allowed; a dictatorship hides its errors.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] How does democracy produce an accountable, responsive and legitimate government?
    Ans:
      Accountable: citizens have the right to examine the process of decision-making and to hold the government to account; democracy follows norms and procedures, and citizens have legal rights such as the right to information.
      Responsive: the government must attend to the needs and expectations of the people, and if it fails to do so it can be voted out; regular elections force governments to respond.
      Legitimate: the government is elected by the people and is therefore the people's own government, so even a slow or inefficient democratic government is preferred to a dictatorship that is not answerable to anyone.
      Supporting evidence: despite many problems, democracies around the world have survived and people have continued to prefer them, which shows that legitimacy is what ultimately sustains a government.

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] Why is per capita income not an adequate indicator of development?
    Ans: Average income hides inequality: two countries with the same average can differ greatly in how income is distributed. It also counts only goods and services bought for money and ignores health, education, freedom, dignity, security and equality. This is why the Human Development Report uses several indicators together.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] Why is per capita income used to compare countries instead of total income?
    Ans: Because countries have different population sizes, so total income does not tell us what an average person earns. Dividing national income by the population gives per capita income, which shows the average income of a person and allows a fair comparison of the standard of living between countries.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] Kerala has a lower per capita income than Haryana but a lower infant mortality rate. Explain.
    Ans: Because Kerala has an adequate provision of basic health and educational facilities. Almost all children are born safe, are immunised and are given nutrition, and the literacy rate is high, so mothers are better able to care for their children. These facilities are public goods that individuals cannot buy, so a higher income alone does not reduce the infant mortality rate.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] Explain the term sustainability of development with reference to groundwater.
    Ans: Sustainability of development means using resources in a way that meets present needs without exhausting them, so that future generations can meet their own needs. Groundwater is a renewable resource but it is being over-exploited in many parts of India, such as western Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan, Punjab and Haryana, where the water table is falling. Since it takes a long time to recharge, continued overuse is not sustainable and will leave future generations without water.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] Why do different people have different notions of development?
    Ans: Because their life situations are different. A landless rural labourer wants more days of work and better wages, a prosperous farmer wants higher prices for his crops and cheap labour, an industrialist wants more electricity and better roads, and a tribal community wants its forest and land protected. What is development for one person may be destructive for another, so development goals are always contested.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes: “A higher BMI always means a healthier person.” Correct the statement, using the BMI ranges.
    Ans:
      BMI (Body Mass Index) = weight in kg ÷ (height in metres)². It compares weight with height.
      A BMI below 18.5 means the person is undernourished; above 25 means overweight, with a higher risk of illness.
      The healthy range lies between 18.5 and 25, so neither a very low nor a very high BMI is better.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] Country A has a total income of Rs 4,00,000 crore and a population of 20 crore. Country B has a total income of Rs 6,00,000 crore and a population of 40 crore. Which country is better off on average? Show your working.
    Ans:
      Compare per capita income (total income ÷ population), because the populations differ.
      Country A: 4,00,000 ÷ 20 = Rs 20,000 crore per crore people, i.e. Rs 20,000 per person. Country B: 6,00,000 ÷ 40 = Rs 15,000 per person.
      Country A is better off on average even though B has the larger total income. (An average, however, hides differences between people.)

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] What does the Human Development Index measure?
    Ans:
      The HDI is a composite index published annually by the UNDP in the Human Development Report.
      It combines three dimensions: a long and healthy life, measured by life expectancy at birth.
      Knowledge, measured by mean years of schooling and expected years of schooling.
      A decent standard of living, measured by gross national income per capita. Countries are ranked on a scale between 0 and 1, so development is compared by human outcomes and not by income alone.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    The table gives illustrative figures for two states. State X: per capita income Rs 2,20,000, literacy rate 78%, infant mortality rate 35 per 1000. State Y: per capita income Rs 1,40,000, literacy rate 94%, infant mortality rate 10 per 1000.
    
    (a) Which state has the higher per capita income?
    (b) Which state does better on health and education?
    (c) Which state is more developed? Explain.
    Ans:
      (a) State X.
      (b) State Y: its literacy rate is higher and its infant mortality rate is lower.
      (c) State Y is likely to be more developed. Development is not income alone: better health and education give its people a longer, better life, and the Human Development Index counts all three.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    In a village, the government school gives free education and a midday meal, and the ration shop sells rice at a low price. A private clinic in the nearby town charges high fees, so poor families cannot use it.
    
    (a) What are public facilities?
    (b) Why are they important for the poor?
    (c) Give two more examples of public facilities that help development.
    Ans:
      (a) Facilities such as schools, hospitals, water supply and roads that the government provides for everyone, often at low or no cost.
      (b) Poor families cannot afford private services, so public facilities give them access to education, food and health care that they would otherwise lack.
      (c) Public hospitals and primary health centres; safe drinking water and sanitation (or roads and public transport).

Q11. [Long · 4 marks] Explain the importance of public facilities and the idea of sustainable development with examples.
    Ans:
      Public facilities such as schools, health centres, safe drinking water, sanitation, public transport and cheap food through the public distribution system benefit the whole community rather than one buyer, and only the government can provide them to people who cannot pay.
      Without them, even a person with money cannot be healthy or educated, since they need schools, hospitals and clean water shared by all.
      Sustainable development means using resources so that they remain available for future generations.
      Example: the Green Revolution made India self-sufficient in food, but in Punjab the water table is falling because of over-irrigation, so the present generation is borrowing from the future.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] What is the Human Development Report and on what basis does it compare countries?
    Ans:
      It is published every year by the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP). It compares countries on the basis of three things: the educational levels of the people, their health status, and their per capita income.
      These three are combined into the Human Development Index (HDI), a figure between 0 and 1; the closer to 1, the higher the level of human development. Countries are grouped as having very high, high, medium or low human development.
      The report is important because it recognises that income alone does not measure development — a country may be rich but have poor health and education, in which case its people are not truly developed. India has been improving its rank and is placed in the medium human development group.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] The table shows illustrative figures for three countries. A: per capita income Rs 5,00,000, literacy 70%, life expectancy 65 years. B: Rs 2,00,000, literacy 98%, life expectancy 80 years. C: Rs 1,00,000, literacy 60%, life expectancy 62 years. Compare the countries and say which has the highest human development. Why does high income not guarantee it?
    Ans:
      By income: A > B > C. By education (literacy): B (98%) > A (70%) > C (60%). By health (life expectancy): B (80) > A (65) > C (62).
      The Human Development Index combines income, education and health, not income alone.
      So country B has the highest human development: it is ahead of A on two of the three measures.
      Income is only a means: A’s high average income does not by itself give its people better schooling or longer lives.
      An average also hides inequality, so a rich country can still have many poorly educated or unhealthy people.

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] Distinguish between the organised and the unorganised sectors.
    Ans: In the organised sector the work is registered, employees have regular hours, paid holidays, provident fund, gratuity, medical benefits and job security, and labour laws apply. In the unorganised sector the work is small-scale and unregistered, wages are low and irregular, working conditions are poor, and there is no job security, no social security and no protection of labour laws.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] Distinguish between the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors with one example of each.
    Ans: The primary sector includes activities that use natural resources directly, such as agriculture, dairy, fishing and mining. The secondary sector includes activities in which natural products are changed into other forms through manufacturing, such as turning sugarcane into sugar or cotton into cloth. The tertiary sector includes activities that support the primary and secondary sectors without producing goods, such as transport, banking, trade, education and health.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] What is disguised unemployment? Give an example from rural India.
    Ans: Disguised or hidden unemployment is a situation in which more people are employed in a job than are actually required, so that withdrawing some of them does not reduce production. In rural India, a family of five may all work on a small plot of land although three would be enough; if the extra two members leave, output does not fall. They appear employed but are in fact surplus.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] What is GDP and why are only final goods counted in it?
    Ans: Gross Domestic Product is the total value of all final goods and services produced within a country during a particular year. Only final goods are counted because the value of intermediate goods is already included in the value of the final product — the value of wheat is included in the flour, and of the flour in the bread. Counting both would amount to double counting.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] What is MGNREGA 2005 and how does it help to reduce unemployment?
    Ans: The Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act 2005 guarantees 100 days of wage employment in a year to every rural household whose adult members volunteer to do unskilled manual work. If the government fails to provide work within fifteen days, the applicant is entitled to an unemployment allowance. It creates a legal right to work, raises rural incomes, and builds rural assets such as ponds, roads and irrigation channels.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] To find its GDP contribution, a student adds the value of wheat (Rs 100), flour (Rs 140) and bread (Rs 200). Find the mistake and give the correct contribution.
    Ans:
      Mistake: wheat and flour are intermediate goods; their value is already included in the price of the bread.
      Adding them counts the same value several times (double counting).
      Only the final good is counted, so the contribution to GDP is Rs 200, not Rs 440.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] Under MGNREGA 2005, what is the maximum number of days of guaranteed wage employment per rural household in a year? If the daily wage were Rs 200, how much could one household earn at most?
    Ans:
      MGNREGA guarantees 100 days of employment per household in a year to any rural household that asks for work.
      Maximum earning = 100 × Rs 200 = Rs 20,000 per year.
      If the government cannot provide the work, it must pay an unemployment allowance. This makes work a legal right.

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] Explain underemployment with an example and suggest two measures to reduce it.
    Ans:
      Underemployment is the condition in which people work less than they could, or more people are engaged in a job than are needed.
      Example: if five members of a family cultivate a plot that needs only two, the other three add nothing to output; they are disguisedly unemployed, especially during the lean season.
      Measures: providing non-farm employment in villages through MGNREGA and rural industries, and improving irrigation so that two or three crops can be grown in a year.
      Other measures: skill training so that workers can move to manufacturing and services, better roads and markets, and cheap institutional credit.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    In a country, the shares of the three sectors (illustrative figures) are: primary — 15% of GDP and 50% of workers; secondary — 25% of GDP and 20% of workers; tertiary — 60% of GDP and 30% of workers.
    
    (a) Which sector contributes the most to GDP?
    (b) Which sector employs the most people?
    (c) What does this tell us about the income of workers in the primary sector?
    Ans:
      (a) The tertiary sector (60%).
      (b) The primary sector (50% of workers).
      (c) Half the workers share only 15% of GDP, so income per worker in the primary sector is low; many may be underemployed or in disguised unemployment.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    In a city, the railways and the electricity board are run by the government, while the shopping mall and the private hospital are owned by individuals.
    
    (a) Which are public-sector and which are private-sector activities?
    (b) Why does the government run railways and electricity supply?
    (c) State one difference in the aims of the two sectors.
    Ans:
      (a) Public sector: railways and the electricity board. Private sector: the mall and the private hospital.
      (b) These services need very large investment and must be available to everyone at reasonable cost, which private firms may not provide.
      (c) The private sector aims at profit; the public sector aims at the welfare of the public (as well as raising revenue).

Q11. [Long · 4 marks] Explain why the tertiary sector has become the most important sector in India, and why this is not entirely a good sign.
    Ans:
      As incomes rise, people spend a larger share of them on services such as education, health, transport, banking, insurance, tourism and entertainment, so the tertiary sector has become the largest contributor to GDP.
      Services are also needed to support agriculture and industry — transport, storage, banking, communication and trade.
      However, much of the employment in this sector is in low-productivity, unorganised activities such as domestic work, retail and repairs, and the manufacturing sector has not grown fast enough to absorb the workers leaving agriculture.
      So a healthy economy needs balanced growth of all three sectors, especially manufacturing, rather than a jump from farms to low-paid services.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Compare the working conditions in the organised and unorganised sectors.
    Ans:
      Organised sector: enterprises are registered with the government and must follow rules on working hours, overtime, leave and holidays. Workers get regular employment, job security, paid leave, provident fund, medical benefits and other facilities, and are protected by laws such as the Factories Act and the Minimum Wages Act.
      Unorganised sector: small, scattered units largely outside government control. Work is irregular and low paid, there is no job security, no paid leave, no provident fund and no medical benefits, and working conditions are often unsafe.
      Scale: about 90% of the workers in India are in the unorganised sector — small farmers, landless labourers, artisans, street vendors, construction workers and domestic workers.
      What is needed: protection of wages, safety at the workplace, and social security such as health cover and old-age support.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] Why has the tertiary sector become the most important sector in India? Give five reasons.
    Ans:
      Basic services: services such as hospitals, educational institutions, postal and telegraph services, police stations, courts, banks and insurance companies are required by every citizen and by the government, and their expansion is essential for development.
      Growing demand with rising income: as incomes rise, people demand more services such as eating out, tourism, shopping, private hospitals and private schools.
      Support to agriculture and industry: the development of the primary and secondary sectors has led to the development of services such as transport, storage and trade.
      New services: information and communication technology has made new services essential, and India has become a major provider of software and business process services.
      Employment and GDP: the sector now contributes the largest share of GDP and has created a wide range of jobs, from call centres to consultancy, making it the largest producing sector in India.

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (13)

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] List the main functions of money.
    Ans: Money works as a medium of exchange, a unit of account (a common measure of value) and a store of value; it also allows deferred payments. Because it removes the need for a double coincidence of wants, it makes trade, specialisation and economic growth possible.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] Why is money called a medium of exchange?
    Ans: Because it acts as an intermediate step in the exchange of goods and services. In a barter system, exchange requires a double coincidence of wants — each person must want what the other has. Money removes this difficulty: a person can sell his goods for money and use that money to buy whatever he wants, so it mediates between the two parties.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] What is collateral and why do banks ask for it?
    Ans: Collateral is an asset that the borrower owns — such as land, a building, a vehicle, livestock or deposits with banks — and uses as a guarantee to a lender until the loan is repaid. Banks ask for it so that if the borrower fails to repay, the bank can sell the asset to recover its money. Because poor households often have no collateral, they are excluded from formal credit.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] Why do most poor households in India still depend on informal sources of credit?
    Ans: Because banks and cooperatives demand collateral and documentation which poor households do not possess, banks are not present in all rural areas, and small borrowers are treated as risky. Moneylenders and traders, by contrast, lend without collateral and are available in the village, even though they charge much higher rates of interest.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] What is the role of the Reserve Bank of India in supervising banks?
    Ans: The RBI supervises the functioning of formal sources of loans. It ensures that banks maintain a minimum cash balance, that they lend not just to profit-making businesses but also to small cultivators, small-scale industries and small borrowers, and that they submit periodical information to the RBI on how much they are lending, to whom and at what interest rate.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes: “Grameen Bank, founded by Muhammad Yunus in India, lends to poor women.” Find the error and state what Grameen Bank is known for.
    Ans:
      Grameen Bank is in Bangladesh, not India. Muhammad Yunus founded it.
      It is known for giving small loans to poor rural women, mostly through groups and without the collateral that ordinary banks demand.
      It showed that poor borrowers, especially women, can be reliable: repayment rates are high.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] Kishore needs Rs 50,000 for a tailoring shop. A bank offers the loan at 8% a year, but asks for collateral. A moneylender offers it at 5% a month, with no papers. Compare the interest for one year and say which is better for him.
    Ans:
      Bank: 8% of Rs 50,000 = Rs 4,000 a year.
      Moneylender: 5% a month = 60% a year, so 60% of Rs 50,000 = Rs 30,000 a year.
      The bank loan is far cheaper. The moneylender’s loan is easy to get but could trap Kishore in debt. The bank’s collateral demand is the price of cheap credit.

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] Explain how the Reserve Bank of India supervises banks and why this supervision is needed.
    Ans:
      The RBI is the apex bank: it issues currency and keeps the cash reserves of the commercial banks.
      It decides how much of their deposits banks must keep as reserves and how much they may lend, and it sets the rules for interest rates and priority-sector lending.
      It inspects banks, licenses them and can penalise them if they break the rules.
      This supervision is needed because banks hold other people's savings; without it they could lend recklessly and collapse, wiping out depositors' money and unsettling the whole economy.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A self-help group of 15 women saves Rs 100 per member every month. After a year the group has a bank account, and the bank agrees to lend it money.
    
    (a) How much has the group saved in a year?
    (b) Why is the bank willing to lend to the group?
    (c) How does the group reduce dependence on moneylenders?
    Ans:
      (a) 15 × Rs 100 × 12 = Rs 18,000.
      (b) The group has a record of regular savings, meets regularly, takes decisions together and repays under group pressure, so the bank sees little risk. The loan is given in the name of the group.
      (c) Members can borrow small amounts quickly at reasonable interest from the group’s pooled savings, so they need not go to moneylenders.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Meena deposits Rs 10,000 in her bank account and pays her landlord Rs 4,000 by writing a cheque.
    
    (a) Why can the deposit be used as money?
    (b) Is the cheque itself money? Explain.
    (c) Give one advantage of paying by cheque instead of cash.
    Ans:
      (a) The deposit can be withdrawn on demand, so it is a demand deposit, accepted along with currency as a means of payment.
      (b) No. The cheque is only an instruction to the bank to pay; the money is the deposit, which moves from Meena’s account to the landlord’s.
      (c) It is safer than carrying cash and lets large payments be made without handling notes.

Q11. [Long · 4 marks] Explain the terms of credit and how they decide whether a loan helps or harms the borrower.
    Ans:
      The terms of credit are the interest rate, the collateral or security, the documents required and the mode and time of repayment.
      A loan helps when the borrower can repay it out of the extra income it creates — a farmer irrigating land to grow a second crop, or a weaver buying yarn.
      It harms when the activity does not earn enough to repay, when the interest rate is high, or when the borrower loses the collateral and has to borrow again to repay the first loan — a debt trap.
      This is why formal credit from banks and cooperatives at lower rates, with supervision and advice, is better than borrowing from an informal moneylender.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Explain with an example how credit can either help or harm the borrower.
    Ans:
      Where credit helps — Salim: Salim, a shoe manufacturer in Delhi, received a large order for 30,000 pairs of shoes to be delivered within a month. He took a loan from a trader, bought the raw material, completed the order on time, made a profit and repaid the loan. Here credit enabled him to expand production and earn more.
      Where credit harms — Swapna: Swapna, a small farmer in Andhra Pradesh, borrowed ₹ 2,500 from a moneylender at a high rate of interest to cultivate groundnut. The crop failed because of pest attack, and to repay the first loan she had to take another. Over three years her debt grew, and she finally had to sell a part of her land to repay the loan. This is a debt trap — a situation where credit pushes the borrower into a cycle from which she cannot escape.
      Conclusion: whether credit improves a person's situation depends on the risk involved and on whether there is support if something goes wrong. Credit is therefore essential but must be available on fair terms.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] What are self-help groups and how do they help poor borrowers?
    Ans:
      A self-help group is a group of about 15–20 members, usually women belonging to one neighbourhood, who meet regularly and save, each contributing anything from ₹ 25 to ₹ 100 or more.
      Internal lending: members can take small loans from the group itself, at a rate of interest decided by the group but lower than that charged by moneylenders.
      Bank loans: after a year or two of regular savings, the group becomes eligible for a loan from a bank. The loan is sanctioned in the name of the group, and the group is responsible for repayment, so members keep track of each other's borrowing.
      Why it helps: it overcomes the problem of lack of collateral, provides credit at a reasonable rate, makes women financially self-reliant, and creates a platform for women to discuss health, nutrition, domestic violence and education. The Grameen Bank of Bangladesh, started by Muhammad Yunus, demonstrated that poor women are reliable borrowers; today more than 9 crore women in India belong to self-help groups.

CHAPTER 20 · Globalisation and the Indian Economy
Unit: Economics · Understanding Economic Development

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (13)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] Distinguish between foreign trade and foreign investment.
    Ans: Foreign trade is the buying and selling of goods and services across national borders. Foreign investment is the money spent by a company of one country to buy land, buildings, machines or companies in another country; it brings capital, technology and management along with ownership and control.

Q2. [Short · 3 marks] What is a multinational corporation? How do MNCs spread their production?
    Ans: A multinational corporation is a corporation that owns or controls production of goods or services in two or more countries other than its home country. MNCs set up factories in regions where they can get cheap labour, resources and other factors of production, and they keep the large profits with themselves. They spread production by setting up new factories, by buying or acquiring a local company and expanding it, or by placing orders with small producers which are then sold under the MNC's brand name.

Q3. [Short · 3 marks] What is liberalisation and why did the Indian government introduce it in 1991?
    Ans: Liberalisation means removing barriers or restrictions set by the government on trade and on foreign investment. The government introduced it in 1991 because it felt that Indian producers needed to improve the quality of what they produced, and that foreign competition would improve the performance of producers in India, since they would have to improve quality to compete with imports.

Q4. [Short · 3 marks] What are Special Economic Zones and what facilities do they offer?
    Ans: Special Economic Zones are industrial areas set up by the government with world-class facilities such as electricity, water, roads, transport, storage, recreational and educational facilities. Companies that set up production in an SEZ do not have to pay tax for an initial period of five years, and labour laws are applied flexibly. SEZs are located at places such as Noida, Gandhidham, Falta, Cochin, Chennai and Kandla, with the aim of attracting foreign investment.

Q5. [Short · 3 marks] What is fair globalisation and how can it be achieved?
    Ans: Fair globalisation would create opportunities for all and ensure that the benefits of globalisation are shared better, rather than concentrating them in the hands of the rich. It can be achieved by the government supporting small producers with credit, technology and training until they are strong enough to compete, by implementing labour laws properly and protecting workers' rights, by negotiating with other developing countries at the WTO for fairer rules, and by supporting people's movements that campaign against unfair trade rules.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes: “Liberalisation means the government has removed all control over the economy.” Correct the statement and name two measures taken after 1991.
    Ans:
      Liberalisation means removing the barriers and restrictions the government had placed on foreign trade, foreign investment and many industries. The government still regulates areas such as labour, the environment and competition.
      Measures after 1991: import taxes and quotas were reduced, and licensing of industries was largely removed.
      Foreign companies were also allowed to invest more freely in India.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] Explain how foreign trade connects markets, with an example, and how it affects consumers and producers.
    Ans:
      Foreign trade lets producers sell in markets outside their own country and lets consumers buy goods produced elsewhere, so markets in different countries become linked.
      Consumers gain a wider choice and often lower prices; for example, they can choose between an Indian-made and an imported television.
      Producers face competition, which can push them to improve quality, but small producers who cannot match cheap imports may lose business.

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] How does a multinational corporation decide where to set up production? Give examples.
    Ans:
      MNCs look for cheap and skilled labour, cheap raw materials, low production costs, good infrastructure and a large, growing market.
      They are also attracted by favourable government policies such as tax concessions, cheap land and relaxed labour and environmental rules.
      For example, garment makers set up units in Bangladesh and Vietnam where labour is cheap, and sell in the markets of the US and Europe.
      Ford and Maruti set up plants in India to serve the Indian market, and Tata Motors bought Jaguar Land Rover in 2008 to gain technology and markets abroad.

Q9. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A multinational company designs a smartphone in one country, makes its parts in three other countries, assembles it in a fourth and sells it all over the world.
    
    (a) How does the company spread its production?
    (b) Why does it choose countries with cheap labour and a large market?
    (c) How can small local firms gain from such a company?
    Ans:
      (a) It owns or controls production in many countries and places each stage where it is cheapest or most convenient.
      (b) Cheap labour lowers its costs, and a large market gives it many buyers, so profits rise.
      (c) Local firms can become suppliers of parts, raw materials or services and earn from the company’s large orders.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A developing country has removed its barriers to farm imports, as trade rules require. Rich countries, however, continue to give large subsidies to their own farmers.
    
    (a) What is a trade barrier? Give one example.
    (b) Why do the developing country’s farmers lose out?
    (c) Which organisation sets the rules of trade among countries?
    Ans:
      (a) A restriction on trade set by a government, for example a tax on imports or a limit (quota) on the quantity imported.
      (b) Subsidised farm goods from rich countries are cheap, so they undercut local farmers who receive no such help.
      (c) The World Trade Organization (WTO), which began in 1995.

Q11. [Long · 4 marks] What is meant by fair globalisation? Suggest measures to achieve it.
    Ans:
      Fair globalisation means that the benefits of globalisation — investment, technology, jobs, cheaper and better goods — are shared more equally, and that workers, small producers and consumers are protected.
      Measures: labour laws that protect workers' rights, safety and wages, especially for women in the export sector.
      Support for small producers through cheap credit, technology, cooperatives and marketing help.
      A stronger voice for developing countries in the WTO, IMF and World Bank, and fairer rules on agricultural subsidies in rich countries.
      Pressure on MNCs to pay fair wages and to follow environmental and safety standards, and the right of citizens and governments to regulate them.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] How has globalisation affected small producers and workers in India?
    Ans:
      Small producers: many small industries producing items such as batteries, capacitors, plastics, toys, tyres, dairy products, vegetable oil, groundnut oil and matchboxes could not compete with cheap imports and had to shut down, leaving lakhs of workers unemployed. Small farmers have also been affected by cheap agricultural imports from developed countries.
      Workers: globalisation has brought pressure on employers to be 'flexible', so workers are increasingly hired on temporary contracts for short periods, with low wages, long hours and no job security or benefits such as provident fund and medical cover. Labour laws are often not implemented.
      The result: globalisation has made the rich richer and has increased the insecurity of the poor, so many workers' organisations and people's movements have been demanding fairer rules.
      But: in some sectors, such as information technology and services, globalisation has created new employment opportunities, and consumers have benefited from lower prices and wider choice.

Q13. [Long · 5 marks] What is the WTO and what criticism is made of it?
    Ans:
      The World Trade Organization was started in 1995 at the insistence of developed countries. It sets the rules for international trade, and all decisions are taken by consensus among its member countries, of which there are about 164.
      Aim: to liberalise international trade, and to ensure that countries remove trade barriers such as taxes on imports, since it is believed that this will allow countries to specialise and grow.
      Criticism: the rules of the WTO are often biased in favour of developed countries. Developed countries have kept some trade barriers, particularly in agriculture, and have supported their own farmers with subsidies, while asking developing countries to remove their barriers. Developing countries have had to comply with WTO rules, but the benefits have not been shared equally. Hence the demand for fair globalisation.

M1. The World Trade Organization was started in: (a) 1991 (b) 1995 (c) 2000 (d) 2005
    Ans: (b) 1995, at the insistence of developed countries, to set the rules for international trade.

M2. Liberalisation means: (a) increasing import duties (b) nationalising industries (c) banning foreign companies (d) removing barriers set by the government on trade and foreign investment
    Ans: (d) — India liberalised trade and investment in 1991.

M3. Which Indian company acquired the car maker Daewoo Motors India and later bought Jaguar and Land Rover? (a) Maruti (b) Mahindra (c) Tata Motors (d) Sundaram Fasteners
    Ans: (c) Tata Motors — Indian companies have themselves become multinationals by acquiring foreign companies.

M4. A tax on imports is an example of a: (a) subsidy (b) foreign investment (c) special economic zone (d) trade barrier
    Ans: (d) trade barrier. Governments tax imports to make foreign goods costlier and so restrict foreign trade; liberalisation reduced such barriers.

M5. Special Economic Zones are set up by the government mainly to: (a) attract foreign investment by offering world-class facilities (b) ban imports of all goods (c) provide free food to poor people (d) protect small producers from competition
    Ans: (a) attract foreign investment by offering world-class facilities. SEZs offer good roads, power and water, and tax concessions, so that companies, including foreign ones, will produce there.

M6. The World Trade Organization was established in: (a) 1995  (b) 1947  (c) 2000  (d) 1991
    Ans: (a) 1995. It replaced GATT and now has more than 160 member countries.

M7. Which of these is a barrier to foreign trade? (a) free exchange of goods  (b) import duties and quotas  (c) lower transport costs  (d) transfer of technology
    Ans: (b) import duties and quotas. They are imposed to protect domestic producers from foreign competition.

M8. Which Indian company bought the Anglo-Dutch steel maker Corus in 2007? (a) Reliance Industries  (b) Infosys  (c) Tata Steel  (d) Mahindra and Mahindra
    Ans: (c) Tata Steel. It was one of the largest overseas acquisitions by an Indian company.
