CHAPTER 1 · Communication Skills–II
Unit: Part A · Employability Skills
========================================================================

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] Enlist the different methods of communication.
    Ans:  (1) Verbal — oral (speech, phone, meeting) + written (letter, email, report). (2) Non-verbal — expressions, gestures, posture, eye contact, tone. (3) Visual — charts, maps, signals, posters, videos.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Which is the easiest method of communication and why?
    Ans: Non-verbal is the easiest — it needs no language or literacy. Gestures, expressions and tone are understood naturally and work across languages.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] What is feedback? Why is it called the lifeline of communication?
    Ans: Feedback is the receiver's response flowing back to the sender. It is the lifeline because it (1) confirms the message was understood and (2) corrects errors early — completing the two-way loop.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] Explain any two Cs of effective communication with examples.
    Ans: (1) Clear — simple, exact message (“Submit the file by Friday 5 pm”). (2) Courteous — polite words (“please”, “thank you”, “sorry for the delay”).

Q5. [Short · 2 marks] Name any four barriers to effective communication.
    Ans: Physical barriers such as noise or distance; language barriers such as jargon or an unfamiliar accent; psychological barriers such as anger, fear or prejudice; and cultural barriers, where the same word or gesture means different things. Poor listening and the absence of feedback make all of them worse.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] Riya messages her team “do it soon”. Identify the violated C and rewrite the message correctly.
    Ans: Violates Clear (vague, no deadline) and Complete (no what/when). Rewrite: “Please submit the sales sheet by Friday 5 pm on email.” — now clear, complete and courteous.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] A team leader’s juniors look confused by his technical jargon. What two steps should he take?
    Ans: (1) Replace jargon with simple words and show a chart (linguistic barrier). (2) Pause, ask “any doubts?”, and paraphrase replies (feedback + active listening).

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] What are the 7 Cs of effective communication? Explain any three.
    Ans:
      The seven Cs are clear, concise, concrete, correct, coherent, complete and courteous.
      Clear: say what you mean in words the listener understands, and avoid jargon.
      Concise: use only as many words as the message needs, so the point is not lost.
      Complete: give all the facts the receiver needs, including who, what, when and where, so that no further questions are needed.

Q9. [Long · 4 marks] Discuss in detail the verbal method of communication.
    Ans:
      Verbal = communication using words, in two forms.
      Oral (speech, phone, meeting): fast, instant feedback; no permanent record.
      Written (letter, email, report): permanent proof, re-readable, formal; slower, needs literacy.
      Use oral for urgent/personal matters, written when a record is needed.

Q10. [Long · 4 marks] Explain any four barriers to communication with one example and one measure each.
    Ans:
      (1) Physical — class noise → quiet room, working mic.
      (2) Linguistic — French manual → simple words + visuals.
      (3) Cultural — a thumbs-up is rude in some places → learn customs.
      (4) Emotional — anger blocks listening → stay calm, choose the right time.

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Amit presents with zero eye contact, 40 text-packed slides in 10 minutes, and says “no questions, I am getting late”. The manager understands nothing.
    
    (a) Identify four communication mistakes.
    (b) Suggest four fixes.
    Ans:
      (a) No eye contact (poor non-verbal) • overloaded slides (not concise) • too fast (not clear) • no questions (blocks feedback).
      (b) Eye contact + open posture • max 10 slides, one idea each • slow, clear speech • invite questions and summarise.

Q12. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    For the school fest, Class X puts up posters at shops, announces on a loudspeaker, and the principal sends a written notice to parents.
    
    (a) Name the three methods used.
    (b) Why is combining them better than one?
    Ans:
      (a) Posters = visual; loudspeaker = oral/verbal; notice = written.
      (b) Visual attracts, oral spreads fast, written is proof — together they cover more audiences with both speed and a record.

B. MCQs (10)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. Which part of communication converts an idea into words or signs? (a) Encoding (b) Receiving (c) Decoding (d) Feedback
    Ans: (a) Encoding. Encoding prepares the sender's idea as a message; decoding interprets it.

M2. A loud generator prevents a worker from hearing instructions. The barrier is: (a) physical (b) linguistic (c) cultural (d) emotional
    Ans: (a) Physical — environmental noise blocks the message.

M3. Which of the 7 Cs means “to the point, no extra words”? (a) Clear (b) Concise (c) Concrete (d) Courteous
    Ans: (b) Concise.

M4. “An honest boy” takes which article? (a) a (b) the (c) no article (d) an
    Ans: (d) an — use an before a vowel sound; h is silent in honest.

M5. A student's puzzled expression after an explanation is: (a) non-verbal feedback (b) written encoding (c) a channel (d) a language rule
    Ans: (a) Non-verbal feedback.

M6. Which sentence is complete? (a) Do that work. (b) Submit the signed form to Room 4 by Friday noon. (c) The form is important. (d) Submit it soon.
    Ans: (b) — task, place and deadline are all present.

M7. Paralanguage refers to: (a) body posture only (b) charts and maps (c) tone, pitch and speed of voice (d) grammar rules
    Ans: (c) Tone, pitch and speed of voice.

M8. The easiest method of communication is: (a) written (b) oral (c) non-verbal (d) email
    Ans: (c) Non-verbal — needs no literacy.

M9. The communication process is complete only when: (a) the message is written  (b) the receiver gives feedback  (c) the sender speaks  (d) the channel is chosen
    Ans: (b) the receiver gives feedback. Feedback tells the sender whether the message was understood.

M10. Which method of communication carries no body language or tone of voice? (a) face-to-face talk  (b) video call  (c) group discussion  (d) written communication
    Ans: (d) written communication. That is why written messages must be especially clear.

CHAPTER 2 · Self-Management Skills–II
Unit: Part A · Employability Skills
========================================================================

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] What is self-management? Give two of its benefits.
    Ans: Controlling one's emotions, thoughts and behaviour to reach goals. Benefits: better work quality, less stress, meeting deadlines, less need for supervision.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Differentiate eustress and distress with one example each.
    Ans: Eustress is helpful pressure — a timed practical that makes you focus. Distress is harmful — so much overtime that errors increase and sleep is lost.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] Distinguish between stress and a stressor.
    Ans: Stress is the way the body and mind react to pressure, while a stressor is the event or situation that triggers it. An examination, a deadline, a family problem or a noisy workplace can each be a stressor; the resulting worry, headache or loss of sleep is the stress.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] What is self-regulation, and how does it differ from self-motivation? Give one example of each.
    Ans:
      Self-regulation is controlling your emotions and behaviour to stay on track — for example, closing social media during study hours despite the urge to scroll.
      Self-motivation is generating your own drive to start and continue — for example, setting a personal target of finishing ten Calc rows before lunch.
      In short: motivation starts the engine, regulation keeps it on the road.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] Meera has three tasks: a 5 pm file, a messy desk, and a social-media reel. Using urgent/important, what should she do first and why?
    Ans: The 5 pm file is urgent and important — do it first. The desk is important but not urgent — schedule it. The reel is neither — drop it until the file is in.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes SMART as “Specific, Achievable, Measurable, Time-bound, Realistic”. Is the order correct? Give the correct expansion and turn “I want to type faster” into a SMART goal.
    Ans:
      The letters must follow the order of the word: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic (or Relevant), Time-bound. The student swapped A and M and put T before R.
      “Type faster” is vague: it is not specific, measurable or time-bound.
      SMART version: “Type 35 words per minute with at most 2 errors in a 5-minute test by the end of this month.”

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Suggest three ways of managing stress and building self-motivation.
    Ans:
      Plan the day, break big tasks into small ones and do the hardest first, so that the work never feels endless.
      Look after the body: sleep enough, exercise, breathe deeply and eat regularly, because a tired body exaggerates every problem.
      Set specific goals, track them, reward yourself when a goal is met, and talk to someone you trust instead of keeping worries inside.

Q8. [Long · 3 marks] Explain the four steps of time management (list → prioritise → one chunk → review) with a study-evening example.
    Ans:
      List — write down everything for the evening: finish the Writer practical, revise ten formulas, pack the bag.
      Prioritise — rank by urgent/important: the practical due tomorrow comes first, packing the bag last.
      One chunk — work on the top task in a single unbroken block instead of switching every ten minutes.
      Review — at the end, tick off what is done and carry only the remainder forward.

Q9. [Long · 4 marks] Explain SMART goals with a data-entry example.
    Ans: Specific (which file), Measurable (how many records), Achievable (within skill), Realistic (hours available), Time-bound (by 5 pm). Example: “Enter 80 customer rows in Calc with zero blanks by 4 pm today.”

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Ravi’s hands shake before every practical, he skips breakfast and then makes typing errors.
    
    (a) Name the type of stress.
    (b) List four management steps.
    (c) Write one SMART goal for his next practical.
    Ans:
      (a) Distress — it is harming performance.
      (b) Breakfast, 4–7 breathing, a 5-minute practice file, a checklist, sleep 7 hours.
      (c) “Complete the Base table of 10 records with primary key set, in 25 minutes, with zero errors, on Friday.”

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Asha knows she is good at organising events but weak at public speaking, so she joins the debate club. On her first day she feels nervous, but she takes deep breaths and goes on stage anyway.
    
    (a) Which skill does knowing her strengths and weaknesses show?
    (b) How does joining the debate club show self-motivation?
    (c) Which skill is shown when she calms herself and goes on stage?
    Ans:
      (a) Self-awareness: understanding one’s own strengths, weaknesses, feelings and values.
      (b) She sets herself a goal to improve and acts on it without being told; the drive comes from inside her.
      (c) Self-regulation: controlling her emotions and behaviour in a situation.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Describe five ways in which a student can manage stress, giving an example of each.
    Ans:
      Plan and prioritise: make a to-do list and do the important task first, so exam work does not pile up.
      Exercise and breathing: a 20-minute walk, yoga or deep breathing calms the body.
      Sleep and food: sleeping 7–8 hours and eating on time keeps the mind fresh before a test.
      Talk it out: sharing a worry with a friend, parent or counsellor stops it from growing.
      Hobbies and breaks: music or sport after study gives the mind a rest; positive self-talk also helps.

B. MCQs (8)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. SMART: the M stands for: (a) Magical (b) Maximum (c) Monthly (d) Measurable
    Ans: (d) Measurable.

M2. A hobby after study is mainly: (a) a waste (b) a stress-management method (c) self-employment (d) a barrier
    Ans: (b) a stress-management method.

M3. Knowing your own strengths is: (a) self-awareness (b) encoding (c) a physical barrier (d) green skill
    Ans: (a) self-awareness.

M4. Which goal is SMART? (a) I will study more (b) I will become famous (c) I will type 40 correct rows in Calc by 3 pm (d) I will try
    Ans: (c).

M5. Which of the following is an example of self-regulation? (a) Feeling angry at a remark but waiting and replying politely later (b) Setting a goal to score 90% in the exam (c) Listing one’s strengths and weaknesses (d) Being able to see the cause of someone else’s stress
    Ans: (a) Feeling angry at a remark but waiting and replying politely later. Self-regulation means controlling one’s emotions and actions; the other options show self-motivation and self-awareness.

M6. A task that is important but not urgent should be: (a) done at once, before anything else (b) given to somebody else (c) scheduled for a fixed time (d) dropped from the list
    Ans: (c) scheduled for a fixed time. In the urgent/important method, important but not urgent tasks are planned for later so that they do not become urgent.

M7. Self-awareness means: (a) only knowing other people  (b) working without help  (c) following orders  (d) knowing your own strengths, weaknesses and feelings
    Ans: (d) knowing your own strengths, weaknesses and feelings. It is the first step of self-management.

M8. The useful stress that pushes you to perform better is called: (a) distress  (b) eustress  (c) burnout  (d) depression
    Ans: (b) eustress. Distress is the harmful kind that lowers performance and health.

CHAPTER 3 · ICT Skills–II
Unit: Part A · Employability Skills
========================================================================

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 1 mark] What is an operating system? Give two examples.
    Ans: System software that manages hardware and runs programs. Examples: Windows, Linux, Android.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Differentiate a file and a folder with one example each.
    Ans: A file stores data (marks.ods). A folder stores files and other folders (Class10/IT/).

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] What is an operating system? Name two examples.
    Ans: An operating system is the system software that manages the computer's memory, processor, disks and devices and lets the user run application programs. Examples are Windows, Linux, macOS and Android.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] What is the Recycle Bin, and how is restoring a file different from deleting it permanently?
    Ans: The Recycle Bin is a holding area where deleted files wait until it is emptied. Restoring returns the file to its original folder, so nothing is lost. Deleting permanently (emptying the Bin, or Shift + Delete) removes the file beyond easy recovery — which is why important files should be backed up before the Bin is emptied.

Q5. [Short · 2 marks] List four basic care rules that protect a computer from physical damage and data loss.
    Ans:
      Keep the machine dust-free and away from food and drink, which damage keyboards and circuits.
      Use a surge protector so voltage spikes do not harm the hardware.
      Shut down through the OS command instead of pulling the plug, which can corrupt files.
      Run updated antivirus software and keep a backup of important work.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] Amit eats chips over the keyboard and never shuts the PC down. List three risks and three care steps.
    Ans: Risks: crumbs jam keys, oil damage, overheating, data loss. Care: no food/drink, proper shutdown, dust-free vents, surge protector, backups.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] A student says, “Windows, Linux and LibreOffice Writer are all operating systems.” Correct the statement.
    Ans:
      Windows and Linux are operating systems: system software that controls the hardware and lets other programs run.
      LibreOffice Writer is application software, a word processor used to create documents; it runs on top of an operating system.
      Other operating systems are macOS and Android; other applications are Calc and a web browser.

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] How would you organise files and folders so that your work is always easy to find?
    Ans:
      Make one folder for each subject or project, and inside it one folder per term or per type of work.
      Give every file a short, descriptive name with the date, such as maths_notes_2026-08-12.
      Keep the folder tree shallow — no more than two or three levels — and delete or archive old versions.
      Keep a backup copy on an external drive or in the cloud, and run an antivirus scan on anything you download.

Q9. [Short · 3 marks] Why should files be given clear names and kept in subject-wise folders? Illustrate with a school-project example.
    Ans:
      Clear names and folders make work findable: a file named aaa.odt on a crowded Desktop is easily lost or overwritten.
      Example — a project split as Project/notes.odt, Project/images/, Project/final-report.odt lets any part be found in seconds.
      Good organisation also protects against loss: one folder is easy to back up, scattered files are not.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Meera’s project file vanished after a power cut. She had saved only on the Desktop as aaa.odt.
    
    (a) Two file-management mistakes.
    (b) Two care/maintenance steps that would have helped.
    Ans:
      (a) No backup; a meaningless file name; Desktop-only copy.
      (b) Surge protector / UPS; Save As into a named folder; weekly backup on a pen drive or cloud.

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Rohan creates a letter in Writer, a budget in Calc and a database in Base. He also wants to give his teacher a copy of the letter that opens in Microsoft Word.
    
    (a) Give the default file extension for each of the three files.
    (b) What is the difference between Save and Save As?
    (c) How can he make a Word-compatible copy?
    Ans:
      (a) Writer: .odt; Calc: .ods; Base: .odb.
      (b) Save overwrites the current file; Save As lets you choose a new name, folder or file type and keeps the original.
      (c) Use File → Save As and choose the Word file type (.docx) in the “Save as type” box.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Describe five basic file-management operations in an operating system and give the shortcut or method for each.
    Ans:
      Create a folder: right-click an empty space and choose New → Folder, then type its name.
      Rename: select the file and press F2 (or right-click → Rename).
      Copy: select the file, press Ctrl + C, open the destination and press Ctrl + V.
      Move: select the file, press Ctrl + X, open the destination and press Ctrl + V (or drag it there).
      Delete: press Delete to send it to the Recycle Bin, from where it can be restored; Shift + Delete removes it permanently.

CHAPTER 4 · Entrepreneurial Skills–II
Unit: Part A · Employability Skills
========================================================================

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] Who is an entrepreneur? Give two qualities.
    Ans: A person who spots a need, takes a risk and organises resources to start a venture. Qualities: initiative and perseverance (or creativity, self-confidence).

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] State two myths about entrepreneurship and correct them.
    Ans: (1) “Born, not made” — skills can be learned. (2) “Needs huge capital” — many start with a small idea and grow.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] List any four qualities an entrepreneur needs.
    Ans: Self-confidence, the willingness to take calculated risks, initiative and perseverance, the ability to plan and organise, and the readiness to learn from failure and try again.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] What is a social entrepreneur? How does the aim differ from that of a business entrepreneur?
    Ans: A social entrepreneur starts an enterprise mainly to solve a social problem — for example, cheap solar lamps for villages — rather than to maximise profit. A business entrepreneur also creates value but measures success primarily in profit and growth. Both take risks and organise resources; they differ in what counts as the return.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] Neha notices students waiting 40 minutes for a shared printer. She starts a paid print-and-bind desk. Which entrepreneurial qualities is she using?
    Ans: Opportunity-spotting, initiative, risk-taking (she invests in a printer), problem-solving and customer focus.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A student says, “Every shopkeeper is an entrepreneur.” Correct this using the ideas of risk and innovation.
    Ans:
      An entrepreneur starts a new venture, brings something new (a product, service or way of working), organises the resources and bears the risk of loss.
      A shopkeeper who only resells goods in the usual way, with no new idea, is a trader or a small business owner.
      A shopkeeper who starts a new kind of service, such as home delivery that no one offered before, takes a risk and innovates, and so acts as an entrepreneur.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] What functions does an entrepreneur perform in running an enterprise?
    Ans:
      Spotting an opportunity and turning it into a product or service people will pay for.
      Arranging the money, buying raw materials, machines and premises, and hiring and training workers.
      Organising production, keeping accounts and marketing the product.
      Bearing the risk of loss, and taking the decisions that keep the enterprise alive.

Q8. [Long · 3 marks] Explain the role of entrepreneurs in society with reference to jobs, products and local resources.
    Ans:
      Jobs — every new enterprise hires people, from a bakery's helpers to a startup's designers.
      Products — entrepreneurs turn needs into goods and services, such as affordable school stationery or repair shops.
      Local resources — they use nearby material and talent, for example fruit from local orchards in a jam unit, keeping wealth in the area.

Q9. [Long · 4 marks] Explain four functions of an entrepreneur with a bakery example.
    Ans: Identify demand for fresh bread; arrange shop, oven and staff; take the risk of unsold stock; market via samples and a board; keep accounts. Each function maps to one quality: opportunity-spotting, organising, risk-taking, selling.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A poster says “Only rich, highly educated people can start a business.”
    
    (a) Name the myth.
    (b) Give two counter-examples from daily life.
    (c) State two roles of entrepreneurship in society.
    Ans:
      (a) Myth of huge capital / only the highly educated.
      (b) A tiffin service from a home kitchen; a tuition class in a garage.
      (c) Jobs, new products, local resources used, local problems solved.

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Amit notices that farmers near his village lose money because vegetables spoil on the way to the city. He buys a van with a cooling unit, collects vegetables directly from farmers, sells them to city shops and hires two local youths.
    
    (a) Which entrepreneurial qualities does Amit show?
    (b) Name two functions he performs as an entrepreneur.
    (c) How does his enterprise help society?
    Ans:
      (a) He spots a need (opportunity recognition), is innovative in finding a solution and is willing to take a financial risk.
      (b) He organises resources (the van, workers, suppliers) and bears the risk of the business.
      (c) It creates jobs, reduces food wastage and increases the farmers’ income.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Describe five steps in setting up a small enterprise.
    Ans:
      Step 1: generate and evaluate a business idea that meets a real need.
      Step 2: study the market: who the customers are, what they will pay and who the competitors are.
      Step 3: prepare a business plan covering the product, costs, expected sales and profit.
      Step 4: arrange the money (capital) from savings, family or a loan, and choose a suitable location.
      Step 5: complete legal formalities such as registration and licences, then start and review the business regularly.

B. MCQs (8)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. An entrepreneur primarily: (a) avoids all risk (b) only works for a salary (c) takes calculated risk to create value (d) copies without a customer
    Ans: (c).

M2. Which is a quality, not a function? (a) perseverance (b) arranging capital (c) hiring staff (d) marketing
    Ans: (a) perseverance.

M3. A social entrepreneur mainly aims to: (a) ignore people (b) avoid accounts (c) work without a product (d) solve a social problem, not only maximise profit
    Ans: (d).

M4. “Entrepreneurs are born, not made” is: (a) a fact (b) a 7 C (c) a myth (d) an OS function
    Ans: (c) a myth.

M5. The first step in starting an enterprise is usually to: (a) register the business (b) find and evaluate a business idea (c) hire employees (d) print the visiting cards
    Ans: (b) find and evaluate a business idea. Everything else depends on having an idea that meets a need and can make a profit.

M6. The money needed to start and run a business is called: (a) capital (b) profit (c) revenue (d) tax
    Ans: (a) capital. Capital is the funds an entrepreneur invests in the enterprise; profit is what remains after costs.

M7. A person who starts a business, organises it and bears its risks is a/an: (a) manager  (b) employee  (c) customer  (d) entrepreneur
    Ans: (d) entrepreneur. A manager runs someone else's business for a salary.

M8. Which of these is a myth about entrepreneurs? (a) they take risks  (b) they are born, not made  (c) they work hard  (d) they need capital
    Ans: (b) they are born, not made. Entrepreneurial skills such as planning, selling and record-keeping can be learned.

CHAPTER 5 · Green Skills–II
Unit: Part A · Employability Skills
========================================================================

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 1 mark] Define sustainable development.
    Ans: Development that meets present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] What are green skills? Give two examples.
    Ans: Skills that help a person live and work in an environmentally friendly way. Examples: waste segregation, saving electricity, water conservation.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] What is sustainable development?
    Ans: It is development that meets the needs of the present without reducing the ability of future generations to meet their own needs — using resources carefully, keeping pollution low and protecting ecosystems while the economy grows.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] What is a green economy? State its three features.
    Ans: A green economy is one that improves human well-being while reducing environmental risks. Its three features are: low carbon emissions, efficient use of resources, and inclusive growth that does not leave the poor behind.

Q5. [Short · 2 marks] Arrange Reduce, Reuse, Recycle in order of preference and justify the order with one example each.
    Ans:
      First Reduce — preventing waste is best: carry a cloth bag instead of taking a plastic one each time.
      Then Reuse — using again costs nothing extra: refill the same water bottle for months.
      Last Recycle — reprocessing still needs energy: send worn-out notebooks for paper recycling only when reuse is over.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A computer lab leaves 20 PCs on overnight. Suggest three green practices and one green skill the lab assistant needs.
    Ans: Shut down PCs, use sleep mode, switch off AC/lights, enable duplex printing. Skill: energy-conservation habit / e-waste handling.

Q7. [Application · 3 marks] A student defines sustainable development as “using no natural resources so that nothing is wasted”. Correct the definition.
    Ans:
      Sustainable development means meeting the needs of the present without harming the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
      It is about wise use of resources, not zero use: we still use water, energy and materials, but carefully.
      Examples are using renewable energy, saving water and recycling.

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] What is a style in Writer? Name any four categories.
    Ans: A named set of formatting that can be reused. Categories: Paragraph, Character, Page, Frame, List, Table.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] What is Fill Format mode?
    Ans: A Styles-window tool that copies the selected style onto each paragraph (or object) you click, like a format paintbrush.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] Why are styles better than formatting each heading by hand?
    Ans: A style stores the whole set of formatting — font, size, spacing, alignment — in one named item. Change the style once and every paragraph using it changes, the document stays consistent, and headings styled with Heading 1, 2 and 3 can be collected automatically into a table of contents.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] What is the difference between a paragraph style and a character style? Give one example of each.
    Ans:
      A paragraph style formats a whole paragraph at once — for example, Heading 1 sets the font, size and spacing of every chapter title.
      A character style formats only selected words inside a paragraph — for example, an Emphasis style that italicises a single term without touching the rest of the line.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A 20-page report uses Bold 16 pt for titles. The examiner wants Heading 1 in navy. What should the student do instead of selecting each title?
    Ans: Create or modify Heading 1 (navy, size, spacing), then Fill Format / select each title and apply Heading 1. Later, one modify updates all.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A student wants one heading in a 30-page report to be red, so she changes the colour in the style “Heading 1”. Every heading in the report turns red. Why did this happen, and how could she have changed only one?
    Ans:
      A style is a stored set of formatting applied to all paragraphs that use it; changing the style changes every paragraph in that style.
      To change only one heading, select it and apply the colour as direct formatting (or give that heading its own new style).
      Use “Update Style” or Modify only when you want a change to apply across the whole document.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Describe how to create and then apply a paragraph style in LibreOffice Writer.
    Ans:
      Format one paragraph exactly as you want it, then open the Styles window with F11 and click the New Style from Selection button.
      Give the style a name such as ‘MyBodyText' and click OK.
      To apply it, click anywhere in the paragraph you want to change and double-click the style name under Paragraph Styles.
      To change the whole document, right-click the style, choose Modify and set the font, size and spacing once.

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] What is ‘Update Style’? Describe a situation in which it saves more time than re-applying a style.
    Ans:
      Update Style takes the manual formatting of the current paragraph and writes it back into the style definition, so every paragraph using that style changes at once.
      Situation — a report has forty Heading 2 titles in black; the teacher wants them navy. Format one title navy, click Update Style, and all forty change in one step instead of forty.

Q9. [Long · 4 marks] Why should a long report use styles instead of manual formatting?
    Ans: One change updates every heading; ToC can be generated; the look stays consistent; less chance of mixed fonts; templates become reusable.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:

    (b) Steps to fix it.
    (c) How will this help next time she changes the colour of all headings?

      (a) Titles used direct formatting, not Heading styles.
      (b) Apply Heading 1/2, then Insert → Table of Contents.
      (c) Modify Heading 1 once — every heading and the ToC pick up the new colour.

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A report has a title, three chapters and sub-sections. The student wants chapter titles in Heading 1, sub-sections in Heading 2, and body text in a style of 12 pt, justified, with 1.5 line spacing.
    
    (a) Which category of style is used for each?
    (b) How can she create the body-text style?
    (c) Why is this better than formatting by hand?
    Ans:
      (a) All three are paragraph styles, because they format whole paragraphs.
      (b) Format a paragraph as wanted, open the Styles sidebar (F11) and choose New Style from Selection, then give it a name such as Body.
      (c) Changing a style once updates every paragraph using it, and Heading styles can be used to build the table of contents automatically.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Explain how to set the page margins, add a header and insert page numbers in a Writer document using styles.
    Ans:
      Margins and header belong to the page style: open Format → Page Style (or the Page Style in the Styles sidebar).
      On the Page tab, set the left, right, top and bottom margins.
      On the Header tab, switch the header on, then click inside the header area on the page and type the text, for example “Annual Report”.

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] Name any four text-wrapping options in Writer.
    Ans: Writer offers these wrap options: None, Before, After, Parallel, Optimal, Through and In Background (any four). Optimal is the default — text flows around the object; Through lets text pass over it; In Background turns the picture into a watermark that sits behind the text. Choose Optimal for a report and In Background for a letterhead logo.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] What does grouping do?
    Ans: It joins selected drawing objects so they move, resize and copy as a single object.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] What is text wrapping and when is it useful?
    Ans: Wrapping decides how text flows around a picture. It is useful when a small picture has to sit inside a paragraph: the text runs beside or around the image instead of leaving a large empty space above and below it, so the page looks filled and balanced.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] What is an image caption, and why should figures be captioned and numbered in a report?
    Ans: A caption is a numbered label under an image, such as ‘Figure 3: School garden’. Captions let the text refer to figures precisely (‘see Figure 3’) instead of vaguely (‘the picture above’), and automatic numbering keeps the sequence correct when images are added or moved.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] An image covers the heading of a report. Which two settings would you change first?
    Ans: Wrap (Optimal or After) and arrangement (Send to Back) or move the anchor to the correct paragraph.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A student drags the side handle of a photograph, and faces in it look unnaturally wide. What went wrong, and how can the photo be resized without distortion?
    Ans:
      Dragging a side handle changes only the width, so the picture is stretched out of proportion.
      To scale it proportionally, drag a corner handle while holding the Shift key.
      Alternatively, open Position and Size and tick “Keep ratio” before changing the width or height.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] List the steps to insert an image and set its position in a Writer document.
    Ans:

      Use Format → Image → Properties to set the size, the position, the cropping and whether the image is anchored to the page, the paragraph or the character.
      Drag the picture to its place and, if you have several drawing objects, select them and choose Format → Group so that they move together.

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] Distinguish between anchoring an image ‘To Paragraph’ and ‘As Character’, with one use-case each.
    Ans:
      To Paragraph — the image attaches to a paragraph and moves with it, while text wraps around freely; use it for a photo beside a paragraph of a report.
      As Character — the image sits inside the text line like a big letter and moves with the words; use it for a small icon or symbol within a sentence.

Q9. [Long · 4 marks] Explain anchoring of images in Writer.
    Ans: Anchor decides what the image sticks to: To Paragraph (moves with the para), To Character, As Character (in the text line), To Page (fixed on that page). Choose To Paragraph + Optimal wrap for reports.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Describe the steps to insert a picture in a Writer document, resize it, crop it, set the text wrap and add a caption.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] What is a document template?

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] How is a table of contents generated automatically in Writer?

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] What is the difference between a template (.ott) and an ordinary Writer document (.odt)?
    Ans: A template is a reusable starting point: it stores styles, margins, headers and layout that every new document based on it inherits. An ordinary document stores one finished piece of work. Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A teacher wants 40 identical permission letters with only the parent name changing. Which Writer feature, and what two files does she need?

Q9. [Long · 4 marks] Explain mail merge and its advantages.

CHAPTER 9 · Scenarios and Goal Seek (Calc)
Unit: Part B · Electronic Spreadsheet (Advanced)
========================================================================

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] What is Goal Seek in Calc?
    Ans: A what-if tool: you set the result you want in a formula cell and Calc finds the input value that produces it.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] What is a scenario?
    Ans: A named set of values for specified cells so you can switch between cases (best/worst) without retyping.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] What is the difference between a scenario and Goal Seek?
    Ans: A scenario is a named set of input values that you save and switch between to compare outcomes, such as best case, worst case and expected case. Goal Seek works the other way: you state the result you want and Calc finds the single input value that produces it.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] What is Solver in Calc, and how does it differ from Goal Seek?
    Ans: Solver finds input values that produce a target result, like Goal Seek — but it can change many cells at once and respect constraints (for example, quantities that must stay whole numbers). Goal Seek changes exactly one cell with no constraints. Use Goal Seek for one unknown, Solver for many.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A shop wants profit of ₹8,000. Profit is =B2*B3-B4 (price × qty − cost). Which tool, and which cell is “By changing” if qty and cost are fixed?
    Ans:
      Use Goal Seek (Tools → Goal Seek). Set cell = the profit cell, To value = 8000, and By changing cell = the price cell B2.
      Goal Seek works backwards: instead of asking what the profit is, it asks what input would produce the profit you want, and it tries values until the formula cell reaches 8000.
      Only one input cell can be changed, so the quantity and the cost must stay fixed while the price is adjusted.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A student tries to use Goal Seek to find the best mix of three products under limits on labour hours and material, and Calc cannot do it. What is wrong, and which tool should she use?
    Ans:
      Goal Seek changes one input cell until one formula cell reaches a target value.
      Finding the best mix of several variables under limits (constraints) needs Solver.
      So she should use Solver, setting the target cell, the variable cells and the constraints.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Describe how to use Goal Seek in Calc, with an example.
    Ans:
      Build the sheet so that the result cell contains a formula, for example the EMI for a loan in B5.
      Choose Tools → Goal Seek and fill in the dialog: Formula Cell B5, Target Value 8000, Variable Cell B2 (the interest rate or the loan amount).
      Click OK; Calc changes the variable cell step by step until the formula gives the target, and reports whether it succeeded.
      This is quicker than trial and error when only one input is unknown.

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] What is the ‘Group’ feature in Calc? Explain with a monthly-expense sheet example.
    Ans:
      Group (Data → Define Range → Group) bundles rows or columns so they can be collapsed to a single line or expanded again with one click.
      Example — a yearly sheet with twelve monthly blocks: group each month's daily rows, collapse all twelve, and only the monthly totals stay visible for review.
      Unlike hiding, grouping shows a clear plus/minus control, so hidden detail is never forgotten.

Q9. [Long · 4 marks] Differentiate Consolidate and Subtotals.
    Ans: Consolidate combines several ranges/sheets into one summary (often another sheet). Subtotals insert summary rows inside one sorted list (sum of each group). Consolidate is across blocks; Subtotals is inside a table.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Four quarterly sheets have the same layout of expenses. The owner wants one yearly total that updates if a quarter changes.
    
    (a) Which tool?
    (b) Two settings to tick/choose.
    (c) Why not just type the four totals by hand?
    Ans:
      (a) Data → Consolidate.
      (b) Function Sum; add each range; Link to source data.
      (c) Manual totals go stale; a link updates and reduces typing errors.

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A school fest committee plans ticket sales in three situations. Best case: price Rs 100, 500 tickets. Normal case: Rs 80, 400 tickets. Worst case: Rs 60, 250 tickets. Revenue = price × tickets.
    
    (a) Find the revenue in each case.
    (b) Which Calc feature stores the three sets of inputs?
    (c) Why should each be given a name?
    Ans:
      (a) Best: 100 × 500 = Rs 50,000; Normal: 80 × 400 = Rs 32,000; Worst: 60 × 250 = Rs 15,000.
      (b) Scenarios: each is a named set of input values that can be shown on the sheet.
      (c) The names let the committee switch between the cases quickly and see how the output changes.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] A teacher has the marks of 30 students with columns Name, Section and Marks. Describe how to use Subtotals to get the average marks of each section.
    Ans:
      Step 1: sort the data by the Section column (Data → Sort), so that students of one section are together.
      Step 2: select the data range and choose Data → Subtotals.
      Step 3: on the “1st Group” tab, group by Section, tick Marks under “Calculate subtotals for” and choose the function Average.
      Step 4: click OK; a subtotal row appears after each section, and the outline buttons at the left show or hide the details.
      Sorting first is essential: if the section values are not together, Subtotals gives wrong groups.

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] What is a macro? Give one use in Calc.
    Ans: A saved list of commands that can be replayed. Use: apply the same marksheet formatting every term.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Write the menu path to record a macro in Calc.
    Ans: Tools → Macros → Record Macro, perform the steps, Stop Recording, name the macro.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] What is a macro and why is it used?
    Ans: A macro is a recorded sequence of commands that can be replayed with one click or one shortcut. It saves time on work you repeat often — formatting a daily report, cleaning imported data, or applying the same page setup to many sheets — and removes the risk of forgetting a step.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] What is LibreOffice Basic, and how is it connected with macros?
    Ans: LibreOffice Basic is the programming language in which Calc macros are stored. When you record a macro, Calc translates your clicks and keystrokes into Basic code; running the macro replays that code. You never need to read the code for the exam, but knowing where it lives explains why a macro file can carry a virus.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] Every Monday you set print area A1:G40, landscape, header “Weekly Sales”. How does a macro help?
    Ans: Record those page-setup steps once. Each Monday, Run Macro — one click instead of eight menu steps, fewer missed settings.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A student starts recording a macro, then clicks around to find the menu and clicks a few wrong cells before doing the task. When the macro is replayed, it does odd things. Why, and how can this be avoided?
    Ans:
      The recorder copies every action, including the wrong clicks, so replaying the macro repeats them.
      Plan the exact steps before pressing Record; rehearse them once without recording.
      Record only what is needed (Tools → Macros → Record Macro … Stop Recording); if a mistake is recorded, delete the macro and record it again.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] When should you not use a macro? Give three situations.
    Ans:
      Do not record a macro for a task you will do only once; learning the steps takes longer than doing the job.
      Avoid macros for work that changes each time and cannot be reduced to fixed steps.
      Never open macros in a file from an unknown sender: macro viruses can damage files and spread to other documents.
      Where a built-in formula, style or tool does the job, use it instead — it is easier for others to check.

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] Describe the steps to run a recorded macro and to delete one you no longer need.
    Ans:
      To run — Tools → Macros → Run Macro, select the library and module where the macro was saved, choose its name, and press Run.
      To delete — Tools → Macros → Organise Macros → LibreOffice Basic, select the macro, and press Delete.
      Tip — save practice macros inside the document (not in My Macros) so they travel with the file to the examiner's machine.

Q9. [Long · 4 marks] Explain macro security levels and a safe practice for students.
    Ans: Very High / High / Medium / Low control whether unsigned macros run. Students should keep High or Medium, enable macros only on their own practical files, and never open a macro-enabled sheet from an unknown chat.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A classmate sends marks.ods that auto-runs a macro on open.
    
    (a) What should you do first?
    (b) When is a macro a good idea in your own file?
    (c) One limitation of recorded macros.
    Ans:
      (a) Do not enable macros until you trust the source; check Security level.
      (b) When the same clicks are repeated often on your own data.
      (c) They record extra clicks; they may break if the sheet layout changes.

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Every Friday a clerk makes the header row of a report bold, sets the font to 12 pt and adds a heading “Weekly Report”.
    
    (a) Which feature can automate this?
    (b) How is it recorded?
    (c) Name one situation in which a macro should not be used.
    Ans:
      (a) A macro, a recorded sequence of commands that can be played back to repeat the work.
      (b) Choose Tools → Macros → Record Macro, do the steps once, click Stop Recording, then give the macro a name and save it in the document or in My Macros.
      (c) When the task is done only once, or when the steps change every time, since recording would waste time.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Discuss the advantages and the risks of using macros in a spreadsheet.
    Ans:
      Advantage 1: a macro automates repeated work, so it saves time.
      Advantage 2: it repeats the steps exactly, so results are consistent and fewer errors occur.
      Risk 1: a macro in a file from an unknown source can contain harmful code and damage files or steal data.
      Risk 2: a wrongly recorded macro can change data in the wrong cells.
      Precautions: set the macro security level to Medium or High (Tools → Options → Security → Macro Security), run macros only from trusted people, and keep a backup before running one.

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] Write a formula that shows cell B4 of sheet Fees on sheet Summary.
    Ans: =Fees.B4 — or, if the sheet name contains spaces or punctuation, quote it: ='Fees'.B4. The sheet name comes first, then a full stop, then the cell address; Calc fills this in for you if you type = and then click the cell on the Fees sheet.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] What is a hyperlink in Calc?
    Ans: A clickable link to a web page, file, email or a location in the document, inserted via Insert → Hyperlink.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] What is meant by linking spreadsheet data?
    Ans: Linking makes one cell take its value from another cell, sheet or file, so that the value is not typed twice. When the source changes, every linked cell updates automatically, which keeps reports consistent and saves the work of retyping.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] What is the difference between a hyperlink and a sheet-link formula? Give one example of each.
    Ans:
      A hyperlink jumps the reader somewhere when clicked — for example, Insert → Hyperlink to https://cbseacademic.nic.in opens the CBSE site.
      A sheet-link formula pulls a live value into a cell — for example, =Term1.G20 shows Term1's total inside the Summary sheet and updates whenever Term1 changes.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] Summary sheet must show Total from three term sheets named Term1, Term2, Term3, each total in G20. Write the formula.
    Ans: =Term1.G20+Term2.G20+Term3.G20 — one reference per term sheet, added together. Linking this way means each term file can be updated on its own and the Summary total follows automatically, with no retyping and no copy-paste errors.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A student e-mails only summary.ods to her teacher. When the teacher opens it, the linked cells show errors or old values. Explain why this happens and how to avoid it.
    Ans:
      summary.ods does not contain the data itself: its cells link to values in another file, data.ods, stored on the lab computer.
      On the teacher’s computer the source file is missing, so the link cannot be updated.
      Send both files and keep them in one folder, or, before sending, convert the linked cells to plain values (or break the link from Edit → Links to External Files).

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] How do you link a cell to another sheet and to a file outside the workbook?
    Ans:
      Within the workbook: type = in the target cell, click the sheet tab, click the source cell and press Enter; the formula reads =Sheet2.B3.
      To an external file: open both files, copy the source cell, click the target cell and choose Edit → Paste Special → Paste Link.
      The formula then contains the full path, for example ='file:///home/user/accounts.ods'#$Sheet1.A1, and you can refresh the links when the source file changes.

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] Why should linked files be kept in one folder? Describe what breaks when a source file is moved or renamed.
    Ans:
      Calc stores external links as paths; keeping all files in one folder keeps those paths short and portable.
      If the source file moves or is renamed, the link cannot be resolved: the dependent cells show errors or stale values instead of live data.
      Fix — keep the set together from the start, and if a move is unavoidable, re-point the links through Edit → Links to External Files.

Q9. [Long · 4 marks] Why is linking sheets better than copy-pasting values for a yearly total?
    Ans: A link stays live: when a term sheet changes, the year sheet updates. Copy-paste values go stale, cause mismatch, and make auditing harder.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:

    (a) Which feature does she use, and how does she insert it?
    (b) Name two other things a hyperlink in Calc can point to.
    (c) How is a hyperlink different from a formula that links to another sheet?
    Ans:

      (b) An e-mail address and a target inside the same document, such as a named cell.
      (c) A hyperlink only opens something when clicked; a sheet-link formula brings a value from another sheet into the cell, and it updates when the source changes.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Explain the steps to bring a table from a web page into a Calc sheet so that it can be refreshed, and give two precautions.
    Ans:
      Step 1: place the cursor in the cell where the table should start.
      Step 2: choose Insert → Link to External Data (in newer versions it is under the Sheet menu).
      Step 3: type the web address in the URL box and press Enter; select the table or range that you want in the list.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Why add a comment to a cell instead of changing the value?

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] What does recording changes do when a spreadsheet is shared?
    Ans: Edit → Track Changes → Record logs every edit with the author's name, the time and the old value. Each change can then be reviewed, accepted or rejected from Edit → Track Changes → Manage, so a team can work on one file without losing track of who changed what.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] What is the difference between accepting and rejecting a recorded change? Where is each done?
    Ans: Accepting keeps the edited value as the final content; rejecting discards the edit and restores the original value. Both are done in the Manage Changes dialogue (Edit → Track Changes → Manage), which lists every change with its author and time so each can be judged separately.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] Three lab partners type in three copies of stock.ods and the totals disagree. What should they have used?

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A teacher types her corrections into a shared spreadsheet and only afterwards switches on Record Changes. Why do her corrections not appear in the review list, and what should she do?
    Ans:
      Recording tracks only edits made after it is switched on; the changes already typed are not marked.
      She should first turn on Edit → Track Changes → Record, and then make the corrections.
      Each change is then shown with the author and date, so it can later be accepted or rejected.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] How do you compare two versions of a document in LibreOffice?
    Ans:
      Open the newer copy and choose Edit → Track Changes → Compare Document.
      Select the older copy in the file dialog and click Insert; LibreOffice shows the differences as tracked changes.
      Review each one with Next and Previous, then accept or reject it from the Manage dialog.
      This works in both Writer and Calc and is the safest way to merge two people's work.

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] What is ‘Protect Sheet’, and why must input cells be unlocked before it is applied?
    Ans:
      Protect Sheet (Tools → Protect Sheet) locks the sheet so that only permitted actions — usually editing unlocked cells — are possible.
      By default every cell is locked, so protecting first would freeze the whole sheet including the inputs.
      Correct order — select the input cells, uncheck Protected in Format → Cells → Protection, then protect the sheet; formulas stay safe while inputs stay editable.

Q9. [Long · 4 marks] Explain recording, showing and accepting changes in Calc.
    Ans: Turn Record on before editing. Each changed cell is marked. Show lists author, date and old/new value. Accept keeps the new value; Reject restores the old. Compare Document does this against a saved copy.

CHAPTER 13 · Introduction to DBMS
Unit: Part B · Database Management System
========================================================================

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] Differentiate data and information with an example.
    Ans: Data are raw facts (42, “Neha”). Information is processed and meaningful (“Neha’s attendance is 42 days”).

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] What is a primary key? Give one example.
    Ans: A field (or set of fields) that uniquely identifies each record and cannot be empty. Example: AdmissionNo in a Student table.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] Distinguish between data and information with an example.
    Ans: Data are raw, unprocessed facts, such as the marks obtained by each student in each subject. Information is data that has been processed and given meaning, such as the class average, the highest mark or a list of students who passed. A database stores data; queries and reports turn it into information.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] What is a foreign key? Explain with a Student–Library example how it links two tables.
    Ans: A foreign key is a field in one table that refers to the primary key of another table. Example — the Issue table stores RollNo as a foreign key pointing to the Student table's RollNo primary key, so each issued book is tied to exactly one student without retyping the student's name and class.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A school stores students in one sheet with teacher phone numbers typed against every student. Two problems, and the DBMS idea that fixes them.
    Ans: Redundancy (phone typed 40 times) and inconsistency (one row has the old number). Split into Student and Teacher tables with a foreign key.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A student writes: “Calc is the DBMS we use in this course.” Correct this and give one reason why a DBMS suits large, related data better than a spreadsheet.
    Ans:
      Calc is a spreadsheet. The DBMS used in this course is LibreOffice Base.
      A spreadsheet keeps everything in one table, so data such as a teacher’s phone number is repeated against every student.
      A DBMS keeps data in related tables linked by keys, which reduces repetition and errors and keeps the data consistent.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] List any four advantages of a DBMS over a simple file system.
    Ans:
      It removes duplication, so the same fact is stored once and every user sees the same value.
      It keeps the data consistent and lets the designer set rules, so that wrong data cannot easily be entered.
      It allows many users to work at the same time without overwriting each other's work.
      It gives security through passwords and permissions, and fast searching, sorting and reporting through queries.

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] Define field, record and table with reference to a school database.
    Ans:
      Field — one attribute, such as Name or Class; a column of the table.
      Record — all the fields for one item, such as one student's complete row.
      Table — the full set of records of one kind, such as the Student table holding every student's row.

Q9. [Long · 4 marks] List four advantages of a DBMS over a paper register or a single Calc sheet.
    Ans: Less duplication; one correction updates all uses; password/security; backup and recovery; many users; validation (integrity). A Calc sheet is easy to duplicate and hard to relate as tables grow.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A clinic keeps patient names in a diary. Two staff write different spellings; a page is lost.
    
    (a) Two problems of this system.
    (b) Two DBMS advantages that help.
    (c) Suggest a primary key.
    Ans:
      (a) Inconsistency, no backup, slow search, redundancy.
      (b) Central consistent data, backup/recovery, validation, sharing.
      (c) PatientID or a unique registration number — not the name.

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A library keeps three tables: Book (BookID, Title), Member (MemberID, Name) and Issue (IssueID, BookID, MemberID, IssueDate).
    
    (a) Which field is the primary key of each table?
    (b) Which fields in Issue are foreign keys?
    (c) Why is a separate Issue table used?
    Ans:
      (a) BookID in Book, MemberID in Member and IssueID in Issue.
      (b) BookID and MemberID, because they refer to the primary keys of Book and Member.
      (c) Many members issue many books, so each issue is recorded as a row in Issue instead of repeating book or member details.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Compare a flat-file database and a relational database on five points.
    Ans:
      Structure: a flat file is a single table, as in a Calc sheet; a relational database has several related tables, as in Base.
      Repetition: a flat file repeats data (for example teacher details against each student); a relational database stores each fact once.
      Updating: a change in a flat file must be made in many rows; in a relational database it is made in one place.
      Links: a flat file has no relationships; a relational database joins tables through primary and foreign keys.
      Use: a flat file suits small, simple lists; a relational database suits large data with many connected items.

CHAPTER 15 · Relationships and Multiple Tables
Unit: Part B · Database Management System

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] What is a one-to-many relationship? Give an example.
    Ans: One record in table A matches many in table B. One student, many library issues. The Issue table stores AdmNo as FK.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] What is referential integrity?
    Ans: A rule that every foreign-key value must match an existing primary key, so you cannot create orphan records.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] What is referential integrity and why is it useful?
    Ans: Referential integrity is the rule that every value of a foreign key must match a value of the primary key in the related table, or be empty. It prevents ‘orphan' records — for example, a fee record belonging to a student who is not in the student table — and keeps the database reliable.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] What is a many-to-many relationship? Explain why it needs a junction table, with an example.
    Ans: Many-to-many means rows on both sides link to many rows on the other — for example, each student borrows many books and each book is borrowed by many students. Neither table can hold the other's key directly, so a junction table (Issue: IssueID, RollNo, BookID, Date) stores each pairing as its own record.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A shop has Customer and Order tables. Where does CustomerID go, and what relationship is this?
    Ans: CustomerID is PK in Customer and FK in Order. One-to-many (one customer, many orders).

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A student relates the tables Customer and Order by making OrderID in Order the primary key and also making CustomerID in Customer the foreign key. What is wrong?
    Ans:
      The foreign key goes on the many side. One customer can place many orders, so Order must contain the CustomerID.
      Correct design: CustomerID is the primary key of Customer, and CustomerID in Order is the foreign key that refers to it.
      The primary key and the foreign key must also have the same data type, or Base will refuse the link.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Explain the three types of relationship with an example of each.
    Ans:
      One-to-one: one record in the first table matches one in the second, for example one student and one library card.
      One-to-many: one record in the first table matches many in the second, for example one class has many students.
      Many-to-many: records on both sides match many on the other, for example students and subjects; this is usually split into two one-to-many relationships through a third table.

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] What is ‘cascade delete’? Describe one situation where it is useful and one where it is dangerous.
    Ans:
      Cascade delete automatically deletes child records when their parent is deleted.
      Useful — removing a passed-out batch's students together with their old fee rows in one step.
      Dangerous — deleting one supplier and silently wiping years of purchase history that accounts still needs. Prefer blocking the delete (referential integrity) unless you are certain the children are disposable.

Q9. [Long · 4 marks] Explain how to relate Student and Issue in Base.
    Ans: AdmNo PK in Student; AdmNo FK in Issue (same type). Tools → Relationships, drag PK to FK, enable referential integrity. Then an issue cannot use a missing admission number.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Deleting student 12 failed because “existing related records”.
    
    (a) What rule is firing?
    (b) Two safe ways to proceed.
    (c) When would cascade delete be wrong?
    Ans:
      (a) Referential integrity — Issue rows still point to 12.
      (b) Delete or reassign the issue rows first; or (if asked) enable cascade delete.
      (c) When those issues are history you must keep — never cascade-delete library history by accident.

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    In a school, each student has exactly one library card and each card belongs to exactly one student.
    
    (a) What type of relationship is this?
    (b) How can it be set up in Base?
    (c) Why are the data often kept in one table instead?
    Ans:
      (a) A one-to-one relationship.
      (b) The primary key of one table is also used (as a unique key) in the other, and the two are linked in Tools → Relationships.
      (c) Because each row matches exactly one row of the other table, the fields can be placed in a single table; separate tables are used only when some data is rarely needed or must be kept apart.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] A shop has customers, products and orders. Design the tables with their primary and foreign keys, and name the relationships.
    Ans:
      Customer (CustomerID — primary key, Name, Phone).
      Product (ProductID — primary key, ProductName, Price).
      Order (OrderID — primary key, CustomerID — foreign key, ProductID — foreign key, Quantity, OrderDate).
      Customer to Order is one-to-many, and Product to Order is also one-to-many.
      Between Customer and Product the relationship is many-to-many, which is why the Order table acts as the linking (junction) table.

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] What is a query in Base?
    Ans: A saved question that selects fields from one or more tables according to criteria and sort order, without storing a second copy of the data.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Write criteria to list students whose names start with A and marks above 70.
    Ans: Put both criteria in the same criteria row: Name with Like 'A*' and Marks with >70. Criteria on the same row are joined by AND, so a student must satisfy both to appear; putting them on different rows would join them by OR and list every student who passes either test.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] What is a query and why is it used?
    Ans: A query is a question put to the database. It selects the fields and records you need, filters them by conditions, sorts them and can calculate totals, so you can answer questions such as ‘which students scored above 90 in Science' without reading the whole table.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] What is the difference between AND and OR in a query grid? Illustrate with one row each.
    Ans:
      AND — two criteria on the same grid row must both be true; for example, Class = 10 AND Marks > 75 lists only high-scoring Class 10 students.
      OR — criteria on separate rows need only one to be true; for example, Class = 10 on one row and Class = 9 on the next lists both classes.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] The librarian wants unpaid fees greater than 500, sorted by fees high to low. Which grid settings?
    Ans: Field Fees, criterion >500, Sort Descending; include Name/AdmNo so the rows are identifiable.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A student opens “Create Query in Design View” in Base and finds that there are no fields to choose from. What did she forget, and what should she do?
    Ans:

      In the Add Table or Query box, select the table (or tables) and click Add, then Close.
      The table boxes with their fields now appear, and fields can be dragged down to the design grid.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Describe how to create a query with a condition in LibreOffice Base.
    Ans:
      Choose Queries, then Create Query in Design View, and add the table or tables you need.
      Double-click each field you want to see, so that it appears in the grid below.
      Type the condition in the Criterion row of the field you want to filter, for example >90, and set Ascending or Descending in the Sort row.
      Run the query to check the result, then save it with a clear name; it can be re-run any time the data changes.

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] What is an alias in a query? Give one example where renaming a field improves a report.
    Ans:
      An alias is a display name given to a field in a query (Field AS Alias) without changing the stored data.
      Example — a report column headed ‘M’ confuses readers; aliasing it as ‘Marks’ makes the printed report self-explanatory while the table field stays M.

Q9. [Long · 4 marks] How do you create a query that shows student name and book title for issues after 1 Apr 2026?
    Ans: Design View → add Student, Issue, Book (related) → fields Name, Title, IssueDate → criterion on IssueDate > {d '2026-04-01'} or the date the lab uses → Run → Save.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A Marks table has the fields Name, Maths and Science. The teacher wants a query that shows each Name and the total of the two subjects under the heading Total, with the highest total first.
    
    (a) What is typed in the Field row for the total?
    (b) How is the heading “Total” set?
    (c) How is the order set?
    Ans:
      (a) The expression "Maths"+"Science" (field names go in double quotes).
      (b) Type Total in the Alias row of that column.
      (c) Set the Sort row of that column to Descending.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Name the rows of the query design grid in Base and state the job of each.
    Ans:
      Field: the field or expression that the column uses; Alias: the heading shown in the result; Table: the table the field comes from.
      Sort: arranges the result ascending or descending on that field.
      Visible: a tick shows the column in the result; unticking hides it but still lets it be used for a condition.

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] Differentiate a hazard and an accident with an IT-lab example.
    Ans: A hazard is a potential source of harm (a loose cable across the aisle). An accident is the event (a student trips and the monitor falls).

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] List four electrical safety rules for a computer lab.
    Ans: Dry hands; earthed plugs; no overloaded boards; don’t open the power supply; report frayed wires; use a surge protector.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] What is a hazard? Give two examples from a computer laboratory.
    Ans: A hazard is anything in the workplace that can cause injury or ill health. In a computer laboratory the hazards include a trailing cable that can trip someone, a socket or adapter that is overloaded and can start a fire, a wet floor, and a chair with a broken back that can cause a fall.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] What is the difference between a hazard and a risk? Give one IT-lab example of each.
    Ans:
      A hazard is anything that can cause harm — for example, a trailing cable across the lab floor.
      Risk is the chance that the harm actually happens — for example, the high risk that someone hurrying between PCs trips over that cable.
      Safety work removes hazards first, then reduces the remaining risk with guards, rules and training.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] A student pours water on a sparking socket. Two mistakes and the correct actions.
    Ans: Water on electrical fire; staying next to a live spark. Instead: don’t touch, switch off mains if safe, alert staff, use CO2/powder extinguisher, evacuate.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A student answers “Be careful with electricity” to the question “State three electrical safety precautions in a computer lab”. Why will this score no marks? Give three concrete precautions.
    Ans:
      The answer is vague. A precaution must name a specific action that prevents harm.
      Precaution 1: never touch plugs or switches with wet hands, and keep liquids away from the computers.
      Precaution 2: report damaged cables and loose sockets at once, and do not overload a socket with many plugs.
      (Another: switch off and unplug the equipment before cleaning or repairing it.)

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] List four fire safety rules for a workplace.
    Ans:
      Keep every fire exit and aisle clear of boxes, cables and furniture, and make sure the exit signs are lit.
      Check that fire extinguishers are in place, visible and within their expiry date, and know which type to use for an electrical fire.
      Switch off and unplug equipment at the end of the day, and never overload a socket with adapters.
      If a fire starts, raise the alarm, cut off the power if it is safe to do so, leave by the marked route without using the lift, and go to the assembly point.

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] List four data-security measures for a school computer lab and explain the purpose of each.
    Ans:
      Unique passwords per user — so every action can be traced to its author.
      Authorised access only — outsiders cannot reach student records or exam files.
      Regular backups — a failed disk or ransomware cannot destroy the only copy of practical files.
      Locked server and storeroom — physical theft of drives is blocked at the door.

Q9. [Long · 4 marks] Explain four types of workplace hazard with one IT-lab example each.
    Ans: Physical — trailing cables; electrical — open socket; ergonomic — low screen causing neck pain; psychosocial — long hours without a break. Control each: cable covers, close sockets, raise the monitor, timetable breaks.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    Lab PCs face a window, chairs don’t adjust, passwords are on sticky notes.
    
    (a) Name three hazard/security issues.
    (b) One control for each.
    (c) Who should you report this to?
    Ans:
      (a) Glare (ergonomic), poor posture, password leak (security).
      (b) Blinds/position screens, adjustable chairs, unique passwords stored in a password manager / teacher vault — never sticky notes.
      (c) Lab teacher / system admin / safety in-charge.

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    In a computer lab, two exits are blocked by boxes, the fire extinguisher is past its expiry date and a printer cable lies across the walkway.
    
    (a) Identify the three hazards.
    (b) Name the type of risk each creates.
    (c) State one corrective action for each.
    Ans:
      (a) The blocked exits, the expired extinguisher and the loose cable.
      (b) Blocked exits risk people being trapped in an emergency; the expired extinguisher risks a small fire spreading; the cable risks a fall (a tripping hazard).
      (c) Clear the exits and keep them free; replace or recharge the extinguisher; tape the cable down or reroute it along the wall.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] Name four types of fire extinguisher and say which one should be used on an electrical fire in a computer lab. State the PASS steps for using an extinguisher.
    Ans:
      Types: water, foam, dry powder and carbon dioxide (CO₂).
      Water and foam must not be used on an electrical fire, because they conduct electricity and can cause a shock or damage the equipment.
      For computers, a CO₂ extinguisher is best because it leaves no residue; dry powder also works but can harm the electronics.
      PASS: Pull the pin; Aim the nozzle at the base of the fire; Squeeze the handle; Sweep from side to side.
      Also switch off the mains supply if it is safe, and raise the alarm.

CHAPTER 19 · Workplace Quality and Ergonomics
Unit: Part B · Healthy, Safe and Secure Working Environment
========================================================================

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] What is ergonomics? Give two workstation rules.
    Ans: Designing work to fit the person. Rules: screen at eye level, feet flat, back supported, wrists straight, mouse beside the keyboard.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Expand 5S.
    Ans: 5S = Sort, Set in order, Shine, Standardise, Sustain. Sort removes what is not needed; Set in order gives everything a fixed place; Shine means cleaning as you go; Standardise writes the method down so anyone can follow it; Sustain makes it a daily habit. The first three clean the workplace, the last two keep it clean.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] What is ergonomics and why does it matter when working at a computer?
    Ans: Ergonomics is the science of designing the workplace to fit the person. At a computer it keeps the body comfortable and prevents backache, neck and shoulder pain, eye strain and repetitive strain injury caused by hours of sitting in a wrong posture with badly placed equipment.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] What are the four features of quality work in IT? Illustrate with a data-entry task.
    Ans:
      Accurate — every figure matches the source; for example, marks typed without a single digit error.
      Complete — nothing is missing; every student's row is filled.
      On time — the file is ready before the deadline, not after.
      Checked — a second pass (spell-check, totals re-added) proves the first three.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] Amit’s laptop is on a high table, he looks up, and his wrists bend on the edge. Two injuries this invites and two fixes.
    Ans: Neck strain and wrist RSI. Fixes: raise the chair or lower the desk, laptop stand + external keyboard, wrist straight, breaks.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A student draws a person hunched over a laptop on a low sofa and labels it “correct posture”. Correct this, and give four features of a correct sitting posture at a computer.
    Ans:
      It is incorrect: a hunched back and a low screen strain the neck, back and wrists.
      Feet flat on the floor and the back straight and supported by the chair.
      Elbows bent at about 90° with the wrists straight, and the top of the screen at or just below eye level.
      The screen about an arm’s length (50–70 cm) away, with short breaks every 20–30 minutes.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Describe a correct workstation arrangement and the working habit that prevents strain.
    Ans:
      Sit back in a chair that supports the lower back, with both feet flat on the floor and knees at about a right angle.
      Keep the top of the screen at or just below eye level, about an arm's length away, with the keyboard and mouse close enough to keep the forearms horizontal and the wrists straight.
      Place the document you are copying beside the screen rather than flat on the desk, so the neck does not keep turning.
      Follow the 20-20-20 habit: every 20 minutes look 20 feet away for 20 seconds, and take a short break and stretch every hour.

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] Explain the 5S method with one computer-lab example for each S.
    Ans:
      Sort — remove what is not needed: discard dried pens and broken CDs from the lab drawers.
      Set in order — give everything a place: label each shelf, cable and software CD.
      Shine — clean regularly: wipe keyboards and screens on a fixed weekly roster.
      Standardise — make it a rule: the same layout and cleaning checklist in every lab.
      Sustain — keep it going: a monthly audit so the lab never slides back.

Q9. [Long · 4 marks] Describe a correct computer workstation and two quality checks before submitting a practical file.
    Ans: Chair and desk so elbows and knees are about 90°, screen arm’s length, top at eye level, no glare. Quality: proof-read, spell-check, file named as asked, print preview, backup copy.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    A lab has tangled wires, unmarked CDs, glare, and students skip spell-check.
    
    (a) Map each problem to 5S or ergonomics or quality.
    (b) One action each.
    (c) Why quality matters in a data-entry job role.
    Ans:
      (a) Wires/CDs → 5S Set in order; glare → ergonomics; skip spell-check → quality.
      (b) Cable ties + labels; blinds or move screens; compulsory preview + peer check.
      (c) Wrong data costs money and trust — the job role is Domestic Data Entry Operator.

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    In a computer lab, cables lie tangled on the floor, unused CDs are piled on the desks and folders have no labels.
    
    (a) Which 5S step removes the unused CDs?
    (b) Which step covers labelling the folders and arranging the cables?
    (c) Name the remaining steps and say what each means.
    Ans:
      (a) Sort: remove what is not needed.
      (b) Set in order: a place for everything, with labels, so that things can be found quickly.
      (c) Shine (clean the area and equipment regularly), Standardise (make rules so that the same order is kept) and Sustain (make the habit continue).

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] What is housekeeping? Describe five housekeeping rules for a computer lab and explain how they improve safety and quality of work.
    Ans:
      Housekeeping means keeping the workplace clean, tidy and organised.
      Keep floors and walkways clear and store cables neatly, which prevents trips and falls.
      Do not eat or drink near the computers, and dust the equipment regularly, which prevents damage and faults.
      Label folders, media and equipment and put things back in their place, so that work is found easily and not lost.
      Dispose of waste correctly and report faults at once; a clean, orderly lab lets people work accurately and safely.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

M1. The first S in 5S is: (a) Shine (b) Sustain (c) Speed (d) Sort
    Ans: (d) Sort.

M2. Screen top should be: (a) at eye level (b) at knee level (c) behind you (d) on the floor
    Ans: (a) at eye level.

M3. 20-20-20 is for: (a) macros (b) primary keys (c) mail merge (d) eye strain
    Ans: (d) eye strain.

M4. Quality in data entry mainly means: (a) speed only (b) accuracy and completeness as well as speed (c) colourful fonts (d) loud music
    Ans: (b).

M5. Ergonomics is the study of: (a) designing workplaces and equipment to suit the user, for comfort, safety and efficiency (b) writing computer programs (c) repairing electronic circuits (d) storing data in a database
    Ans: (a) designing workplaces and equipment to suit the user, for comfort, safety and efficiency. Good ergonomics reduces strain and injury and helps people work better.

M6. For correct posture, the top of the computer screen should be: (a) well above the head (b) at or slightly below eye level (c) on the floor (d) at the level of the keyboard
    Ans: (b) at or slightly below eye level. This lets the user look slightly down without bending the neck.

M7. The 5S workplace method consists of: (a) Sit, See, Save, Stop, Start  (b) Safety, Speed, Size, Skill, System  (c) Sort, Set in order, Shine, Standardise, Sustain  (d) Scan, Send, Store, Sell, Sort
    Ans: (c) Sort, Set in order, Shine, Standardise, Sustain. It comes from Japanese workplace practice.

M8. Repetitive Strain Injury is caused mainly by: (a) a single heavy fall  (b) loud noise  (c) repeating the same small movement for long periods  (d) cold weather

CHAPTER 20 · Prevent Accidents and Emergencies
Unit: Part B · Healthy, Safe and Secure Working Environment
========================================================================

A. WRITTEN QUESTIONS (12)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. [Short · 2 marks] List four steps of evacuation in the correct order.
    Ans: Hear the alarm → leave via stairs (not lift) → go to the assembly point → answer the headcount and wait for the all-clear.

Q2. [Short · 2 marks] Why report a near-miss?
    Ans: A near-miss shows a hazard that almost caused harm. Reporting it lets the school fix the condition before someone is actually injured.

Q3. [Short · 2 marks] What is an emergency, and what should a workplace emergency plan contain?
    Ans: An emergency is an unexpected event that threatens life, health or property, such as a fire, an earthquake, a chemical spill or a sudden illness. A plan should name the escape routes and the assembly point, the person responsible for each task, the emergency telephone numbers, the location of first-aid kits and extinguishers, and how often evacuation drills will be held.

Q4. [Short · 2 marks] What is the difference between an accident and a near-miss? Why must both be reported?
    Ans: An accident is an event that causes harm — for example, a shock from a damaged cable. A near-miss is an event that could have caused harm but did not — for example, spotting the same damaged cable before anyone touched it. Both must be reported because the near-miss reveals the hazard while there is still time to fix it.

Q5. [Application · 3 marks] The fire alarm rings. A student runs back for a USB and takes the lift. Two wrong actions and the correct ones.
    Ans: Going back for belongings and using the lift. Correct: leave immediately by stairs, USB can be replaced, assemble for headcount.

Q6. [Application · 3 marks] A student says that toothpaste or ice should be put on a minor burn. Correct this and give the right first aid.
    Ans:
      Toothpaste, butter and ice must not be put on a burn; they can damage the skin or cause infection.
      Hold the burn under cool running water for about 10–20 minutes.
      Then cover it loosely with a clean, non-sticky dressing, do not burst any blisters, and get medical help if the burn is large or deep.

Q7. [Short · 3 marks] Describe the first aid for a minor burn and for a person who has fainted.
    Ans:
      For a minor burn: hold the area under cool running water for about ten minutes, remove rings or tight clothing near it, cover it with a clean, non-sticky cloth, and do not apply ice, oil or ointment and do not break blisters.
      For fainting: lay the person flat on their back, raise the legs above the level of the heart, loosen tight clothing and keep the airway clear.
      Do not crowd around, do not throw water on the face and do not give anything to drink until the person is fully conscious.
      Call for medical help if the person does not recover within a minute or if breathing is not normal.

Q8. [Short · 3 marks] What should a workplace emergency plan contain? List its essential parts with one line on each.
    Ans:
      Alarm and exits — how the alarm sounds and which stairs and exits to use (never the lift).
      Assembly and headcount — where to gather outside and who checks that everyone is out.
      First aid and numbers — where the kit is and the emergency number 112 for fire, police and ambulance.
      Roles and reporting — who leads the evacuation and how every incident and near-miss is written up afterwards.

Q9. [Long · 4 marks] Explain first aid for (i) a small cut (ii) a minor burn (iii) suspected electric shock.
    Ans: Cut: wash, pressure, clean dressing. Burn: cool with running water 10 minutes, loose sterile cover, no toothpaste/ice. Shock: do not touch until power is off; then separate with a dry non-conductor, call help, monitor breathing.

Q10. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    During a practical a student gets a shock from a damaged mouse cable, laughs it off, and no one writes it down.
    
    (a) Immediate first actions.
    (b) Why a report still matters.
    (c) Two prevention steps so it does not recur.
    Ans:
      (a) Switch off power, check breathing, first aid, inform the teacher.
      (b) Near-miss/incident record → cable replaced, others warned, pattern spotted.
      (c) Inspect peripherals, remove damaged devices, remind: report all shocks.

Q11. [Competency · 4 marks] Read and answer:
    
    During a practical, smoke rises from a UPS in the lab.
    
    (a) What should the teacher and students do first?
    (b) Which emergency numbers can be called?
    (c) How should the room be left?
    Ans:
      (a) Raise the alarm and, if it is safe, switch off the main power supply; do not use water on electrical equipment.
      (b) 101 for the fire service, or 112, the national emergency number (100 for police and 102 for an ambulance).
      (c) Everyone leaves calmly by the stairs, not the lift, and gathers at the assembly point, where the teacher takes a head count.

Q12. [Long · 5 marks] List five items of a first-aid box and five rules that a first-aider should follow.
    Ans:
      Items: sterile gauze and bandages, adhesive plasters, antiseptic solution, scissors, and disposable gloves (also cotton wool and a burn dressing).
      Rule 1: first make sure the area is safe, for example switch off the power in case of electric shock.
      Rule 2: call for help and the emergency number quickly.
      Rule 3: do not move a seriously injured person unless there is danger, and reassure them.
      Rule 4: wear gloves and wash hands; do not give food or drink to an unconscious person.
