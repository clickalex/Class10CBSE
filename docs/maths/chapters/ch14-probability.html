🎯 Marks lens

The remaining 3–4 marks of the 11-mark unit.
Almost always one 2–3 mark question on a die, a pair of dice, a deck of cards or coloured balls.
The CBSE syllabus restricts this to the classical definition and simple single-event problems. No two-event multiplication rules.

📖 Deep concepts

1. Classical definition. P(E) = (number of outcomes favourable to E) / (total number of possible outcomes), assuming all outcomes are equally likely.
2. Range. 0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1. An impossible event has probability 0; a certain event has probability 1.
3. Complementary events. P(E) + P(not E) = 1. When “not E” is easier to count, compute that instead.
4. Standard totals to memorise. One die: 6 outcomes. Two dice: 36 outcomes. A well-shuffled deck: 52 cards, 26 red and 26 black, 4 of each rank, 12 face cards (J, Q, K of each suit) and 4 aces. A coin: 2 outcomes; two coins: 4.
5. “At least one” questions are nearly always fastest as 1 − P(none).

🧮 Formulas to memorise

  • P(E) = favourable outcomes / total outcomes
  • P(not E) = 1 − P(E)
  • 0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1
  • Two dice: 36 outcomes
  • Deck: 52 cards, 26 red, 12 face cards, 4 aces

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Always write the total number of outcomes first. Half of all probability errors are wrong totals, not wrong favourable counts.
  • For two dice, list the pairs systematically (1,1) to (6,6) when the condition is unusual, such as “sum is a prime number”.
  • “Face card” means Jack, Queen or King — not the ace. Twelve face cards in a deck.
  • A red king is 2 in a deck; a black face card is 6.
  • “At least one” → use 1 − P(none). “At most” → count directly, it is usually short.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Forgetting that a die has 6 outcomes, not 6 faces times something — and that two dice have 36, not 12.
  • Counting an ace as a face card.
  • Simplifying the fraction incorrectly, or leaving it unsimplified when the answer is expected as a fraction.
  • Writing a probability greater than 1. If that happens, your total is wrong.

SHORTS11 mark

1. A card is drawn from a well-shuffled deck. What is the probability that it is a red king?

Show Answer

Ans:

There are 2 red kings, so P = 2/52 = 1/26.

SHORTS22 marks

2. A die is thrown once. Find the probability of getting (i) a prime number, (ii) a number greater than 4.

Show Answer

Ans:

Primes on a die are 2, 3, 5, so P = 3/6 = 1/2.
Numbers greater than 4 are 5 and 6, so P = 2/6 = 1/3.

SHORTS33 marks

3. Two dice are thrown together. Find the probability that the sum is 9.

Show Answer

Ans:

Total outcomes = 36.
Favourable pairs: (3,6), (4,5), (5,4), (6,3) — 4 outcomes.
P = 4/36 = 1/9.

SHORTS42 marks

4. A bag contains 5 red and 3 green balls. One ball is drawn at random. Find the probability that it is not red.

Show Answer

Ans:

Total = 8. P(not red) = P(green) = 3/8.
Equivalently, 1 − 5/8 = 3/8.

SHORTS53 marks

5. A card is drawn at random from a well-shuffled deck. Find the probability that it is (i) a face card, (ii) neither a king nor a red card.

Show Answer

Ans:

(i) There are 12 face cards, so P = 12/52 = 3/13.
(ii) Red cards are 26, of which 2 are kings. Neither a king nor red means a black card that is not a king: 26 − 2 = 24.
P = 24/52 = 6/13.
