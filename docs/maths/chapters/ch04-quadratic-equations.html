Chapter 4 · Quadratic Equations

Chapter 4 of 14. The discriminant decides the nature of the roots, and that single idea answers most of this chapter’s questions.

🎯 Marks lens

5–7 marks. A nature-of-roots question (2–3 marks) and a word problem (4–5 marks).
Word problems are usually on consecutive integers, speed–time–distance, or the perimeter and area of a rectangle.

📖 Deep concepts

1. Standard form. ax² + bx + c = 0, with a ≠ 0.
2. Factorisation. Split the middle term: find two numbers whose product is a × c and whose sum is b.
3. Completing the square. Divide by a, move c/a across, add (b/2a)² to both sides, then take the square root.
4. Quadratic formula. x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a.

Discriminant D = b² − 4acNature of roots
D > 0Two distinct real roots
D = 0Two equal real roots
D < 0No real roots
D > 0 and a perfect squareTwo distinct rational roots

5. Finding k for equal roots. Set D = 0 and solve for k. This is the single most repeated question type here.

🧮 Formulas to memorise

  • x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a
  • D = b² − 4ac
  • Equal roots ⇔ D = 0
  • Sum of roots = −b/a, Product of roots = c/a

🛠️ Method, step by step

  1. Bring everything to one side so the equation equals 0, and write it in standard form.
  2. Identify a, b, c — with their signs.
  3. Compute D. If D is negative, stop: there are no real roots.
  4. Factorise if D is a perfect square; otherwise use the formula.
  5. For word problems, reject any root that is physically impossible (negative age, negative length).

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Splitting the middle term: you need two numbers with product a × c (not just c) and sum b. Forgetting the a is the most common error when a ≠ 1.
  • Nature questions are discriminant questions. Never solve the equation to test the nature — just compute D.
  • Consecutive integers: x, x+1, x+2. Consecutive even integers: x, x+2, x+4.
  • “Roots are reciprocal” ⇒ c = a. “One root is double the other” ⇒ 2b² = 9ac.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Dropping the sign of b when writing −b in the formula.
  • Taking the square root of D and then forgetting the ±.
  • Accepting a negative or fractional answer in a word problem about people, lengths or counts.
  • For “equal roots” questions, setting D > 0 instead of D = 0.

❓ Exam Q&A

2 marks Find the nature of the roots of 2x² − 6x + 3 = 0.

D = (−6)² − 4(2)(3) = 36 − 24 = 12.
D > 0 but 12 is not a perfect square, so the roots are real, distinct and irrational.

3 marks Find the value of k for which 2x² + kx + 3 = 0 has two equal roots.

For equal roots, D = 0, so k² − 4(2)(3) = 0.
k² = 24, so k = ±2√6.

3 marks Solve 6x² − x − 2 = 0 by factorisation.

Split −x using numbers with product 6 × (−2) = −12 and sum −1: these are −4 and 3.
6x² − 4x + 3x − 2 = 2x(3x − 2) + 1(3x − 2) = (3x − 2)(2x + 1).
So x = 2/3 or x = −1/2.

5 marks The sum of the areas of two squares is 468 m². If the difference of their perimeters is 24 m, find the sides of the two squares.

Let the sides be x and y with x > y. Then x² + y² = 468 and 4x − 4y = 24, so x − y = 6.
Substituting y = x − 6: x² + (x − 6)² = 468 ⇒ 2x² − 12x + 36 = 468.
2x² − 12x − 432 = 0, so x² − 6x − 216 = 0.
(x − 18)(x + 12) = 0, so x = 18 (rejecting −12) and y = 12.
The sides are 18 m and 12 m.

✍️ Hands-on task

Take three quadratics with a ≠ 1 and solve each by both factorisation and the formula. Where the answers disagree, the error is nearly always in the sign of b.
