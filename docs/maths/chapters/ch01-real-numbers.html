🎯 Marks lens

6 marks, always procedural. Expect one HCF/LCM word problem (2–3 marks), one irrationality proof (3 marks), and one terminating-decimal question (1 mark).
Euclid’s division lemma itself is asked as a 1-mark statement question.

📖 Deep concepts

1. Euclid’s Division Lemma. Given positive integers a and b, there exist unique integers q and r satisfying a = bq + r, where 0 ≤ r < b. It is the basis of the HCF algorithm: divide, then repeat with (b, r) until the remainder is 0. The last non-zero divisor is the HCF.
2. HCF and LCM by prime factorisation. Write each number as a product of primes. HCF = product of the lowest powers of the common primes. LCM = product of the highest powers of all primes present.

RelationUse
HCF × LCM = product of the two numbersValid for two numbers only — never for three
HCF always divides both numbersQuick check on your answer
LCM is always a multiple of bothQuick check on your answer

3. Irrationality proofs. The standard method is proof by contradiction: assume the number is rational p/q in lowest terms (q ≠ 0, p and q coprime), square both sides, then show a prime divides both p and q — contradicting coprimality.
4. Terminating decimal expansion. A rational number p/q in lowest terms has a terminating decimal if and only if q is of the form 2^m × 5^n, where m and n are non-negative integers. Otherwise it is non-terminating repeating.

🧮 Formulas to memorise

  • a = bq + r, 0 ≤ r < b
  • HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b
  • p/q terminates ⇔ q = 2ᵐ × 5ⁿ

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Terminating test: look only at the denominator. If, after reducing, the denominator has any prime other than 2 or 5, it repeats. Ignore the numerator entirely.
  • Irrationality answer shape: assumption → square → prime divides p → prime divides q → contradiction. Five lines, every time.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Using HCF × LCM = product for three numbers. It fails. Factorise instead.
  • In the irrationality proof, writing “p/q” without stating coprime and q ≠ 0. That is a marked step.
  • Forgetting to reduce the fraction before applying the terminating-decimal test. 12/60 looks non-terminating; it is 1/5.
  • Stopping the Euclid algorithm one step early. Stop when the remainder is 0, then take the previous divisor.

❓ Exam Q&A

1 marks State Euclid’s division lemma.

Given positive integers a and b, there exist unique integers q and r such that a = bq + r, where 0 ≤ r < b.

2 marks Without performing long division, state whether 13/3125 will have a terminating decimal expansion.

3125 = 5⁵, which is of the form 2ᵐ × 5ⁿ with m = 0, n = 5. The fraction is already in lowest terms, so yes — it terminates.

3 marks Prove that √3 is irrational.

Assume √3 is rational. Then √3 = p/q where p, q are coprime integers and q ≠ 0.
Squaring: 3 = p²/q², so p² = 3q². Hence 3 divides p², and since 3 is prime, 3 divides p.
Write p = 3k. Then 9k² = 3q², so q² = 3k². Hence 3 divides q², so 3 divides q.
So 3 divides both p and q, contradicting that they are coprime.
Therefore √3 is irrational.

3 marks The HCF of 96 and 404 is 4. Find their LCM.

HCF × LCM = product, so LCM = (96 × 404)/4 = 38784/4 = 9696.

4 marks An army contingent of 616 members marches behind an army band of 32 members. Both groups must march in the same number of columns. What is the maximum number of columns?

Maximum columns = HCF(616, 32).
616 = 2³ × 7 × 11 and 32 = 2⁵.
HCF = 2³ = 8, so the maximum is 8 columns.

✍️ Hands-on task

Take any two numbers above 200. Find their HCF by both Euclid’s algorithm and prime factorisation, then verify HCF × LCM = product. If the two HCFs disagree, your algorithm stopped a step early.
