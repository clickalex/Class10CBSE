🎯 Marks lens

3–4 marks. The reactivity series and displacement reactions are asked every year; ionic bond formation and the extraction of a metal (usually copper or zinc) fill the 3-mark slot.
Reasoning questions dominate: 'why is sodium kept in kerosene', 'why are food cans coated with tin and not zinc', 'why does aluminium not corrode'.

📖 Deep concepts

1. Physical properties. Metals are lustrous, malleable, ductile, sonorous and good conductors of heat and electricity; sodium and potassium are soft enough to cut with a knife, mercury is the only liquid metal, gallium and caesium melt in your palm. Non-metals are the opposite: dull, brittle, poor conductors — except graphite, which conducts, and iodine, a lustrous non-metal.
2. Chemical properties of metals. With oxygen they form basic (sometimes amphoteric) oxides — Al2O3 and ZnO are amphoteric. With water they form hydroxide + H2 (K and Na react with cold water; Mg with hot water; Al, Zn, Fe with steam). With dilute acids they give salt + H2 — but copper, silver and gold do not. Aqua regia (3 parts conc. HCl : 1 part conc. HNO3) dissolves gold.
3. The reactivity series (K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > (H) > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au). A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one from its salt solution: Fe + CuSO4 → FeSO4 + Cu, the blue solution turning green.
4. Ionic (electrovalent) bond formation. Sodium loses one electron to become Na+, chlorine gains it to become Cl−, and the oppositely charged ions attract. Ionic compounds are hard solids with high melting and boiling points, are soluble in water, and conduct electricity in the molten or aqueous state — but not as solids.
5. Occurrence and extraction. Mineral → ore (the mineral from which metal can be extracted profitably) → gangue (impurities). Steps: crushing and grinding → concentration (froth flotation, leaching) → conversion to oxide (calcination in the absence of air, roasting in the presence of air) → reduction (carbon for Zn/Fe/Pb; electrolytic reduction for Na, Mg, Al) → refining (electrolytic refining with impure anode, pure cathode, acidified CuSO4 electrolyte). Thermite: Fe2O3 + 2Al → 2Fe + Al2O3 + heat, used to weld broken railway tracks.
6. Corrosion prevention — painting, oiling, greasing, galvanising (zinc coating), chrome plating, anodising, and making alloys. Alloy = homogeneous mixture of a metal with another metal or non-metal: steel (Fe + C), stainless steel (Fe + Cr + Ni + C), brass (Cu + Zn), bronze (Cu + Sn), solder (Pb + Sn, low melting point), amalgam (metal + mercury).

🧮 Formulas to memorise

  • 2Cu + O2 → 2CuO (black); 4Al + 3O2 → 2Al2O3
  • 2Na + 2H2O → 2NaOH + H2↑
  • Fe2O3 + 2Al → 2Fe + Al2O3 + heat (thermite)
  • ZnCO3 →(calcination) ZnO + CO2
  • 2ZnS + 3O2 →(roasting) 2ZnO + 2SO2
  • ZnO + C → Zn + CO

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Reactivity series mnemonic: Please Send Charlie's Monkey And Zebra Into Lions' Pens, Come Home Monkeys, All Safe And sound (K Na Ca Mg Al Zn Fe Pb H Cu Hg Ag Au).
  • Amphoteric = Al and Zn only. They react with both acids and bases to give salt + hydrogen.
  • Extraction rule of thumb: metals above carbon in the series need electrolysis; metals below carbon can be reduced with coke.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Saying metals are oxidised by nitric acid to give H2 — HNO3 is an oxidising acid, so it oxidises the H2 formed to water.
  • Calling calcination 'roasting'. Calcination = heating in the absence of air (for carbonate ores); roasting = heating in the presence of air (for sulphide ores).
  • Forgetting that ionic compounds conduct only when molten or dissolved — in the solid state the ions cannot move.
  • Answering 'aluminium does not corrode' without naming the protective Al2O3 layer.

❓ Exam Q&A

1 marks Name a metal that is liquid at room temperature and a non-metal that is lustrous.

Mercury (metal, liquid at room temperature) and iodine (non-metal, lustrous).

2 marks Why is sodium kept immersed in kerosene oil?

Sodium is so reactive that it reacts vigorously with oxygen, moisture and carbon dioxide in air, catching fire. Kerosene cuts off air and moisture, so the metal stays unreacted.

3 marks Why are food cans coated with tin and not with zinc?

Zinc is more reactive than tin and would itself react with the acidic contents of the food, contaminating it. Tin is less reactive, so it protects the can without reacting.

3 marks Show the formation of MgCl2 by the transfer of electrons, and state two properties of the compound formed.

Mg (2,8,2) loses 2 electrons → Mg2+; each Cl (2,8,7) gains one electron → Cl−. Mg2+ + 2Cl− → MgCl2, held by strong electrostatic force.
Properties: it is a hard crystalline solid with a high melting point, and it conducts electricity when molten or dissolved in water.

5 marks Explain electrolytic refining of copper.

The impure copper is made the anode and a thin strip of pure copper the cathode, both dipped in acidified copper sulphate solution.
On passing current, copper from the anode dissolves as Cu2+ and an equal amount deposits on the cathode.
Soluble impurities go into the solution; insoluble impurities settle below the anode as anode mud (which contains the precious metals).

2 marks Why does aluminium not corrode easily even though it is a reactive metal?

It reacts with oxygen to form a thin, tightly adhering layer of aluminium oxide on its surface. This layer prevents further reaction with air or moisture, so the metal below is protected.

✍️ Hands-on task

Put an iron nail in copper sulphate solution and a copper wire in ferrous sulphate solution for 30 minutes. Record the colour of each solution and of each metal before and after, then explain the difference using the reactivity series.
