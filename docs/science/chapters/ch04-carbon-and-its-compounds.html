Chapter 4 · Carbon and its Compounds

Chapter 4 of 13. The hardest Chemistry chapter and the most rewarding — nomenclature alone is a guaranteed 2–3 marks once the rules are automatic.

🎯 Marks lens 📖 Deep concepts 🧮 Formulas 🛠️ Steps 🧠 Tricks ⚠️ Mistakes ❓ Exam Q&A ✍️ Task

🎯 Marks lens

4–5 marks. One naming question (IUPAC name or structure), one comparison of soap and detergent or of ethanol and ethanoic acid, and one reaction of carbon compounds.
Case-based questions often give a homologous series table and ask you to identify the pattern, the general formula and the next member.

📖 Deep concepts

1. Why carbon is special. Carbon has four valence electrons; gaining or losing four electrons is energetically impossible, so it shares electrons and forms covalent bonds. Two properties make it unique: catenation (bonding to itself in long chains, branches and rings) and tetravalency. Covalent compounds have low melting and boiling points (weak intermolecular forces), are usually insoluble in water, and do not conduct electricity (no ions).
2. Allotropes. Diamond (each carbon bonded to four others in a rigid 3-D network — hardest natural substance), graphite (hexagonal layers that slide — soft, a good lubricant and a conductor), and buckminsterfullerene C60.
3. Saturated and unsaturated compounds. Alkanes (CnH2n+2, only single bonds — methane, ethane, propane, butane), alkenes (CnH2n, one C=C double bond — ethene) and alkynes (CnH2n−2, one C≡C triple bond — ethyne). A homologous series has the same general formula, successive members differing by −CH2− (14 u), similar chemical properties and gradually changing physical properties.
4. Chemical properties of carbon compounds. (a) Combustion — saturated hydrocarbons burn with a clean blue flame; unsaturated ones give a sooty flame. (b) Oxidation of ethanol with alkaline KMnO4 or acidified K2Cr2O7 gives ethanoic acid. (c) Addition — hydrogen adds across a double bond in the presence of nickel (hydrogenation of oils to give vanaspati). (d) Substitution — methane with chlorine in sunlight gives chloromethane and so on.
5. Ethanol (C2H5OH) — a liquid, miscible with water, a good solvent. With sodium it gives sodium ethoxide and hydrogen; with conc. H2SO4 at 443 K it dehydrates to ethene; with ethanoic acid it esterifies to ethyl ethanoate (a sweet-smelling ester). Denatured alcohol contains methanol so it cannot be drunk. Ethanoic acid (CH3COOH) — glacial acetic acid freezes in winter; it is a weak acid (partially ionised), turns blue litmus red, reacts with NaHCO3 to give CO2 (the test that distinguishes it from an alcohol), and with a base gives a salt and water.
6. Soaps and detergents. A soap is the sodium or potassium salt of a long-chain fatty acid; its ionic end is hydrophilic and its carbon tail is hydrophobic. In water soap molecules form a micelle, trapping oil in the centre — that is how soap cleans. Soaps do not lather in hard water (they form insoluble calcium/magnesium scum); detergents are ammonium or sulphonate salts of long-chain acids and do work in hard water.

🧮 Formulas to memorise

  • Alkanes CnH2n+2 · Alkenes CnH2n · Alkynes CnH2n−2
  • CH3COOH + C2H5OH →(conc. H2SO4) CH3COOC2H5 + H2O (ester)
  • C2H5OH →(conc. H2SO4, 443 K) CH2=CH2 + H2O
  • 2C2H5OH + 2Na → 2C2H5ONa + H2↑
  • CH3COOH + NaHCO3 → CH3COONa + H2O + CO2↑
  • CH3COOH + NaOH → CH3COONa + H2O

🛠️ Method, step by step

  1. Count the carbons and decide the homologous series from the ending: −ane, −ene, −yne, −ol, −al, −one, −oic acid. That gives the general formula and the suffix for the name.
  2. Draw the structure carbon by carbon, remembering carbon is tetravalent; check every carbon has four bonds before you count hydrogens.
  3. For a reaction, name the reagent and the condition — combustion needs air, hydrogenation needs a nickel or palladium catalyst, oxidation needs alkaline KMnO4 or acidified K2Cr2O7.
  4. Close with the observation and the product's use — soapy vs detergent behaviour in hard water, or why ethanol is used as a solvent.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Carbon counts: Meth-1, Eth-2, Prop-3, But-4, Pent-5, Hex-6. The suffix gives the bond: -ane single, -ene double, -yne triple.
  • Ester test = fruity smell; acid test = brisk effervescence with NaHCO3. These two tests sort alcohols, acids and esters in any question.
  • Hard water + soap = scum, not lather. Detergents work because their calcium salts are soluble.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Writing the general formula of alkenes as CnH2n+2. Check by counting ethene: C2H4 fits CnH2n.
  • Calling the reaction of ethanol with ethanoic acid 'neutralisation'. It is esterification.
  • Explaining cleansing action without the word micelle — the marking scheme looks for it.
  • Saying 'carbon compounds conduct electricity'. Covalent compounds do not; they have no ions.

SHORTS12 marks

1. Why do covalent compounds generally have low melting and boiling points?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because the forces of attraction between their molecules are weak, even though the covalent bonds within a molecule are strong. Little energy is needed to separate the molecules.

SHORTS22 marks

2. Give a chemical test to distinguish between butter and cooking oil.

Show Answer

Ans:

The bromine water test (or the alkaline KMnO4 test). Cooking oil is unsaturated, so it decolorises bromine water/KMnO4; butter is largely saturated and does not.

SHORTS32 marks

3. Why is a mixture of ethyne and pure oxygen used for welding instead of ethyne and air?

Show Answer

Ans:

In air, ethyne burns incompletely and gives a sooty flame with less heat. In pure oxygen combustion is complete, producing a clean flame at about 3000 °C (the oxy-acetylene flame), which is hot enough for welding.

SHORTS42 marks

4. Why is methane called a saturated hydrocarbon? Describe its electron-dot structure in words.

Show Answer

Ans:

In methane (CH₄) all four valencies of carbon are satisfied by four single covalent bonds with hydrogen atoms, so no more atoms can be added — the molecule is saturated. In the electron-dot structure, carbon sits at the centre and shares one pair of electrons with each of the four hydrogen atoms, giving every atom a stable octet (duplet for hydrogen).

SHORTS53 marks

5. What is a homologous series? Write the general formula and the third member of the alkane series.

Show Answer

Ans:

A homologous series is a group of compounds with the same general formula and similar chemical properties, in which successive members differ by a −CH2− unit (14 u).
Alkanes: CnH2n+2. Third member = propane, C3H8.

SHORTS63 marks

6. Explain the cleansing action of soap with the help of a diagram description.

Show Answer

Ans:

A soap molecule has a hydrophilic ionic head and a hydrophobic hydrocarbon tail.
In water, the tails attach to the oily dirt while the heads remain in water, forming a spherical cluster called a micelle with the oil trapped inside.
The micelle is washed away with the water, taking the dirt with it; agitation helps detach the oil.

SHORTS93 marks

9. Write the equation for the combustion of ethanol and explain why ethanol burns with a clean blue flame.

Show Answer

Ans:

C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O + heat and light.
Ethanol already contains oxygen and has a low carbon-to-hydrogen ratio, so it burns almost completely.
Saturated hydrocarbons contain relatively more carbon and often burn incompletely, depositing unburnt carbon, which gives a sooty yellow flame.

APPLICATIONA124 marks

12. Why does soap not lather well in hard water, and what is used instead?

Show Answer

Ans:

Hard water contains dissolved calcium and magnesium salts, which react with soap to form an insoluble sticky precipitate (scum).
Soap is therefore wasted and lather forms only after all the Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions have been precipitated.
Detergents are used instead: their molecules are sulphonate or sulphate salts whose calcium and magnesium salts stay dissolved, so they lather even in hard water.

LONGL135 marks

13. With equations, describe three chemical properties of ethanoic acid.

Show Answer

Ans:

With a base (neutralisation): CH3COOH + NaOH → CH3COONa + H2O.
With a metal carbonate: 2CH3COOH + Na2CO3 → 2CH3COONa + H2O + CO2.
With a hydrogen carbonate: CH3COOH + NaHCO3 → CH3COONa + H2O + CO2 (brisk effervescence).
Esterification (with ethanol, conc. H2SO4): CH3COOH + C2H5OH → CH3COOC2H5 + H2O — a sweet-smelling ester used in perfumes and flavourings.
