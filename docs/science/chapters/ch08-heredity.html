🎯 Marks lens

2–3 marks. A monohybrid cross with a Punnett square, or a reasoning question on Mendel's experiments, is asked almost every year.
The chapter is now titled Heredity — the evolution section has been removed from the syllabus, so ignore it in older books and question banks.

📖 Deep concepts

1. The basis of heredity. Heredity is the transmission of characters from parents to offspring; the functional unit of inheritance is the gene, a segment of DNA on a chromosome. Each character has a pair of alleles, one from each parent. A dominant allele expresses itself in the F1 generation; the recessive allele is masked and reappears in F2. Genotype is the genetic make-up (TT, Tt, tt); phenotype is what appears (tall or dwarf).
2. Mendel's monohybrid cross. Pure tall (TT) × pure dwarf (tt) → F1 all tall (Tt) — the recessive character is hidden, not lost. Self-pollination of F1 gives F2 in the ratio 3 tall : 1 dwarf (phenotypic) and 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt (genotypic). Conclusion: characters are inherited as separate, independent units, and one can dominate the other.
3. Mendel's dihybrid cross. Round-yellow (RRYY) × wrinkled-green (rryy) → F1 all round-yellow (RrYy). F2 phenotypic ratio 9 round-yellow : 3 round-green : 3 wrinkled-yellow : 1 wrinkled-green. Conclusion: the two pairs of characters are independently inherited — new combinations (round-green and wrinkled-yellow) appear that were not in the parents.
4. Sex determination in human beings. Sex chromosomes: XX = female, XY = male. All eggs carry an X; sperms carry either X or Y. If an X-bearing sperm fertilises the egg the child is a girl (XX); if a Y-bearing sperm does, the child is a boy (XY). The chance of a boy or a girl is therefore 50 : 50 — sex is determined by the father's sperm, and it is a matter of chance, not of any parent's doing.
5. Acquired vs inherited traits. A trait acquired during life (a beetle becoming lighter after injury, a wrestler's muscles, a pierced ear) affects only the somatic cells and is not passed on. Only changes in the DNA of germ cells are inherited. A small population faces a greater risk of extinction because inbreeding reduces variation, and genetic drift can wipe out rare alleles.

🧮 Formulas to memorise

  • Monohybrid F2 phenotypic ratio = 3 : 1
  • Monohybrid F2 genotypic ratio = 1 : 2 : 1
  • Dihybrid F2 phenotypic ratio = 9 : 3 : 3 : 1
  • Sex ratio = 50% XY (boy) : 50% XX (girl)

🧠 Memory tricks

  • 3:1 is phenotypic, 1:2:1 is genotypic. If the question asks 'how many were tall', use 3:1; if it asks 'how many were heterozygous', use 1:2:1.
  • Dihybrid = (3:1) × (3:1) = 9:3:3:1. Deriving it is faster than memorising it.
  • Father decides the sex. The mother always gives X; only the sperm can give Y.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Writing F1 as 'TT × tt = all Tt, so the dwarf trait is destroyed'. It is masked, and reappears in F2.
  • Forgetting to draw the Punnett square when the question says 'show with the help of a cross'.
  • Stating that the sex of a child depends on the mother. It depends on which sperm fertilises the egg.
  • Confusing genotype and phenotype in the final ratio line.

❓ Exam Q&A

1 marks What is the sex chromosome combination in a human male, and what is the functional unit of inheritance?

XY; the gene (a segment of DNA on a chromosome).

2 marks A man with blood group A marries a woman with blood group O and their daughter has blood group O. Is this enough to tell which trait is dominant? Why?

No. From one family we cannot tell. If A were dominant, the father could be heterozygous (IAi) and the daughter could inherit i from both parents; but a single observation does not establish dominance, which requires studying many such crosses.

3 marks Explain Mendel's monohybrid cross with a Punnett square.

Pure tall (TT) × pure dwarf (tt). Gametes: T and t. F1: all Tt — tall, because tall is dominant and dwarf is recessive.
Selfing F1 (Tt × Tt) gives gametes T and t from each parent. Punnett square → TT, Tt, Tt, tt.
Phenotype: 3 tall : 1 dwarf. Genotype: 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt. The dwarf character was only masked in F1, not lost.

3 marks How is the sex of a child determined in human beings? Show diagrammatically.

Mother (XX) produces only X-bearing eggs; father (XY) produces X-bearing and Y-bearing sperms in equal numbers.
X egg + X sperm → XX = girl; X egg + Y sperm → XY = boy.
The probability of either is 50%, so the sex of the child is decided by chance and not by either parent's wish or action.

5 marks Describe Mendel's dihybrid cross and state the conclusion drawn from it.

He crossed pea plants with round-yellow seeds (RRYY) with plants having wrinkled-green seeds (rryy).
F1: all plants had round-yellow seeds (RrYy) — round and yellow are dominant.
On self-pollinating F1, F2 gave four phenotypes: 9 round-yellow : 3 round-green : 3 wrinkled-yellow : 1 wrinkled-green (16 combinations in the 4×4 Punnett square).
Conclusion: the two pairs of characters are inherited independently of each other, which is why new combinations (round-green and wrinkled-green) appear in F2 that were absent in the parents. This is the law of independent assortment.

2 marks Distinguish between acquired and inherited traits, with one example of each.

An acquired trait develops during an organism's lifetime due to experience or environment and is not passed on — for example, a wrestler's well-developed muscles or a pierced ear. An inherited trait is present from birth and is passed on through the DNA of germ cells — for example, the colour of eyes or free ear lobes.

✍️ Hands-on task

Draw the 4×4 Punnett square for the dihybrid cross RrYy × RrYy by hand, count the sixteen squares into the four phenotype classes, and confirm the 9:3:3:1 ratio. Then convert each genotype into its phenotype to check you have 9 round-yellow, 3 round-green, 3 wrinkled-yellow and 1 wrinkled-green.
