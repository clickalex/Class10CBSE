🎯 Marks lens

4–5 marks. A pH/reasoning question (2), a salt-family question such as bleaching powder or plaster of Paris (2–3), and one indicator or neutralisation question.
Case-based questions are built around a pH scale diagram or a soil/acidity scenario — they are application, not recall, so learn why, not just what.

📖 Deep concepts

1. What makes an acid an acid. Acids produce H+(aq) ions in water; bases produce OH−(aq) ions. Distillation water does not conduct electricity, but adding an acid or base makes it conduct, because the ions are the charge carriers. Note: dry HCl gas does not turn dry blue litmus red — water is needed for ionisation.
2. The pH scale runs 0 to 14. pH 7 is neutral, below 7 acidic, above 7 basic. A lower pH means a higher H+ concentration. Our body works within a narrow range: blood is 7.0–7.8, and tooth decay begins when mouth pH falls below 5.5.

3. Acid + metal → salt + hydrogen. Zinc and dilute sulphuric acid give ZnSO4 + H2↑; the gas burns with a pop sound. Acid + metal carbonate → salt + CO2 + water; the CO2 turns lime water milky.
4. Neutralisation. Acid + base → salt + water, always exothermic. A universal indicator shows the strength of the acid or base, not merely its presence.
5. The four salts the board asks about. Sodium chloride — from seawater; its electrolysis (chlor-alkali process) gives NaOH, Cl2 and H2. Bleaching powder — Ca(OCl)Cl, made from slaked lime and chlorine; used for disinfecting water and bleaching. Baking soda — NaHCO3 (Solvay process); used in cooking, as an antacid and in fire extinguishers. Washing soda — Na2CO3·10H2O; used to soften hard water and in making glass. Plaster of Paris — CaSO4·½H2O, made by heating gypsum at 373 K; it re-hardens on adding water, so it is used for setting fractured bones.

🧮 Formulas to memorise

  • pH = −log[H+] (concept: lower pH ⇒ more H+)
  • Ca(OH)2 + Cl2 → Ca(OCl)Cl + H2O (bleaching powder)
  • NaCl + H2O + CO2 + NH3 → NaHCO3 + NH4Cl (Solvay)
  • 2NaHCO3 → Na2CO3 + H2O + CO2 (on heating)
  • CaSO4·2H2O →(373 K) CaSO4·½H2O + 1½H2O
  • CaSO4·½H2O + 1½H2O → CaSO4·2H2O (setting of POP)

🧠 Memory tricks

  • pH memory: the scale counts down as acidity goes up. pH 2 is a hundred times more acidic than pH 4 (each unit is a factor of ten).
  • POP loses 1½ water molecules at 373 K. Remember 'gypsum minus one-and-a-half = POP'. Above 373 K you get dead-burnt plaster, CaSO4, which will not set.
  • Antacid vs antiseptic: antacids are weak bases (Mg(OH)2, NaHCO3) that neutralise excess stomach HCl.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Saying 'an acid gives H+ ions' without (aq). Ionisation needs water — dry HCl is not acidic.
  • Confusing baking soda (NaHCO3) with washing soda (Na2CO3·10H2O).
  • Writing the setting of POP as a physical change. It is a chemical change — a new compound, gypsum, is formed.
  • Answering 'why does milk become sour' without naming lactic acid.

SHORTS12 marks

1. Why does dry HCl gas not change the colour of dry litmus paper?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because acidic behaviour requires the formation of H+(aq) ions, which needs water. Dry HCl gas has no water, so it does not ionise and cannot affect dry litmus.

SHORTS22 marks

2. A solution turns red litmus blue. Its pH is likely to be: 1, 4, 7 or 10. Justify.

Show Answer

Ans:

  1. Turning red litmus blue shows the solution is basic, and only pH 10 among the options is on the basic side of 7.
SHORTS33 marks

3. Why is plaster of Paris written as CaSO4·½H2O? Is it possible to have half a water molecule?

Show Answer

Ans:

The formula is written per formula unit of CaSO4. One molecule of water is shared between two CaSO4 units, so each unit effectively holds half a water molecule. Heating gypsum at 373 K drives out 1½ molecules of water.

SHORTS43 marks

4. A milkman adds a very small amount of baking soda to fresh milk. Why does the milk take longer to set as curd?

Show Answer

Ans:

The baking soda makes the milk slightly alkaline, so the lactic acid produced by bacteria is first neutralised before the pH can fall low enough to coagulate the casein. Setting is therefore delayed.

LONGL55 marks

5. Explain the chlor-alkali process with an equation, and give two uses each of the products.

Show Answer

Ans:

Electrolysis of concentrated aqueous sodium chloride (brine) gives sodium hydroxide, chlorine and hydrogen: 2NaCl(aq) + 2H2O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + Cl2(g) + H2(g).
Sodium hydroxide — making soaps and detergents, and in the paper industry.
Chlorine — disinfecting drinking water and making bleaching powder/PVC.
Hydrogen — as a fuel and in the manufacture of ammonia and margarine (hydrogenation of oils).

SHORTS62 marks

6. Fresh milk has a pH of 6. Explain why the pH falls as it turns into curd.

Show Answer

Ans:

Bacteria convert lactose into lactic acid. The acid increases the H+ ion concentration, so the pH drops below 6 and the milk proteins coagulate into curd.
