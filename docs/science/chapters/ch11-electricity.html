Chapter 11 · Electricity

Chapter 11 of 13. The most numerical-heavy chapter in the paper. Three formulas cover everything; the rest is careful reading of the circuit.

🎯 Marks lens 📖 Deep concepts 🧮 Formulas 🛠️ Steps 🧠 Tricks ⚠️ Mistakes ❓ Exam Q&A ✍️ Task

🎯 Marks lens

3–4 marks. A series or parallel circuit numerical is almost certain, plus one question on the heating effect or on power rating.
Case-based questions usually give a household circuit (parallel wiring) and ask why appliances are connected in parallel and what happens to the resistance.

📖 Deep concepts

1. Electric current and potential difference. Current I = Q/t, measured in amperes with an ammeter connected in series. Potential difference V = W/Q, measured in volts with a voltmeter connected in parallel; 1 volt = 1 joule per coulomb. By convention current flows from the positive to the negative terminal, opposite to the flow of electrons. A cell maintains the potential difference that drives the charge.
2. Ohm's law. At constant temperature, the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across its ends: V = IR. The V–I graph for an ohmic conductor is a straight line through the origin; its slope gives R. Resistance is the property that opposes the flow of current, R = ρl/A, measured in ohms (Ω) — it increases with length, decreases with area of cross-section, and depends on the material and temperature.
3. Resistors in series. The same current flows through each; total R = R1 + R2 + R3, which is greater than the largest individual resistance. Voltages divide in the ratio of the resistances. Series wiring is used when the total resistance must be increased, as in a decorative bulb string.
4. Resistors in parallel. The same potential difference is across each; 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3, and the total is less than the smallest individual resistance. This is why domestic appliances are connected in parallel — each gets the full supply voltage, each has its own switch, and the failure of one does not affect the others.
5. Heating effect of current. H = I²Rt (joules) — also written H = VIt = V²t/R. Applications: the electric heater, geyser and iron (high-resistance nichrome heating element), the electric bulb (tungsten filament, melting point 3380 °C, filled with argon and nitrogen so the filament does not oxidise), and the electric fuse (a wire of low melting point in series that melts if the current exceeds a safe value; ISI mark and rating such as 5 A). A circuit breaker and earthing (the metal body of an appliance connected to the earth) protect the user from electric shock.

🧮 Formulas to memorise

  • I = Q/t ; 1 A = 1 C/s
  • V = W/Q ; 1 V = 1 J/C
  • V = IR ; R = ρl/A
  • Series: Rs = R1 + R2 + R3
  • Parallel: 1/Rp = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3
  • H = I²Rt = VIt = V²t/R
  • P = VI = I²R = V²/R ; 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J

🛠️ Method, step by step

  1. Sketch the circuit in words and decide whether the resistors are in series (same current, Rs = R1 + R2 …) or parallel (same voltage, 1/Rp = 1/R1 + 1/R2 …).
  2. Write down Ohm's law V = IR for the part of the circuit you are solving, and keep the units straight — current in amperes, potential difference in volts, resistance in ohms.
  3. For heating, use H = I²Rt when the current is known and H = VIt when the voltage is known; state the power as P = VI = I²R = V²/R.
  4. Check the physical sense of your answer: in series, total resistance must exceed the largest single resistor; in parallel, it must be smaller than the smallest.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Series = same current, voltage divides. Parallel = same voltage, current divides. Read that sentence before every circuit question.
  • Parallel resistance is always smaller than the smallest resistor. If your answer is bigger, you have used the series formula.
  • Units for energy: the board wants kWh for a household bill. 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J. Always convert hours, not minutes.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Connecting the ammeter in parallel and the voltmeter in series in a circuit diagram — this is a marked error.
  • Using H = I²Rt and then quoting the answer in kWh without converting joules.
  • Forgetting that the resistance of the connecting wires and the ammeter is taken as zero, while the voltmeter has very high resistance.
  • In the parallel formula, adding the resistances instead of the reciprocals, and not taking the reciprocal of the final sum.

SHORTS11 mark

1. Define one ohm and name the device used to measure potential difference.

Show Answer

Ans:

One ohm is the resistance of a conductor when a potential difference of 1 volt across its ends produces a current of 1 ampere through it. Potential difference is measured with a voltmeter, connected in parallel.

SHORTS22 marks

2. Why are the coils of electric toasters and electric irons made of an alloy rather than a pure metal?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because alloys have higher resistivity than the pure metals, so they produce more heat for the same current, and they do not oxidise (burn) readily at high temperatures.

SHORTS32 marks

3. An electric iron of rating 750 W, 220 V is used for 2 hours daily. Calculate the cost of using it for 30 days at ₹5 per unit.

Show Answer

Ans:

Energy per day = P × t = 750 W × 2 h = 1500 Wh = 1.5 kWh.
Energy for 30 days = 1.5 × 30 = 45 kWh = 45 units.
Cost = 45 × ₹5 = ₹225.

SHORTS42 marks

4. Why are household appliances connected in parallel and not in series?

Show Answer

Ans:

In a parallel circuit every appliance gets the full supply voltage and works independently, so switching one off does not affect the others and a fault in one branch does not stop the rest — none of which is possible in a series circuit.

SHORTS53 marks

5. State Ohm's law and draw the V–I graph for an ohmic conductor.

Show Answer

Ans:

At constant temperature, the current flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across its ends: V = IR, where R is the resistance.
The graph of V against I is a straight line passing through the origin; its slope (V/I) gives the resistance of the conductor.

SHORTS63 marks

6. Three resistors of 2 Ω, 3 Ω and 6 Ω are connected in parallel. Find the equivalent resistance.

Show Answer

Ans:

1/Rp = 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/6 = (3 + 2 + 1)/6 = 6/6 = 1.
So Rp = 1 Ω — less than the smallest resistor (2 Ω), as expected for a parallel combination.

SHORTS93 marks

9. Resistors of 4 Ω and 12 Ω are connected in parallel and the combination is joined to a 6 V battery. Find the equivalent resistance and the current drawn from the battery.

Show Answer

Ans:

1/Rₚ = 1/4 + 1/12 = (3 + 1)/12 = 4/12, so Rₚ = 3 Ω.
Current I = V/R = 6/3 = 2 A.
Check: the branch currents are 6/4 = 1.5 A and 6/12 = 0.5 A, which add to 2 A. Note the equivalent resistance is less than the smaller resistor.

APPLICATIONA124 marks

12. A 100 W bulb and a 60 W bulb are both rated at 220 V. Which has the higher resistance, and what current does each draw?

Show Answer

Ans:

Using R = V²/P: R₁₀₀ = 220²/100 = 484 Ω and R₆₀ = 220²/60 ≈ 806.7 Ω.
So the 60 W bulb has the higher resistance — the lower-power bulb resists the current more.
Currents: I = P/V, so the 100 W bulb draws 100/220 ≈ 0.45 A and the 60 W bulb draws 60/220 ≈ 0.27 A.

LONGL135 marks

13. A 4 Ω resistor and a 6 Ω resistor are connected in series with a 20 V battery. Find the total resistance, the current, the potential difference across each resistor, and the heat produced in the 4 Ω resistor in 1 minute.

Show Answer

Ans:

Total resistance Rs = 4 + 6 = 10 Ω.
Current I = V/R = 20/10 = 2 A — the same through both resistors.
Potential differences: across 4 Ω, V = 2 × 4 = 8 V; across 6 Ω, V = 2 × 6 = 12 V. (8 + 12 = 20 V ✓)
Heat in the 4 Ω resistor H = I²Rt = (2)² × 4 × 60 = 960 J.
