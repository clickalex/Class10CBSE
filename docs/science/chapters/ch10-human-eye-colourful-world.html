Chapter 10 · The Human Eye and the Colourful World

Chapter 10 of 13. Four defects, four corrections, and four sky-colour reasons. Small chapter, guaranteed marks.

🎯 Marks lens

2–3 marks. Myopia/hypermetropia with a labelled diagram is the single most repeated question in Physics.
The 'why is the sky blue / why is the sunrise red' question appears regularly as a 2-mark reasoning item.

📖 Deep concepts

1. The eye as a camera. Cornea — most of the refraction happens here. Iris — the dark muscular diaphragm that controls the size of the pupil and hence the amount of light entering. Crystalline lens — a convex lens of adjustable focal length; ciliary muscles change its curvature, so a clear image is formed on the retina for objects at all distances. This is the power of accommodation. The image on the retina is real and inverted; the optic nerve carries the signal to the brain. The blind spot is where the optic nerve leaves and there are no photoreceptors. Least distance of distinct vision = 25 cm for a normal adult eye; far point = infinity.
2. Defects of vision. Myopia (short sight) — the person sees near objects clearly but not distant ones; caused by excessive curvature of the lens or elongation of the eyeball; the image forms in front of the retina; corrected with a concave lens. Hypermetropia (long sight) — cannot see nearby objects clearly; image forms behind the retina; corrected with a convex lens. Presbyopia — the power of accommodation decreases with age; corrected with a bifocal lens.
3. Dispersion. The splitting of white light into its seven constituent colours (VIBGYOR) by a glass prism. Newton showed with two identical prisms, one inverted over the other, that white light is a mixture of colours. Red bends least, violet most, because different colours travel at different speeds in glass. Rainbow formation: refraction, dispersion and total internal reflection of sunlight by water droplets acting as tiny prisms — you see it with the sun behind you.
4. Atmospheric refraction. The refractive index of air decreases with altitude, so light from a star bends continuously as it enters the atmosphere. Effects: stars twinkle (the apparent position and brightness keep changing), planets do not twinkle (they are nearer and act as extended sources, so the variations average out), the sun appears above the horizon about two minutes before actual sunrise and two minutes after sunset (daylight is lengthened by about 4 minutes), and stars appear slightly higher than their true position.
5. Scattering of light. Fine particles scatter shorter (blue) wavelengths more strongly — the Tyndall effect. This explains the blue colour of the sky, the reddish appearance of the sun at sunrise and sunset (blue is scattered away over the long path, leaving red), the white appearance of clouds (large water droplets scatter all colours equally), and why danger signals are red (red scatters least and travels farthest through fog and smoke). At noon the sun looks white because the path through the atmosphere is short.

🧮 Formulas to memorise

  • Power of a lens: P = 1/f (f in metres, P in dioptres)
  • Correction: myopia → concave (diverging) lens of negative power; hypermetropia → convex (converging) lens of positive power
  • Presbyopia → bifocal lens; astigmatism → cylindrical lens
  • Scattering intensity ∝ 1/λ⁴ — shorter wavelength scatters far more
  • Refractive index and deviation: violet deviates most, red least

🛠️ Method, step by step

  1. For a defect of vision, name it, say where the image forms (myopia: in front of the retina; hypermetropia: would form behind it), and quote the correcting lens — concave for myopia, convex for hypermetropia.
  2. For a dispersion question, state that white light splits into seven colours because different wavelengths refract by different amounts — violet deviates most, red least.
  3. For scattering, apply the inverse-fourth law: shorter wavelengths scatter far more, which is why the sky is blue and the sun looks red at sunrise and sunset.
  4. End with the everyday example asked for — the apparent position of stars due to atmospheric refraction, or the rainbow after rain.

🧠 Memory tricks

  • Myopia = image in front = concave lens. 'My-o-pia → My oh, I can't see far → Concave.'
  • Hypermetropia = image behind = convex lens. 'Hyper → beyond → behind → convex.'
  • VIBGYOR: red deviates least, violet most. Same order explains the rainbow and the prism.

⚠️ Mistakes that cost marks

  • Drawing the corrective lens on the wrong side of the eye diagram, or labelling the image position incorrectly (in front for myopia, behind for hypermetropia).
  • Saying 'the sky is blue because it reflects the sea'. It is scattering by air molecules.
  • Explaining twinkling as 'stars burning unevenly'. It is atmospheric refraction.
  • Forgetting that presbyopia is due to the weakening of the ciliary muscles and loss of flexibility of the lens, not a change in the eyeball's length.

1. Name the part of the eye that controls the amount of light entering it, and the least distance of distinct vision for a normal eye.

Show Answer

Ans:

The iris (which adjusts the size of the pupil); 25 cm.

SHORTS22 marks

2. Why do stars twinkle but planets do not?

Show Answer

Ans:

Starlight passes through layers of air of changing refractive index, so its path keeps changing slightly and the apparent brightness and position fluctuate — the star twinkles. Planets are much nearer and behave as a collection of many point sources, so the fluctuations from different parts cancel out and the total light remains steady.

SHORTS32 marks

3. Why does the sun appear reddish early in the morning?

Show Answer

Ans:

Because near the horizon sunlight travels through a much thicker layer of air. The shorter blue wavelengths are scattered away, and mainly the longer red wavelengths reach our eyes, so the sun looks red. At noon the path is short, little blue light is scattered, and the sun looks white.

SHORTS42 marks

4. What is the power of accommodation of the eye?

Show Answer

Ans:

It is the ability of the eye lens to change its focal length — by the action of the ciliary muscles — so that both near and distant objects are focused sharply on the retina. In a normal eye the near point is about 25 cm and the far point is infinity.

SHORTS53 marks

5. What is myopia? State its two causes and its correction.

Show Answer

Ans:

Myopia (short-sightedness) is the defect in which a person can see nearby objects clearly but cannot see distant objects distinctly; the image of a distant object forms in front of the retina.
Causes: (i) excessive curvature of the eye lens, (ii) elongation of the eyeball.
Correction: a concave lens of suitable power, which diverges the light so that the image is formed exactly on the retina.

SHORTS63 marks

6. Explain the formation of a rainbow in the sky.

Show Answer

Ans:

After rain, tiny water droplets remain suspended in the air and act as small glass prisms.
Sunlight entering a droplet is first refracted and dispersed into its constituent colours, then undergoes total internal reflection at the inner surface of the droplet, and is refracted again as it leaves.
Because different colours emerge at slightly different angles, the observer sees a coloured arc — a rainbow — with the sun always behind them.

SHORTS93 marks

9. What is presbyopia? State its cause and how it is corrected.

Show Answer

Ans:

Presbyopia is the loss of the eye's power of accommodation with age: the ciliary muscles weaken and the lens becomes less flexible.
As a result the near point recedes and reading or threading a needle becomes difficult.
It is corrected with converging (convex) spectacles; when a person has both myopia and presbyopia, bifocal lenses are used.

APPLICATIONA124 marks

12. Explain why the sky looks blue and the sun looks white at noon but red at sunrise.

Show Answer

Ans:

Air molecules and fine particles scatter light of short wavelength much more strongly than long wavelength light (scattering ∝ 1/λ⁴).
Blue light is scattered the most, so light reaching the eye from all parts of the sky is rich in blue.
At noon the sun is overhead and its light crosses the shortest possible path, so very little blue is removed and the sun looks white.
Near sunrise and sunset the path through the atmosphere is much longer, most of the blue is scattered away, and the remaining direct light looks red.

LONGL135 marks

13. Explain atmospheric refraction and describe any three phenomena based on it.

Show Answer

Ans:

Atmospheric refraction is the bending of light as it passes through layers of air of different densities; the refractive index of air decreases as we go up, so light from a celestial body bends gradually toward the normal.
(1) Twinkling of stars: the continuously changing refractive index makes the apparent position and brightness of a star fluctuate.
(2) Advance of sunrise and delay of sunset: light from the sun below the horizon is refracted toward the earth, so the sun becomes visible about two minutes before actual sunrise and remains visible about two minutes after sunset — the day is lengthened by about 4 minutes.
(3) Apparent flattening of the sun's disc at sunrise and sunset, and stars appearing slightly higher than their true position, are also caused by the same refraction.
